The immune system is a layered defense network

This lecture moves from the body's rapid, broad defenses to its specific, memory-forming defenses, then shows how an otherwise protective response can become harmful in hypersensitivity. The central organizing idea is: recognize → signal → eliminate → remember. pp. 1–11

Central question

How does the body detect a pathogen, recruit the correct cells and molecules, remove the threat, and respond better the next time?

The lecture answers this through two connected branches. Innate immunity acts first and quickly. Adaptive immunity takes longer during first exposure but targets the pathogen more precisely and generates memory cells.

How the topics connect

Barrier failure permits invasion. Microbial sensors recognize the threat and trigger cytokines. Cytokines recruit and activate innate cells while antigen-presenting cells bridge into adaptive immunity. MHC molecules show antigen fragments to T cells; T-cell signals then support cytotoxic killing or B-cell antibody production. After clearance, memory cells remain. When this machinery is excessive or misdirected, the lecture classifies the damage as one of four hypersensitivity reactions.

Immunology, innate immunity, and adaptive immunity

Start with the two defense branches and the difference that organizes the entire lecture. p. 1

What immunology studies

p. 1
Immunology studies how the immune system detects, interacts with, and defends the body against infectious pathogens.

The lecture includes bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. The immune system must accomplish three linked tasks: recognize a foreign invader, distinguish it from the body's own cells, and mount a response that eliminates or controls it.

Why it matters

Diagnostic and clinical immunology helps explain infectious diseases, allergy, immunodeficiency, autoimmunity, cancer, and transplantation, supporting better diagnosis and management.

The two main branches

Key differentiator

Speed identifies innate immunity; memory identifies adaptive immunity. Adaptive immunity is slower on first exposure but becomes faster and stronger when the same antigen returns.

One connected response, not two isolated systems

The lecture describes different recognition systems, but they work toward one goal: eliminating the pathogen or its products. Innate immunity limits spread immediately; adaptive immunity adds specificity and memory. Dendritic cells, macrophages, cytokines, and antigen presentation are important bridges between the two.

Innate immunity I

Physiologic barriers and microbial sensors

Innate defense begins before phagocytosis: first prevent entry, then detect what gets through. pp. 1–2

Innate immunity II

Phagocytosis, natural killer cells, and complement

These are the major “do the killing” systems of innate immunity. pp. 2–4

What this flow means: phagocytosis is not a single “engulf” event. It requires recruitment, endothelial interaction, migration, uptake, and several parallel killing mechanisms.

Inside the phagosome: four killing mechanisms

  1. Acidification: phagosomal pH falls to 3.5–4.0, which is bacteriostatic or bactericidal.
  2. Oxygen-derived products: superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and singlet oxygen are generated.
  3. Nitrogen oxides: nitric oxide is formed.
  4. Antimicrobial peptides: macrophages contain cathelicidin and macrophage-elastase-derived peptides; neutrophils produce α-defensins, β-defensin, cathelicidin, and lactoferricin.
Key idea

These mechanisms are used together. The lecture presents them as a combined intracellular antimicrobial environment, not as mutually exclusive options.

Opsonization makes phagocytosis more efficient

Phagocytosis can occur without antibodies, but coating the bacterium improves recognition and ingestion. This coating is opsonization.

  1. Antibody alone acts as an opsonin.
  2. An antigen–antibody complex activates the classical complement pathway and generates opsonin.
  3. The alternative pathway produces C3, providing another opsonin route.

Macrophages have receptors for the antibody Fc portion and for C3, allowing them to bind coated pathogens.

Lecture Figure 5, p. 3. Antibody coating and complement-derived C3 both improve phagocyte attachment and ingestion.

Natural killer (NK) cells

NK cells are large granular lymphocytes that rapidly kill abnormal host cells without the same antigen-specific recognition described for adaptive T cells.
  • Make up about 10–15% of blood leukocytes in the lecture.
  • Have activating and inhibitory surface receptors.
  • Contain perforin and granzyme.
  • Recognize and kill virus-infected cells and tumor cells.
  • Use Fc receptors to kill antibody-coated cells through ADCC.
  • Produce IFN-γ, described as antiviral and immunoregulatory.
Lecture Figure 6, p. 3. Classical, alternative, and lectin pathways converge at C3 cleavage, then lead to inflammation, opsonization, and MAC-mediated lysis.

Complement: three starts, one shared endpoint

The complement system consists of about 30 interacting proteins in serum or on selected cell membranes. Activation proceeds as a cascade and culminates in pathogen destruction.
PathwayTriggerTiming idea
ClassicalAntibody–antigen complexDelayed during first exposure because antibody production takes time; associated with reinfection in the lecture.
AlternativeMicrobial surface; no antibody requiredImmediate first-line defense.
LectinMannose-binding lectin and MASPImmediate first-line defense; bypasses antibody.
Do not confuse

The pathways differ mainly in how they begin. Their shared defense outputs are anaphylatoxin-driven inflammation, C3b opsonization, and MAC lysis.

Complement cascade taught step by step

Classical pathway

  1. Antibody binds pathogen antigen, creating an antigen–antibody complex.
  2. C1 binds and becomes activated.
  3. C1 cleaves C2 and C4.
  4. C2 and C4 fragments form C3 convertase.
  5. C3 convertase splits C3 into C3a and C3b.
  6. C3a diffuses away and recruits phagocytes; C3b binds the pathogen, opsonizes it, and helps activate C5.
  7. C5 becomes C5a and C5b. C5a is inflammatory and chemotactic; C5b initiates MAC.
  8. C5b joins C6, C7, C8, and C9 to form the membrane attack complex, which creates a pore and causes lysis.

Alternative and lectin pathways

Alternative: skips C1, C2, and C4 and begins with C3 on the microbial surface. C3 interacts with Factors B, D, and P (properdin), then enters the common pathway from C3 onward.

Lectin: mannose-binding lectin-associated serine protease (MASP) cleaves C2 and C4. Unlike classical activation, the trigger is lectin recognition rather than an antigen–antibody complex. It then proceeds to the common pathway.

Three major outputs

C3a/C4a/C5a: anaphylatoxins and inflammatory signaling.
C3b: opsonization.
C5b–C9: MAC formation and lysis.

Lock It In

Phagocytes engulf; NK cells kill abnormal cells and perform ADCC; complement amplifies inflammation, coats targets with C3b, and lyses cells through MAC. All complement pathways converge after different starting triggers.

Innate immunity III

Cytokines: messages that shape the response

Cytokines explain how immune and non-immune cells coordinate inflammation, cell development, recruitment, and regulation. pp. 3–5

What cytokines are

Cytokines are small soluble proteins secreted by immune and non-immune cells. They bind specific receptors on target cells and change what those cells do.

The lecture links cytokines to inflammation and fever, immune-cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation, and the response to infection or injury.

Why they matter

Cytokines allow many different cells to behave as one coordinated system. A cytokine may appear in several pathways because its effect depends on the producing cell, target cell, and surrounding signals.

Lecture Figure 7, p. 3. A producing cell releases a soluble signal, which binds a receptor on a target cell and produces an effect.

Functional response patterns

Pro-inflammatory / intracellular pathogen response

  • IFN-α and IFN-β limit viral replication.
  • IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 act on immune cells, endothelium, liver, and brain, supporting leukocyte migration, acute-phase reactants, and fever.
  • IL-12 activates NK cells and supports Th1 differentiation.
  • IL-6 + IL-23 + TGF-β support Th17 differentiation; IL-17 recruits neutrophils.

Parasitic / allergic response

IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 help handle large parasites and also induce asthma and allergy responses. They promote Th2 differentiation and stimulate mast cells, basophils, and eosinophils; Th2 cells then produce more of these cytokines.

Regulatory response

IL-10 and TGF-β inhibit inflammation and promote regulatory T cells. Those cells produce more IL-10 and TGF-β, maintaining an anti-inflammatory state and reducing co-stimulatory receptor expression.

Growth and differentiation

GM-CSF and M-CSF support differentiation of monocytes and granulocytes. IL-7 promotes progenitor differentiation into lymphocytes including B, T, and NK cells.

Chemotactic response: chemokines as traffic controllers

IL-8 recruits phagocytes to inflammation. IL-17, produced by CD4+ Th17 cells, strongly induces chemokine production. The lecture's “air traffic controller” phrasing captures the main idea: chemokines tell immune cells where to go.

Exam trap

The number of an interleukin does not reveal its function; numbers reflect discovery order. Learn each cytokine by its target and effect rather than by numerical sequence.

Lock It In

Signal range: autocrine = self, paracrine = nearby, endocrine = distant. Functional anchors: IL-8 recruits neutrophils; IL-10 and TGF-β regulate; type I IFNs limit viral replication; IFN-γ activates macrophages.

Adaptive immunity

Lymphocytes, cell-mediated immunity, humoral immunity, and MHC

Adaptive immunity creates antigen specificity by selecting and expanding the lymphocyte clone that recognizes the threat. pp. 5–7

Cellular basis: where T and B cells come from

Bone-marrow stem cells differentiate into myeloid or lymphoid series. Lymphoid progenitors produce the two main adaptive lymphocyte populations: T cells and B cells.

Lecture Figure 9, p. 5. Bone-marrow stem cells give rise to T-cell and B-cell lineages with distinct maturation and effector outcomes.

Cell-mediated immunity: six-step sequence

  1. Antigen presentation: APCs process a pathogen and display fragments. MHC II carries exogenous antigen; MHC I carries endogenous antigen.
  2. T-cell activation: CD4 recognizes MHC II; CD8 recognizes MHC I through TCRs.
  3. Cytokine release: supports proliferation and maturation.
  4. Clonal expansion: activated T cells produce many antigen-specific copies.
  5. Target destruction: CD8 cells use perforin/granzyme or Fas/FasL.
  6. Memory formation: some activated T cells become memory T cells.
Main outcomes

CD4 cells stimulate B cells and promote delayed hypersensitivity. CD8 cells destroy graft cells, tumor cells, and virus-infected cells.

Antibody-mediated immunity: six-step sequence

  1. Antigen recognition: antigen binds directly to a specific B-cell receptor.
  2. Antigen processing: the B cell engulfs antigen and displays fragments on MHC II.
  3. Helper T-cell co-stimulation: activated CD4 binds the antigen–MHC II complex.
  4. B-cell proliferation: CD4 secretes IL-4, producing a large clone of antigen-specific B cells.
  5. Differentiation: clones become plasma cells and memory B cells.
  6. Antibody action: secreted antibodies neutralize, opsonize, and activate complement.
Lecture Figure 10, p. 6. MHC II presents peptide to a helper T cell; MHC I presents peptide to a cytotoxic T cell.

Major histocompatibility complex (MHC)

MHC proteins are cell-surface “identity tags” and antigen-display platforms. In humans they are called human leukocyte antigens (HLA).

MHC molecules place small peptide fragments on the cell surface. T cells patrol these displays. A self-like display is left alone; a foreign, infected, or cancer-associated display can trigger an immune response.

Why it matters

T cells do not simply inspect whole pathogens. They inspect processed peptide fragments presented by MHC. The MHC class determines which T-cell subset receives the message.

Adaptive immunity II

Antigens and antibodies

Antigens determine what is recognized; antibodies provide a highly specific soluble response to that antigen. pp. 7–9, 13–14

Antigen versus immunogen

Antigens are substances that react with antibodies. Immunogens induce an immune response. The lecture states that most antigens are also immunogens.

Five determinants of immunogenicity

  1. Foreignness: self molecules are generally not immunogenic.
  2. Size: molecules below MW 10,000 are weakly immunogenic; very small molecules are non-immunogenic.
  3. Chemical and structural complexity: heteropolymers are more immunogenic than homopolymers.
  4. Host genetics: different MHC alleles can produce different responses to the same antigen.
  5. Dose, route, and timing of administration.
Haptens

Haptens are small lipid or amino-acid molecules that become immunogenic only when linked to a carrier protein.

Antibody production

Antibodies are immunoglobulins that react specifically with the antigen that stimulated their production. They mediate humoral immunity.

B cells differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, and memory B cells, which support later responses.

TypeOrigin and binding pattern
Monoclonal antibodiesArise from one clone, are homogeneous, and bind one specific target. The lecture describes them as laboratory-produced synthetic proteins.
Polyclonal antibodiesCome from multiple B-cell clones and bind multiple epitopes on one complex antigen; they are heterogeneous.
Lecture Figure 15, p. 7. Two heavy and two light chains form a Y-shaped immunoglobulin with variable antigen-binding tips and a constant Fc region.

Antibody structure: what each region does

  • Each molecule contains two identical light chains (MW 25,000) and two identical heavy chains (MW 50,000).
  • The four chains are linked by disulfide bridges.
  • The amino-terminal parts of light and heavy chains contribute to the antigen-binding site.
  • The heavy-chain carboxyl terminal forms the Fc fragment.
  • Heavy chains define the five classes: γ, μ, α, δ, and ε.

Five protective functions of antibodies

  1. Complement-mediated lysis: antibody binding to a microbial wall or abnormal cell can activate complement and cause lysis.
  2. Enhanced phagocytosis: antibody coating makes organisms easier for phagocytes to ingest, especially encapsulated microbes in the lecture.
  3. Neutralization of toxins: antibodies inactivate toxins such as diphtheria, tetanus, and botulism toxins.
  4. ADCC: NK cells, macrophages, neutrophils, or eosinophils bind the antibody Fc portion and kill the coated target. IgE-coated helminths recruit eosinophil degranulation.
  5. Virus neutralization: antibody blocks viral attachment to the cellular receptor, preventing entry and replication.

Hypersensitivity I

Type I: immediate, IgE-mediated allergy

A harmless substance or allergen triggers an exaggerated IgE and mast-cell response, ranging from itching or hay fever to anaphylaxis.

Two-stage mechanism

  1. Sensitization: first exposure causes production of allergen-specific IgE. IgE attaches to mast cells and basophils, priming them. No immediate “memory” diagram is needed; the key is that the cells are now coated with specific IgE.
  2. Re-exposure: the allergen binds and cross-links IgE on the primed mast cell. The cell rapidly releases inflammatory chemicals, primarily histamine, producing immediate inflammation.
Recognition cue

IgE + mast cell + immediate symptoms after re-exposure = Type I.

Type II: antibody against a cell or receptor

IgG or IgM binds an antigen on the surface of a host cell. The antibody marks that cell for destruction or changes how a receptor functions.

Four mechanisms

  1. Complement activation: MAC forms pores and causes cell lysis.
  2. Opsonization and phagocytosis: antibody and complement coat the cell, allowing macrophages and neutrophils to engulf it.
  3. ADCC: NK cells bind antibody-coated cells and release toxic granules, causing apoptosis.
  4. Cellular dysfunction: antibody blocks a receptor or overstimulates it continuously.
Recognition cue

The antibody is directed at a fixed cell-surface target or receptor. This is different from Type III, where soluble complexes circulate and deposit.

Type I: IgE cross-linking on mast cells → histamine → immediate allergy. Type II: IgG/IgM binds a host cell surface or receptor → lysis, phagocytosis, ADCC, blockade, or overstimulation.

Type III and Type IV hypersensitivity

The key separation is deposited immune complexes in Type III versus T-cell-driven delayed injury in Type IV. pp. 10–11, 13

Lecture Figure 22, p. 11. Immune complexes deposit in a vessel wall, activate complement, recruit neutrophils, and produce enzyme-mediated tissue injury.

Type III: immune complex-mediated injury

IgG or IgM binds soluble antigens in the bloodstream. When complexes are excessive and not cleared, they deposit in tissues and trigger inflammation.
1Complex formation
Antibody binds soluble antigen.
2Incomplete clearance
Excess complexes remain in circulation.
3Tissue deposition
Complexes lodge in small vessels, joints, kidneys, or lungs.
4Complement activation
Chemotactic signals are generated.
5Neutrophil/macrophage injury
Proteolytic enzymes and ROS damage surrounding tissue.

What this flow means: the target is not one fixed cell. The damage occurs wherever circulating complexes become deposited.

Lecture Figure 23, p. 11. Sensitization generates antigen-specific T cells; re-exposure activates T-cell cytokine release and macrophage-mediated inflammation.

Type IV: delayed, T-cell-mediated injury

Type IV is not antibody-mediated. T cells produce localized inflammation, tissue damage, and cell death, usually after 24–72 hours.

Two phases

  1. Sensitization phase: first antigen exposure generates memory T cells; no allergic reaction occurs during this first exposure.
  2. Elicitation phase: on re-exposure, memory T cells migrate to the antigen site and release inflammatory cytokines, producing tissue damage.
Recognition cue

Delayed 24–72 hours + T cells + no antibody = Type IV.

Type III: soluble antigen–antibody complexes circulate, deposit, activate complement, and recruit neutrophils. Type IV: sensitized T cells produce a delayed cytokine-driven reaction 24–72 hours after exposure.

Cram Sheet

Core framework

  • Innate: rapid, non-specific, no memory.
  • Adaptive: specific, delayed initially, memory, stronger on re-exposure.
  • Defense sequence: barrier → sensor → cytokine → effector → antigen presentation → clonal response → memory.

Physiologic barriers

  • Tight junctions: prevent penetration.
  • Mucus + cilia: prevent adhesion, trap, clear.
  • Lysozyme: dissolves some bacterial walls.
  • Defensins: make membrane holes.
  • Acid / enzymes / fatty acids: hostile chemical environment.

Microbial sensors

  • TLR: transmembrane PRR; PAMP → cytokines.
  • NLR: cytoplasmic microbial products → NF-κB.
  • RIG-I-like/MDA5: viral ssRNA → type I IFN.
  • TLR4: Gram-negative LPS.
  • TLR5: flagellin.
  • TLR3: viral dsRNA; TLR7/8: viral ssRNA; TLR9: microbial DNA.

Phagocytosis

IL-8/IL-17 attraction → endothelial adhesion (P-selectin) → migration → phagosome → acid pH 3.5–4.0 + ROS + NO + antimicrobial peptides → killing → neutrophil apoptosis.

Opsonins: antibody, classical complement products, alternative-pathway C3. Macrophage receptors bind Fc and C3.

NK cells

  • Kill virus-infected and tumor cells.
  • Contain perforin + granzyme.
  • Perform ADCC using Fc receptors.
  • Produce IFN-γ.

Complement

  • Classical: antibody.
  • Alternative: microbial surface.
  • Lectin: MBL/MASP.
  • C3a/C4a/C5a: anaphylatoxins.
  • C3b: opsonization.
  • C5b–C9: MAC lysis.

Adaptive cells

  • T cells: bone marrow origin, thymic maturation, cell-mediated immunity.
  • B cells: bone marrow development, unique immunoglobulin receptor, plasma + memory cells.
  • CD4: helper, MHC II.
  • CD8: cytotoxic, MHC I.

MHC

MHC I: all nucleated cells; HLA-A/B/C; endogenous; ER; CD8; direct killing.

MHC II: APCs; HLA-DP/DQ/DR; exogenous; endosome/lysosome; CD4; coordinated response.

  1. Complement-mediated lysis
  2. Enhanced phagocytosis / opsonization

Responses and immunity forms

  • Primary: slow; IgM first; memory formed.
  • Secondary: fast, high, persistent IgG, higher affinity.
  • Active: own antibodies, delayed, long duration, memory.
  • Passive: preformed antibody, immediate, short duration, no memory.

Last 20-second recall

Innate acts now. Adaptive remembers. MHC I shows inside to CD8. MHC II shows outside to CD4. IgM comes first; IgG comes back stronger; IgA guards mucosa; IgE drives allergy. Type I is immediate, Type II targets cells, Type III deposits complexes, Type IV is delayed T-cell injury.
