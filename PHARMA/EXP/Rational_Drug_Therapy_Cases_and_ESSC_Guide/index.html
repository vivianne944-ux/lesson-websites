What this file fixes

How the cases are actually solved

The lecture gives the sequence and the completed ESSC tables, but it does not fully spell out the thinking between the case stem and every plus sign. This reviewer makes that reasoning visible without changing the lecture’s chosen answers.

Source: Trans 1.06 V1, pp. 2–8
First decision

What is the problem?

Turn the stem into a concise diagnosis using the age, pattern, severity, key positives, key negatives, examination findings, and likely cause.

Second decision

What must treatment achieve?

The primary therapeutic objective controls the rest of the analysis. A drug may affect a symptom but still be a poor choice if it does not address the main objective.

Final decision

Which option best fits?

Compare drug groups using Efficacy, Safety, Suitability, and Cost; select the winning group, then repeat ESSC for the specific drugs inside that group.

Your question about the pluses
Yes, the lecture states that scores run from + to ++++ and that drugs should be compared vertically or column-by-column. However, it does not provide fixed numerical cutoffs defining exactly when a drug deserves one, two, three, or four pluses. The scoring is therefore comparative and justified by the data written in each column.

How to analyze any sample case

Use the same order every time. Do not jump straight from the diagnosis to a favorite drug.

Lecture process

Build a one-line problem representation

Who is the patient? Include age, main symptom, time course, severity, important examination findings, comorbidities, current drugs, and clues that change treatment choice.

Example
“A 1-year-old child with frequent watery non-bloody stools and clinical signs of mild dehydration, without fever, vomiting, abdominal pain, or concerning examination findings.”

Define the diagnosis completely enough to guide treatment

Do not stop at the symptom. The lecture’s Case 2 includes the illness, hydration status, and probable etiology: acute gastroenteritis, mild dehydration, probably viral.

State the primary therapeutic objective

Phrase it as an action: suppress the cough, rehydrate and prevent further dehydration, stop an attack as soon as it starts, or relieve itch and clear wheals.

Common mistake
Treating a visible symptom while missing the actual priority. In Case 2, “stop the diarrhea” is not the primary objective; the lecture prioritizes rehydration.

List non-drug measures before drugs

These are part of rational therapy, not decorative extras: stop smoking, continue breastfeeding, avoid triggers, prevent scratching, and similar lecture-listed measures.

Create a reasonable inventory of effective drug groups

Only compare options that could plausibly address the condition or objective. The lecture first scores groups, not individual brands or formulations.

Fill one ESSC column at a time

Compare all groups under Efficacy, then all under Safety, then Suitability, then Cost. This prevents the first drug you read from setting an unfair standard.

Select the winning group, then repeat with specific drugs

The first winner is the P-drug group. The second round identifies the specific P-drug. A lower-scoring option is not universally “wrong”; it can remain an alternative when circumstances change.

Write, counsel, and monitor

The final answer is not merely a drug name. It includes the prescription and patient information on effects, side effects, instructions, warnings, follow-up, and confirmation of understanding.

Case clues that usually affect Suitability

  • Age: pediatric or geriatric dose and dosage form
  • Occupation/routine: dosing convenience and ability to adhere
  • Comorbidities: contraindications and vulnerability
  • Current drugs: possible interactions
  • Local availability: practicality

Keep each fact in the correct column

  • MOA, PK, PD → Efficacy
  • Adverse effects and toxicity → Safety
  • Contraindications, age, interactions, form, frequency → Suitability
  • Total cost for the treatment duration → Cost

How to assign the pluses

The lecture gives a relative scale, not rigid cutoffs. Use these anchors to make your score defensible and consistent.

+ to ++++
MarkPractical anchorHow to phrase the rationale
−Not appropriate / disqualifying for this case or criterion.“Not indicated for this patient,” “no appropriate dosage form,” or another clear incompatibility shown by the case data.
+Clearly unfavorable; major limitations compared with the other options.“Has several important adverse effects,” “poor patient fit,” or “most expensive in the comparison.”
++Mixed or moderate; usable, but with meaningful limitations.“Effective but less convenient,” “similar safety but requires preparation,” or “mid-range cost.”
+++Good and favorable; only minor disadvantages.“Effective with manageable adverse effects,” “appropriate form and frequency,” or “relatively inexpensive.”
++++Best or most favorable among the options being compared.“Most directly achieves the objective,” “fewest relevant harms,” “best fit for this patient,” or “lowest total treatment cost.”
Important
This anchor scale is a practical interpretation to help you apply the lecture. The trans itself only states the range and the need for vertical comparison. A plus score is not a universal property of a drug; it may change when the patient, objective, dose, formulation, availability, or price changes.

Score vertically, not drug-by-drug

  1. Compare every option’s efficacy.
  2. Rank them and justify the differences.
  3. Move to safety only after efficacy is finished.
  4. Repeat for suitability and cost.

Do not contaminate columns

A drug should not lose Efficacy points because it has adverse effects; those belong under Safety. A pregnancy or age restriction belongs under Suitability, not Safety.

A four-question check before accepting a score
  1. Compared with what? A score only makes sense relative to the other options.
  2. For which patient? Suitability is patient-specific.
  3. For which objective? Efficacy must match the primary therapeutic objective.
  4. Can I defend every plus using the data inside that same column? If not, revise it.

How to think through it

What the stem already tells you

  • The acute cold symptoms have mostly resolved.
  • The remaining treatment target is the cough.
  • Smoking is a modifiable factor and is therefore included as a non-drug measure.
  • The patient’s work as a taxi driver is a patient-specific clue that should be considered under Suitability when comparing concrete drugs.

What you must not invent

The V1 lecture states that no ESSC criteria table was given for this sample case. Therefore, the lecture does not establish a winning group or a specific P-drug.

Correct exam behavior
Present the diagnosis, objective, non-drug measure, and inventory. Then explain what information you would compare under ESSC rather than fabricating the missing scores.
CriterionQuestions to answer before scoring the two groups
EfficacyHow well does each group suppress the persistent cough? What do its MOA, PK, and PD show?
SafetyWhat adverse effects, frequency, seriousness, predictability, and toxicity are listed?
SuitabilityDoes the choice fit the patient’s age, occupation, current medicines, contraindications, dosing schedule, and available forms?
CostWhat is the total cost for the intended treatment duration?

Sample Case 2

1-year-old with acute gastroenteritis and mild dehydration

This case is the clearest example of how the therapeutic objective controls ESSC selection.

pp. 3–4

A 1-year-old boy has seven episodes of loose watery, non-bloody, non-mucoid, non-foul-smelling stool, about half a cup per episode. There is no fever, vomiting, or abdominal pain. He remains active but is thirsty, with dry lips and slightly sunken eyes; pulses are full and the abdomen is soft and non-tender.

Diagnosis
Acute gastroenteritis, mild dehydration, probably viral
Objective
Rehydrate and prevent further dehydration
Non-drug measure
Continue breastfeeding
Why the diagnosis is written this way
The lecture includes the illness, the hydration status, and the probable etiology. That level of detail directs management better than writing only “diarrhea.”

Why ORS wins

The primary objective is rehydration. ORS directly fits that objective and is also rated best in Safety and Suitability for this child. Its total of 13 exceeds zinc sulfate’s 11 and the opioid agonist’s 5.

Why “stops diarrhea” is not enough

The opioid agonist may affect intestinal activity and therefore receives efficacy points, but it performs poorly in Safety and is marked not suitable for this patient. A drug can do something physiologically yet still lose the overall comparison.

Arithmetic note
The V1 table displays a total of 5 for the opioid agonist: +++ (3) + + (1) + − (0) + + (1) = 5.

Appendix mini case

Thirdy: recurrent itchy rash after outdoor activity

This appendix case applies the entire rational-therapy process, including group ESSC and specific-drug ESSC.

pp. 6–8

Thirdy is a 10-year-old boy who develops itchy maculopapular rashes on the chest, back, and extremities after playing outdoors during a provincial visit. Similar episodes occurred with climate changes and travel. Family history is positive for atopic dermatitis. Vital signs are stable, weight is 30 kg, breath sounds are clear, and the other systems are unremarkable.

Step 1 · Diagnose from the pattern

Mechanism described in the appendix

Antigen-triggered cutaneous mast-cell degranulation releases mediators such as histamine. Histamine signaling produces itch, while vasodilation and increased permeability contribute to edema and the wheal-and-flare appearance.

Why the appendix calls it physical urticaria

The condition is recurrent and linked to climate changes, travel, heat, and outdoor activity. The appendix identifies the pattern as physical urticaria / chronic inducible urticaria.

Therapeutic objectives
  • Relieve itchiness
  • Clear rash and wheals
  • Maintain skin integrity and prevent infection
  • Identify triggers
  • Educate the child and family
Non-drug measures
  • Wear sunscreen daily
  • Avoid tight clothing
  • Do not scratch
  • Keep nails short and clean
  • Minimize high temperature and excessive sweating

ESSC memory cue

  • E: Can it work?
  • S: What harm can it cause?
  • S: Does it fit this patient?
  • C: What is the whole-course cost?

Scoring memory cue

  • ++++ best in this comparison
  • +++ good
  • ++ mixed/moderate
  • + poor
  • − not appropriate
Exam traps
  • Do not put side effects under Efficacy.
  • Do not put age, pregnancy, contraindications, or drug interactions under Safety when the issue is patient fit; place them under Suitability.
  • Do not compare unit price alone when the full treatment duration differs.
  • Do not invent a winner for Case 1.
  • Do not hide arithmetic inconsistencies in the lecture tables; flag them.

Final sequence
