Overview · orientation only

One family, six organisms, three recurring questions

The lecture organizes the family by asking: How does the organism look and behave in the laboratory? How does it produce disease? and Which clinical pattern should make you recognize it? The major split is between opportunistic gut flora and true pathogens.

Opportunists: Escherichia, Klebsiella, Proteus, CitrobacterTrue pathogens: Salmonella, ShigellaShared core: Gram-negative, glucose-fermenting enteric rods

1. Identify

Start with lactose fermentation, then use motility, indole, citrate, urease, H₂S, and IMViC/TSI to narrow the organism.

2. Explain disease

Connect the virulence factor to the outcome: fimbriae → adherence, T3SS → cell manipulation, capsule → anti-phagocytosis, urease → alkaline urine and stones, and LPS → septic shock.

3. Recognize

Match the signature clue: metallic green sheen, black-centered colonies, bacillary dysentery, currant-jelly sputum, swarming with ammonia odor, or neonatal brain abscess.

Enterobacteriaceae foundations

pp. 1–2, 13–15

What the family is

The lecture presents these as Gram-negative enteric rods that are generally catalase positive, oxidase negative, nitrate positive, and able to ferment glucose. The family includes organisms that normally live in the gut but cause disease in the wrong site, as well as organisms that are intrinsically pathogenic.

Why the opportunist/pathogen split matters

Opportunists become dangerous after displacement, host vulnerability, device exposure, or transformation. True pathogens are emphasized as organisms that directly cause disease after acquisition. This distinction helps explain why some infections are healthcare-associated while others follow contaminated food or water.

Antigens: the repeating framework

O antigen · somatic

Part of LPS. It is linked to endotoxin activity and immune recognition. All members in the lecture have an O antigen.

H antigen · flagellar

Represents flagella and motility. The lecture mnemonic marks Shigella and Klebsiella as lacking H antigen.

K antigen · capsular

Represents the capsule, which can reduce phagocytosis. The lecture marks Shigella and Proteus as lacking K antigen.

Other antigens

Fimbrial adhesins, invasion plasmid antigens, siderophores, and related structures explain adherence, invasion, biofilm, or nutrient acquisition.

Lecture mnemonics: FaKSHet = Klebsiella and Shigella lack H antigen; K(e)SH P = Shigella and Proteus lack K antigen; “Kawawa ako lang walang exotoxin” = Klebsiella is listed as the only member without exotoxin. p. 1

How the laboratory tree works

1. MacConkey lactose reactionPink suggests lactose fermentation; colorless/pale suggests non-fermentation or delayed fermentation.
2. MotilitySeparates non-motile Shigella/Klebsiella from motile organisms.
3. Indole/citrate/ureaseRefines E. coli, Proteus, Klebsiella, Citrobacter, and related genera.
4. TSI/H₂SDistinguishes organisms such as Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus, and some Citrobacter.

What this flow means: no single test identifies the family member. The lecture uses a sequence of reactions, with morphology and clinical context supporting the final identification.

Lock It In

Core concept: identify by a branching pattern, not one isolated test. Main differentiator: lactose fermentation and motility. Key mechanism: O/H/K and other virulence structures repeatedly explain disease. Exam cue: black center = think H₂S producer; mucoid colony = think capsule.

Escherichia

Escherichia coli

pp. 1–3, 13–15

Identity: beneficial gut flora and major pathogen; leading cause of community- and hospital-acquired UTIs, with important diarrheal, septic, pulmonary, and neonatal disease.

Laboratory identity

Short Gram-negative rods; large gray moist colonies on blood agar; pink/red on MacConkey; metallic green sheen on EMB. Motile, indole positive, lactose fermenting.

IMViC: ++--; TSI: A/A with gas, no H₂S.

Virulence identity

P fimbriae anchor UPEC to urinary tissue; intimin anchors intestinal strains; T3SS produces attaching-and-effacing lesions; toxins include LPS, LT/ST, and Shiga toxins.

Clinical identity

Watery diarrhea from ETEC/EPEC, bloody diarrhea without high fever from EHEC, UTI/pyelonephritis, urosepsis, neonatal meningitis, and HUS.

Pathogenesis: from attachment to disease

AdherenceUPEC uses P fimbriae; intestinal EPEC/EHEC use intimin to resist flushing.
Cell manipulationT3SS injects proteins and rearranges host actin.
A/E lesionAttaching-and-effacing damage reduces water absorption, producing watery diarrhea.
Toxin outcomeLT/ST drive water and salt secretion; Shiga toxin stops protein production and may cause colonic bleeding and HUS; LPS can trigger shock.

What this flow means: E. coli disease depends on the strain. The organism first secures attachment, then either alters fluid handling, damages intestinal cells, or spreads outside the gut.

Host response

  • Mechanical flushing: gut hypermotility/diarrhea and urinary frequency.
  • Innate: TLR4 recognizes LPS; TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 cause inflammation and fever; neutrophils produce pyuria.
  • Adaptive: antibodies target O, H, and K antigens, neutralize toxins, block motility, and opsonize the capsule.

HUS emergency

Lecture sequence: 5–10 days after diarrheal illness, apparent improvement may be followed by hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney failure with oliguria/anuria and edema. About 5%–10% of STEC infections are stated to progress to HUS.

Diagnosis → interpretation

Routine identification

Use urine, stool, or blood as appropriate. Urine dipstick may show nitrites and leukocyte esterase. MacConkey turns bright pink; EMB shows metallic sheen. IMViC is ++--.

Pathogenic strain tests

SMAC: EHEC O157:H7 forms colorless/pale colonies while regular E. coli turns pink. ELISA detects Stx1/Stx2. PCR detects stx1/stx2, lt/st, or eae depending on the pathotype.

Management and prevention stated in the lecture

Intestinal infection

ORS for mild–moderate cases and IV Lactated Ringer’s when severe or unable to tolerate intake. The lecture states that loperamide is contraindicated when toxin-producing infection is suspected and that antibiotics are restricted in EHEC/STEC because of HUS risk.

Extraintestinal infection

Lecture-listed options: nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, or TMP-SMX for uncomplicated cystitis; ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin for complicated UTI/pyelonephritis; carbapenems or piperacillin-tazobactam for ESBL sepsis; high-dose IV cephalosporin combinations for neonatal meningitis.

Prevention anchor: WASH, thorough cooking, avoiding cross-contamination, safe water, well chlorination, hand hygiene, hospital infection control, and antimicrobial stewardship.

Lock It In

Core: E. coli spans normal flora, UTI, diarrhea, sepsis, and neonatal meningitis. Differentiator: lactose fermenter with metallic green sheen and IMViC ++--. Sequence: adherence → T3SS/A/E or toxin → diarrhea/systemic effect. Exam cue: bloody diarrhea + no high fever + HUS risk = EHEC.

Salmonella

Salmonella

pp. 3–5, 13–15

Identity: a true pathogen divided clinically into non-typhoidal food poisoning and typhoidal systemic enteric fever.

Laboratory identity

Gram-negative rods, non-lactose fermenting, motile, indole negative, urease negative. On SS/XLD/HE media, colonies develop black centers from H₂S.

Antigenic identity

O antigen in LPS, diphasic H antigen with phase variation, and Vi capsular antigen especially in S. Typhi.

Clinical identity

Rapid gastroenteritis after contaminated food versus a staged four-week typhoid pattern with stepladder fever, Faget sign, rose spots, typhoid state, and perforation risk.

Pathogenesis: why non-typhoidal and typhoidal disease diverge

M-cell invasionSalmonella enters through M cells over Peyer patches.
SPI-1 / T3SS-1Effectors rearrange actin and cause membrane ruffling, pulling the organism into the cell.
SCV survivalSPI-2 / T3SS-2 modifies the Salmonella-containing vacuole and prevents lysosomal digestion.
Path splitsNon-typhoidal strains remain local and inflame the gut; typhoidal strains use the Vi capsule to survive in macrophages and reach liver, spleen, and marrow.

What this flow means: both groups invade, but the ability to persist in macrophages and disseminate converts intestinal infection into systemic typhoid fever.

Host response

  • TLR4 senses LPS; TLR5 senses flagella.
  • Caspase-1 drives pyroptosis and release of IL-1β/IL-18.
  • Neutrophilic proteolysis contributes to intestinal injury in gastroenteritis.
  • In typhoid, macrophages transport hidden bacteria through the bloodstream.
  • Th1 IFN-γ reactivates macrophage killing; antibodies opsonize O and H antigens.

Typhoid progression

Week 1: stepladder fever to persistent 40°C, relative bradycardia, headache, myalgia, dry cough, fatigue. Week 2: rose spots, distension, stool change. Week 3: typhoid state, hepatosplenomegaly, hemorrhage/perforation. Week 4: slow recovery or death from septic shock.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis

Gastroenteritis: stool or rectal swab. Typhoid: blood culture is the lecture’s first-week gold standard; stool/urine cultures become useful in weeks 2–3; bone marrow culture is most sensitive even after antibiotics. TSI is stated as K/A + gas + H₂S. Serotyping uses O, H, and Vi antigens. Widal is presented with false-positive limitations.

Management

Uncomplicated non-typhoidal disease: fluids and no routine antibiotic; avoid anti-diarrheals. High-risk bacteremia: lecture lists ciprofloxacin or ceftriaxone. Enteric fever: ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin, azithromycin for MDR; dexamethasone as adjunct in severe disease; surgery for perforation. Prevention includes food safety, WASH, and typhoid vaccination.

Lock It In

Core: Salmonella invades M cells and survives in an SCV. Differentiator: motile non-lactose fermenter with H₂S black centers. Sequence: SPI-1 entry → SPI-2 survival → local gastroenteritis or macrophage dissemination. Exam cue: fever + relative bradycardia + rose spots + altered mentation = typhoid.

Shigella

Shigella

pp. 5–7, 13–15

Identity: a highly contagious true pathogen of the colon causing bacillary dysentery, with an unusually low infectious dose.

Laboratory identity

Gram-negative short rods; non-lactose fermenting, non-motile, no capsule, no H₂S. Colorless on MacConkey and SS; red/pink translucent on XLD.

Virulence identity

Large virulence plasmid encodes IpaB/IpaC/IpaD for T3SS and IcsA (VirG) for actin-based lateral movement. S. dysenteriae type 1 produces Shiga toxin.

Clinical identity

Large-intestinal disease with painful frequent stools containing blood, mucus, and pus. As few as 10–100 cells may cause infection; symptoms appear in 1–3 days.

Pathogenesis: why dysentery becomes bloody and painful

M-cell gatewayShigella crosses the mucosal barrier through M cells.
Macrophage pyroptosisT3SS effectors trigger macrophage rupture, releasing bacteria near the basolateral epithelial surface.
Lateral spreadIcsA generates an actin “rocket tail,” allowing cell-to-cell movement without returning to the extracellular space.
UlcerationCell death plus neutrophil migration disrupt tight junctions, leaving raw bleeding ulcers; Shiga toxin further stops protein synthesis.

What this flow means: Shigella does not simply increase secretion. It physically invades and destroys colonic tissue, explaining blood, mucus, pus, pain, and urgency.

Host response

Pyroptotic immune cells release IL-1β and IL-18, recruiting neutrophils. Neutrophil movement and bacterial intracellular killing together cause sloughing and loss of absorption. Secretory IgA targets O antigen and T3SS proteins to block renewed invasion.

Complications

  • Febrile seizures and encephalopathy in children
  • HUS from S. dysenteriae type 1 Shiga toxin
  • Toxic megacolon with paralysis and possible rupture
  • Post-infectious reactive arthritis: arthritis, urethritis, conjunctivitis in HLA-B27-associated cases

Diagnosis, management, prevention

Diagnosis

Use fresh stool containing blood/mucus/pus; if delayed beyond 1–2 hours, use Cary-Blair or buffered glycerol saline and keep cool. Fecal smear shows many neutrophils and RBCs. TSI: K/A, no gas, no H₂S. Motility and urease are negative. PCR detects ipaH; ELISA/PCR may detect Shiga toxin.

Management and prevention

Lecture lists oral/IV rehydration and targeted azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, or ceftriaxone, with resistance noted. Anti-diarrheals are strictly contraindicated because they trap invasive organisms/toxin. Prevention: hand hygiene, isolation, disinfection, safe water, and fly control.

Lock It In

Core: Shigella is an intracellular colonic invader. Differentiator: non-motile, no H₂S, very low infectious dose. Sequence: M cell → macrophage pyroptosis → IcsA lateral spread → ulceration. Exam cue: painful small-volume bloody/mucoid stools = bacillary dysentery.

Klebsiella

Klebsiella

pp. 7–8, 13–15

Identity: a heavily encapsulated opportunistic pathogen associated with healthcare infection, necrotizing pneumonia, catheter UTI, sepsis, and resistant strains.

Laboratory identity

Large wet mucoid colonies; lactose fermenter; non-motile; urease positive. MacConkey: large bright pink-red mucoid colonies. EMB: mucoid pink-purple without E. coli’s green sheen.

Virulence identity

Thick K capsule blocks phagocytosis and traps complement; Type 1/3 fimbriae support adherence and device biofilm; siderophores acquire iron; LPS drives shock.

Clinical identity

Currant-jelly sputum, lung necrosis/abscess/cavitation, catheter-associated UTI, hvKP liver abscess with metastatic spread, and severe bacteremia.

Pathogenesis: the “armor + attachment + iron” pattern

Capsule armorThick K polysaccharide prevents macrophage/neutrophil phagocytosis and diverts complement.
Fimbrial attachmentType 1/3 fimbriae bind tissue and medical devices; Type 3 supports biofilm.
Iron captureSiderophores remove iron from host proteins and fuel rapid growth.
Inflammatory damageLPS activates TLR4; neutrophil NETosis and proteolytic enzymes produce alveolar necrosis and bloody thick mucus.

What this flow means: the capsule prevents clearance while fimbriae and siderophores allow persistence and growth. The host’s intense neutrophil response then contributes to destructive pneumonia.

Clinical manifestations

  • Lobar pneumonia: sudden fever/chills, severe productive cough, currant-jelly sputum, pleurisy, dyspnea, cyanosis; CXR may show abscesses/cavitation.
  • UTI: dysuria, urgency, frequency, cloudy alkaline urine, pyelonephritis; emphasized in catheter users.
  • Hypervirulent syndrome: pyogenic liver abscess with endophthalmitis or meningitis, even in previously healthy people.
  • Sepsis: tachycardia, tachypnea, altered mental state, shock, multiorgan failure.

Resistance and high-risk groups

The lecture emphasizes NICU/ICU patients, chronic disease, hemodialysis, and device exposure. It reports increasing carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae and high mortality in multidrug-resistant infection.

Diagnosis and treatment logic

Diagnosis

Gram-negative short plump rods; capsule may be shown by India ink. String test >5 mm supports hypermucoviscosity. IMViC is listed as --++; urease positive, motility negative, TSI A/A with heavy gas and no H₂S. PCR targets carbapenemase genes such as blaKPC, blaNDM, blaOXA-48, and blaVIM.

Management and prevention

Lecture-listed choices: ceftriaxone, piperacillin-tazobactam, or levofloxacin for susceptible strains; carbapenems for ESBL; ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, polymyxins, or tigecycline for CRE/KPC. Support respiratory failure, minimize devices, use isolation and environmental disinfection, and practice stewardship.

Lecture discrepancy: page 7 lists Klebsiella as oxidase positive, while the family framework and appendix list Enterobacteriaceae/Klebsiella as oxidase negative.

Lock It In

Core: capsule-driven immune evasion plus device biofilm. Differentiator: large mucoid lactose-fermenting colonies and non-motility. Sequence: capsule/fimbriae/siderophores → persistence → neutrophilic necrosis. Exam cue: currant-jelly sputum + cavitary lobar pneumonia = Klebsiella.

Proteus

Proteus

pp. 8–10, 13–15

Identity: an opportunistic urinary pathogen whose defining chain is swarming → urease → alkaline urine → crystalline biofilm → struvite/staghorn stones.

Laboratory identity

Non-lactose fermenter, motile, H₂S positive, strong urease positive, PAD positive, gelatin liquefaction positive. On blood/nutrient agar it forms concentric swarming rings and may release a strong ammonia odor.

Virulence identity

Peritrichous flagella generate swarming; MR/P fimbriae attach to urinary tissue/catheters; urease raises pH; biofilm traps crystals and blocks antibiotics and immune access.

Clinical identity

Complicated UTI with ammonia odor, alkaline urine, pyuria/hematuria, pyelonephritis, struvite or staghorn calculi, catheter blockage, wounds, bacteremia, and shock.

Stone formation, step by step

1. Swarming ascentOn a solid surface, Proteus becomes elongated and hyperflagellated, moving against urine flow.
2. Firm adherenceIt returns to short cells and uses MR/P fimbriae to lock onto bladder/kidney or catheter surfaces.
3. Urease reactionUrea is hydrolyzed to ammonia and carbamate; ammonia generates ammonium and hydroxyl ions, raising urine from about pH 6 to >7.2, often 8–9.
4. Crystal precipitationStruvite and carbonate apatite precipitate in alkaline urine.
5. Crystalline biofilmMinerals become trapped in biofilm, producing stones, catheter crusts, antibiotic exclusion, and recurrent infection.

What this flow means: urease is not just a lab result. It directly changes urine chemistry, injures urothelium, precipitates minerals, and creates a protected reservoir of infection.

Host response

TLR4 senses LPS and induces IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. IL-8 recruits neutrophils, creating pyuria. Stones cannot be phagocytosed and mechanically damage urinary tissue, causing chronic inflammation and hematuria. Antibodies may target flagella and urease.

Emergencies

  • Pyelonephritis: fever/chills, CVA tenderness, nausea/vomiting
  • CAUTI obstruction: crystalline crust blocks drainage and may trigger autonomic dysreflexia
  • Extraintestinal spread: wounds, bacteremia, septic shock

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis

Urinalysis: alkaline pH, nitrites, leukocyte esterase. Blood agar: swarming rings. MacConkey: pale/colorless. Urease rapidly turns medium bright fuchsia. TSI: K/A with heavy H₂S. PAD and gelatin liquefaction are positive. The lecture also describes the legacy Weil-Felix cross-reaction using OX-19, OX-2, and OX-K Proteus strains.

Management and prevention

Lecture-listed antimicrobials include ampicillin, cephalexin, or ciprofloxacin, with carbapenems/piperacillin-tazobactam for ESBL strains. Complete stone removal by PCNL or ESWL is emphasized; acetohydroxamic acid is listed for chronic unresectable stones. Prevention centers on closed catheter drainage, hydration/urine acidification measures, and hand hygiene.

Lecture nuance: the general Proteus profile lists indole negative, while the lecture separately notes that Proteus vulgaris is indole positive.

Lock It In

Core: Proteus changes urine chemistry. Differentiator: swarming + strong urease + ammonia odor. Sequence: attachment → urease → alkaline pH → struvite/apatite → crystalline biofilm. Exam cue: recurrent UTI with staghorn calculus = Proteus.

Citrobacter

Citrobacter

pp. 10–12, 13–15

Identity: an emerging opportunistic healthcare-associated pathogen, particularly dangerous in neonates, immunosuppressed patients, and debilitated elderly patients.

Laboratory identity

Gram-negative motile rods; citrate positive; variable lactose/indole/urease behavior by species. C. freundii may slowly ferment lactose and produce H₂S; C. koseri may be non-lactose fermenting and H₂S negative.

Virulence identity

Some strains have a Vi-like capsule; LPS drives shock; fimbriae aid adherence; AmpC enzymes and mobile resistance genes protect against antibiotics; selected strains may cause A/E lesions or produce Shiga toxin.

Clinical identity

Nosocomial UTI, gastroenteritis, intra-abdominal/wound infection, sepsis, and—most distinctively—C. koseri neonatal meningitis with brain abscess.

Pathogenesis: three lecture pathways

Gut A/E lesion

The LEE locus encodes T3SS effectors that rearrange actin into pedestals, destroy absorptive microvilli, and lead to watery diarrhea.

Shiga toxin in selected strains

Some C. freundii strains are stated to acquire Stx1/Stx2, stop protein production, and produce bloody diarrhea with potential kidney failure.

BBB crossing

C. koseri travels in macrophages and uses outer membrane proteins to cross brain endothelial barriers, causing neonatal meningitis, liquefactive necrosis, and abscess formation.

Host response

TLR4 recognizes O antigen and TLR5 recognizes H antigen, triggering IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α with neutrophil recruitment. This response contributes to intestinal injury and, in the CNS, liquefactive necrosis and pus. Adaptive antibodies target O, H, and Vi-like capsular antigens.

Complications

  • Neonatal meningitis/sepsis in infants under 2 months
  • Brain abscesses, seizures, bulging fontanelle, rapid deterioration, developmental delay or death
  • Biliary infection, surgical-site/soft-tissue infection
  • Endotoxic bacteremia and shock

Diagnosis, management, prevention

Diagnosis

Use urine, wound, blood, or CSF. MacConkey and EMB patterns vary; C. freundii may form H₂S black centers on XLD/HE. Citrate and ONPG are positive. Automated identification includes MALDI-TOF and VITEK/MicroScan. The lecture emphasizes AST, AmpC/ESBL testing, and molecular panels.

Management and prevention

For severe infection, the lecture lists cefepime or carbapenems because of the AmpC trap. Neonatal meningitis/brain abscess: high-dose IV meropenem or cefepime plus surgical drainage. Mild cystitis: ciprofloxacin or TMP-SMX. Gastroenteritis: ORS; loperamide is banned when Shiga toxin is a concern. Prevention: nursery/NICU surveillance, device stewardship, WASH, and hand/contact precautions.

Lock It In

Core: Citrobacter is a variable opportunist with important resistance and neonatal CNS disease. Differentiator: citrate positive, species-variable lactose/indole/H₂S. Sequence: adhesion/A/E or macrophage transport → tissue injury; C. koseri → BBB crossing → abscess. Exam cue: neonatal meningitis with brain abscess = C. koseri.

Do not confuse these pairs

Salmonella vs Shigella

Both are non-lactose fermenters and may give K/A TSI. Salmonella is motile and H₂S positive; Shigella is non-motile and H₂S negative. Salmonella often produces black-centered colonies; Shigella stays colorless.

E. coli vs Klebsiella

Both rapidly ferment lactose. E. coli is motile, indole positive, and has a metallic green EMB sheen. Klebsiella is non-motile, heavily mucoid, capsule-rich, and lacks the green sheen.

Proteus vs Salmonella

Both may be motile non-lactose fermenters with H₂S. Proteus shows swarming and strong urease activity; Salmonella is urease negative and is organized around M-cell invasion/SCV survival.

Citrobacter vs E. coli/Salmonella

Citrobacter can resemble either. Use citrate positivity, species-specific IMViC, variable lactose fermentation, and possible H₂S. Clinical context—especially neonatal CNS disease—may be decisive.

Cram Sheet

entire lecture
Fastest way to retrieve the lecture: identify the organism by colony + motility + a standout test, then connect its virulence factor to the signature clinical pattern.

Family core

Gram-negative, glucose-fermenting enteric rods; generally catalase+, oxidase−, nitrate+. O = somatic/LPS, H = flagella, K = capsule.

Antigen mnemonics

FaKSHet: Klebsiella + Shigella lack H. K(e)SH P: Shigella + Proteus lack K. Klebsiella is lecture-listed as the only one without exotoxin.

E. coli

Pink MacConkey, green metallic EMB, motile, indole+, IMViC ++--. UPEC = P fimbriae. EPEC/EHEC = intimin/A-E. ETEC = LT/ST. EHEC O157:H7 = Shiga toxin, bloody diarrhea without high fever, HUS. Avoid loperamide and restrict antibiotics in suspected STEC.

Salmonella

Motile, non-lactose, H₂S black center. M cells → SPI-1 entry → SCV/SPI-2 survival. NTS stays local; Typhi uses Vi capsule/macrophages for systemic disease. Faget sign + rose spots + typhoid state. Blood culture week 1; bone marrow most sensitive.

Shigella

Non-motile, non-lactose, no H₂S, no capsule. 10–100 cells. M cell → macrophage pyroptosis → IcsA actin tail → lateral spread → dysentery. S. dysenteriae type 1: Shiga toxin/HUS. Avoid anti-diarrheals.

Klebsiella

Large pink mucoid colonies, non-motile, urease+, capsule. Type 1/3 fimbriae + siderophores + LPS. Currant-jelly sputum, abscess/cavitation, catheter UTI. String test >5 mm = hypermucoviscosity. CRE genes: KPC, NDM, OXA-48, VIM.

Proteus

Swarming, strong urease, H₂S+, ammonia odor, PAD+. Urease → NH₃/OH− → alkaline urine → struvite/apatite → staghorn stone + crystalline biofilm. P. vulgaris may be indole+.

Citrobacter

Citrate+, variable lactose/indole/H₂S. C. freundii: often H₂S and AmpC. C. koseri: neonatal meningitis + brain abscess. May use A/E lesion, selected Stx, or OMP-mediated BBB crossing.

TSI snapshot

E. coli: A/A gas, no H₂S. Salmonella: K/A gas + H₂S. Shigella: K/A, no gas/H₂S. Klebsiella: A/A gas, no H₂S. Proteus: K/A + H₂S. Citrobacter: variable ± H₂S.

Top emergencies

EHEC/Shigella: HUS. Salmonella: intestinal perforation, iNTS sepsis. Klebsiella: necrotizing pneumonia, hvKP metastatic spread, CRE sepsis. Proteus: obstruction/pyelonephritis/sepsis. Citrobacter: neonatal CNS abscess and shock.

Exam traps

Salmonella vs Shigella: motility/H₂S. E. coli vs Klebsiella: motility, indole, green sheen vs mucoid capsule. Proteus vs Salmonella: urease and swarming. Citrobacter: can mimic both lactose fermenters and H₂S producers.

Lecture discrepancies to remember

Salmonella citrate is inconsistent between its organism section and appendix. Klebsiella oxidase is listed positive in one section but negative in the family/appendix. The Citrobacter section repeats the Proteus-based Weil-Felix description.
