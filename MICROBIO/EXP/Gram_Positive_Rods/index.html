Must-know framework

Use the same six questions for every organism

  1. What does it look like? Rod, curved rod, branching filament, arrangement, spore.
  2. Where does it come from? Soil, animals, food, water, respiratory droplets, normal flora.
  3. How does it enter? Inhalation, ingestion, wound contamination, or intracellular invasion.
  4. What makes it pathogenic? Capsule, toxin, adhesin, motility, or enzymes.
  5. What pattern does it produce? Eschar, pseudomembrane, diarrhea, meningitis, ulcer, or soft-tissue infection.
  6. How is it recognized? Culture appearance, biochemical test, motility, hemolysis, or antigen/toxin test.

Foundation

Sporulation and germination

Understand the environmental switch between the growing form and the resistant dormant form.

PDF p. 1
Simple idea

One organism, two survival states

Vegetative cells grow when conditions support life. Endospores form when moisture or nutrients become inadequate, allowing the organism to remain dormant and resistant.

Vegetative cell

  • Develops under favorable conditions: adequate nutrients, temperature, and moisture.
  • In living animals and humans, the lecture describes bacilli as being in the vegetative form.

Endospore

  • Forms under unfavorable conditions such as low moisture and low nutrients.
  • Contains the genetic material enclosed in several protein coats.
  • Resists heat, drying, and most chemicals and can remain viable for years in contaminated pasture.

The complete switch

Favorable conditionsnutrients + moisture + suitable temperature
→
Vegetative cellgrowth and multiplication
⇄
Environmental stresslow nutrients / low moisture
→
Sporulationdormant resistant endospore
→
Germinationmoist, nutrient-rich surface at 37°C

What this flow means: sporulation is the survival response to depletion; germination is the return to active growth when favorable conditions return.

Spore resistance

  • Extremely resistant to chemical and physical environments.
  • Lecture states: inactivated at 120°C for 15 minutes by autoclave.
  • The soil or a dead animal provides depleted conditions that favor the spore form.

Culture-time clue

A 72-hour culture may already show spores, whereas a 24-hour culture may not. The key is that sporulation needs time and unfavorable conditions to develop.

Major Gram-positive bacillus

Bacillus anthracis

Connect environmental spores, the poly-D-glutamic acid capsule, the three-part toxin, and the three routes of human disease.

PDF p. 1–2

Identity and epidemiologic pattern

B. anthracis is a large, straight, right-angle-ended bacillus whose durable spores persist in soil and animal products. Herbivores are the primary hosts; humans are accidental hosts.

Large bacillusStraight rodRight-angle endsSingle or pairs

Spore characteristics

  • Oval, centrally located, and does not distend the cell.
  • Dormant in cultures older than 24 hours, soil, and dead animals.

Source

Contaminated blood and tissues of animals, including hides, wool, and hair.

Three human routes

RouteExposureLecture-linked disease
Skin contactContaminated animal products through a woundMalignant pustule / cutaneous anthrax
IngestionContaminated food productGastroenteritis
InhalationContaminated dustWoolsorter’s disease

The route determines the first tissue involved, but systemic spread may occur.

Virulence factor 1

Capsule: the antiphagocytic shield

  • Antigenic and made of D-glutamic acid.
  • Present in infected animals or humans.
  • Encoded by plasmid pXO2.
  • Its major lecture-stated function is antiphagocytosis.

The macrophage can engulf the organism, but the capsule and exotoxin make it difficult to destroy.

Virulence factor 2

Exotoxin: three components working together

  • Encoded by plasmid pXO1 and described as heat-labile.
  • Protective antigen (PA): attachment/adherence.
  • Edema factor (EF): generates cAMP and inhibits neutrophil phagocytosis.
  • Lethal factor (LF): induces macrophage death via inactivation of IL-1, TNF, and O₂ radicals.

All three act synergistically to produce systemic anthrax.

→

→
Macrophage releases IL-1, TNF, O₂ radicals
→
LF interferes with these defenses

This explains why attachment, antiphagocytosis, edema, and macrophage injury are taught as one coordinated pathogenic system.

Clinical recognition

FormMechanism / routeRecognition patternMajor consequence
CutaneousSpores enter a wound or subcutaneous tissue.Painless dark pustule / black eschar surrounded by vesicles; facial and neck edema.May enter circulation and sometimes produce septicemia.
PulmonaryInhalation of spores.Inflammation of pulmonary lymph nodes, hemorrhagic necrosis, respiratory distress, cyanosis.Lecture states death may occur within 24 hours if untreated.
GastrointestinalIngestion of contaminated food.Lecture lists gastroenteritis as the outcome.Potential systemic disease is not further explained in the main lecture section.

Laboratory identification

  1. Choose the specimen: malignant pustule, sputum, or blood for septicemia.
  2. Bicarbonate agar: demonstrates capsule; encapsulated bacteria form a mucoid colony.
  3. 5% BAP: irregular fringe-like edge / Medusa head.
  4. Gamma phage: lysis supports B. anthracis; no lysis supports B. cereus.
  5. Mouse inoculation: death in 2–5 days; lecture notes animal-testing concerns.
  6. ELISA: detects antibodies.

Treatment, prevention, and control

Treatment section

Ciprofloxacin or doxycycline with one or two additional antibiotics: rifampin, vancomycin, penicillin, or imipenem.

Control

  • Bury dead animals deeply to prevent spore spread to new pastures.
  • Gas sterilize hides and wool from epidemic areas.
  • Animals: live spore vaccine, Stearne strain.
  • Humans: killed spore vaccine to control an outbreak.

Lecture-specific inconsistency: drug of choice

The detailed treatment section lists ciprofloxacin or doxycycline with additional antibiotics, while the later summary table lists penicillin. Both are present in the lecture; this reviewer does not silently choose between them.

Biological-weapon relevance

The lecture identifies B. anthracis as a CDC Category A organism and describes the September 18, 2001 letter attacks, which caused 5 deaths and 17 infections.

Bacillus cereus & B. thuringiensis

The key contrast is between B. cereus food-associated enterotoxin disease and B. thuringiensis insect-larva toxicity.

B. cereus: what it is

B. cereus causes food poisoning, often linked to fried rice, cereals, pasta, milk, meat, and sauces. The lecture calls it “Chinese Restaurant Syndrome.”

Virulence factors

  • Enterotoxin, linked to the diarrheal effect.
  • Glycoprotein S-layer, used for attachment/adherence and interaction with polymorphonuclear cells.

Toxin-mediated protein-synthesis arrest

Corynebacterium diphtheriae

Recognize the organism by its clubbed ends and Chinese-letter arrangement, then connect the beta-prophage toxin to pseudomembrane formation and systemic complications.

PDF p. 3–4

Morphology first

The bacillus swells at one or both ends because of metachromatic granules, also called Babes–Ernst or volutin granules.

  • Gram-positive bacillus.
  • Arranged as X, Y, or V shapes: the “Chinese letter arrangement.”
  • Divides by snapping.

Exotoxin structure

  • Toxigenicity is a lysogenic property.
  • The toxin is encoded by a beta prophage carrying the tox gene.
  • Without this prophage, the organism is non-toxigenic.
  • Subunit B binds the cell surface.
  • Subunit A performs ADP-ribosylation and interrupts protein synthesis through Elongation Factor 2.

Step-by-step pathogenesis

  1. Subunit B binds the host membrane.
  2. Subunits A and B enter through the toxin receptor by endocytosis.
  3. Subunit A leaves the lysosome where cleavage occurs.
  4. Subunit A interrupts transfer of the polypeptide chain at the ribosome.
  5. Elongation Factor 2 ceases, halting protein synthesis.

Key idea: B gets the toxin into the cell; A performs the damaging intracellular action.

Clinical manifestations

Cutaneous

Via a punctured wound; can be caused by toxigenic or nontoxigenic strains. The lesion is shown as a deep erosive non-healing ulcer with a gray-brown membrane.

Respiratory

  • Colonizes tonsils, nasal cavity, and throat.
  • Toxin-induced epithelial death produces a greenish-to-gray pseudomembrane.
  • Pronounced lymph-node swelling produces the bull-neck appearance.

Complications

  • Airway obstruction if the inflamed membrane hardens.
  • Myocarditis.
  • Cranial nerve and muscle paralysis.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Specimen: nasopharyngeal or throat swab.
  • Biotypes: gravis, intermedius, mitis; each may be toxigenic or nontoxigenic.
  • Blood agar plate: lecture states black colonies.
  • Loeffler’s slant: demonstrates volutin granules.
  • Potassium tellurite: grayish-black colonies.
  • Elek toxigenicity test: antitoxin in filter paper reacts with bacterial toxin; precipitation from the positive control meets the test-organism line when toxigenic.

Treatment and prevention

  • Antitoxin prevents attachment of fragment B.
  • Penicillin is an adjunct to antitoxin.
  • Humans are the only reservoir.
  • Prevention uses a toxoid vaccine and Tdap.
  • Exposed contacts require prophylaxis and close surveillance.

Lecture mnemonic: ABCDEFG

ADP-ribosylation · Beta prophage · Corynebacterium · Diphtheriae · Elongation Factor · Granules (metachromatic).

Cold-tolerant facultative intracellular bacillus

Listeria monocytogenes

Its story is a sequence: food exposure → epithelial entry → phagosomal escape → actin-based movement → cell-to-cell spread → bacteremia and target-organ disease.

PDF p. 4–6

Who and what food are emphasized?

The lecture case links Listeria-contaminated frozen foods to severe outcomes in pregnant women, newborns, older adults, and immunocompromised people.

Risky foods

  • Sprouts
  • Raw / unpasteurized milk
  • Soft cheeses
  • Cold deli meats and hot dogs that are not heated
  • Smoked seafood

Recognition profile

  • Short Gram-positive bacilli, singly or in short chains, sometimes resembling streptococci.
  • Facultative intracellular: may be seen inside or outside cells.
  • Produces acid but not gas from carbohydrate utilization.
  • 13 serovars; serotype 4b is linked to foodborne outbreaks.
  • Tumbling motility at 25°C, not 37°C.
  • Catalase-positive, esculin hydrolysis-positive, narrow beta hemolysis.
  • Tolerates 2–4°C, low pH, and high salt.
  • Colonizes the GI and female genitourinary tract and is widespread in soil, plants, and animals.

Lecture-specific toxin note

The lecture lists Listeria as (+) endotoxin and provides a recall statement defining endotoxin as part of the cell wall. This is presented as a lecture-stated point without adding outside correction.

Seven-step intracellular life cycle

  1. Entry through InlA and InlB.
  2. Vacuolar lysis and escape by LLO and PlcA.
  3. Replication with ActA recruitment.
  4. Actin-based intracellular movement through polarized actin polymerization.
  5. Cell-to-cell spread by formation of Listeriapods.
  6. Lysis of the double-membrane vacuole by LLO and PlcB.
  7. The cycle repeats in the new host cell.

The cell-to-cell route allows spread without exposure to circulating antibodies, complement, and neutrophils.

Pathogenesis and clinical outcomes

Contaminated food reaches the gut → lymph nodes → bloodstream, producing bacteremia. From blood, the organism may reach:

  • Liver and spleen.
  • Brain: meningitis or meningoencephalitis.
  • Placenta: abortion and neonatal septicemia.

Risk-group pattern

  • Pregnancy: septicemia, placental abscess, spontaneous abortion.
  • Neonates: early-onset granulomatosis infantiseptica; late-onset neonatal meningitis.
  • Children and immunocompromised patients: meningitis; bacteremia and rarely focal infections are also listed.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Tumbling motility.
  • Beta hemolysis.
  • Catalase positive.
  • CSF: high PMN, low glucose, and normal protein as stated in the lecture.

Treatment and prevention

Lecture-listed agents: ampicillin, vancomycin, ciprofloxacin.

Pregnant and immunocompromised patients should avoid unpasteurized milk products and raw vegetables.

Four distinct recognition patterns

Minor Gram-positive bacilli

Occupation, ecological role, branching morphology, weak acid-fastness, and processing resistance are the main discriminators.

PDF p. 6–7

Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae

Animal pathogen, especially in swine; fishermen and butchers are commonly affected through direct contact with infected fish or pig meat.

  • Resists smoking, pickling, and desiccation.
  • Catalase-, oxidase-, and indole-negative.
  • Hyaluronidase hydrolyzes hyaluronic acid.
  • Neuraminidase cleaves N-acetylneuramic acid in surface glycoproteins and supports adhesion.

Human disease: erysipeloid

“Seal finger” or “whale finger”: painful, slowly spreading inflammation of traumatized skin.

Do Not Confuse

Erysipeloid is linked to E. rhusiopathiae; the lecture states erysipelas in humans is caused by Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus.

Diagnosis and control

  • Aspirate or biopsy from lesion margin.
  • BAP with 5–10% CO₂: alpha hemolysis.
  • Short-chain Gram-positive rods and H₂S production.
  • Penicillin; protect humans from infected animal secretions; live attenuated vaccine for animals.

Lactobacilli

Normal flora of the oral cavity, GIT, and vagina. Sugars in the gut and free glycogen in the vagina are used for energy, producing lactic acid and an acidic environment.

  • Non-sporeforming Gram-positive rods.
  • Non-motile, facultative anaerobes.
  • MRS agar: small, white, creamy colonies.

Uses

  • Main use: probiotic. The lecture quotes the WHO definition: live microorganisms that confer a health benefit when given in adequate amounts.
  • Sour milk, cheese, yogurt, and Yakult.
  • Taken by mouth to treat and prevent diarrhea.
  • Used in fermented vegetables, beverages, sourdough, and some sausages.

Streptomycetes

Gram-positive aerobic soil bacteria that form chains of spores at maturity and colorful colonies resembling fungi.

  • Decompose organic matter and contribute to earthy soil odor and soil fertility.
  • Mycetoma is linked to S. sudanensis and S. somaliensis; biopsy may show black granules.
  • Diagnosis: direct microscopy, colorful culture, histopathology, PCR.

Antibiotic production

Nocardia

Ubiquitous saprophytes in soil, water, and organic matter; also found in healthy gingiva as part of the normal flora.

  • Gram-positive branching filamentous rod.
  • Weak acid-fast: light pink; the lecture contrasts this with the darker pink of M. tuberculosis.
  • Inhalation may spread infection to lungs, brain, and kidney.
  • Traumatic implantation may produce mycetoma; N. brasiliensis is listed.

Diagnosis

  • Sputum or blood.
  • Direct microscopy: filamentous branching acid-fast bacilli with Gram-positive rods.
  • Histology: grains surrounded by neutrophils.
  • PCR.
  • BCYE: “breadcrumb” colonies after 2–10 days.

Campylobacter jejuni

Connect poultry and water exposure to a microaerophilic, seagull-shaped organism that attaches to the lower small bowel and colon.

Gastric survival through urease

Helicobacter pylori

The organism survives the stomach by neutralizing acid locally, moving through mucus, attaching to epithelium, and producing inflammatory injury.

PDF p. 7–9

Case-recognition profile

The lecture scenario features burning epigastric pain worse on an empty stomach, relief after meals, a duodenal ulcer, and a positive rapid urease test.

  • Gram-negative curved or spiral-shaped bacillus.
  • Highly motile with multiple polar flagella.
  • Microaerophilic.
  • “Triple positive”: urease, oxidase, catalase.
  • Helical rod with 4–5 lophotrichous flagella.

Isolated by Barry Marshall and Robin Warren in 1982 from a patient with gastritis and gastric ulcer.

Why urease matters

Urease converts urea into ammonium and carbon dioxide, producing a locally alkaline environment that helps the organism survive gastric acidity.

This is the first step in the lecture's mucosal-invasion sequence: survive first, then move, attach, and injure.

Four-step mucosal invasion

  1. Neutralization: urease activity prevents immediate destruction in the gastric lumen.
  2. Penetration: polar flagella propel the organism through viscous mucus.
  3. Attachment: surface adhesins bind gastric epithelial cells.
  4. Destruction: toxins and inflammation cause mucosal injury, exposing connective tissue to acid and forming ulcers.

The lecture gives an ulcer-size range of 0.6–12 cm.

Testing and eradication timing

When to test

  • Peptic ulcer.
  • First-degree relative with stomach cancer.
  • Selected dyspepsia cases.

Confirming eradication

  • Wait 4 weeks after treatment.
  • Use urea breath test or stool antigen.
  • A negative test confirms “clinical eradication.”

Treatment and prevention

  • Lecture regimen: 14 days of two antibiotics, such as amoxicillin and clarithromycin, plus a proton-pump inhibitor.
  • No licensed vaccine.
  • Improved sanitation, hand washing, and safe food/water practices.
  • The lecture states transmission may occur through saliva, including mouth-to-mouth contact and shared utensils/cups; it is not normal flora.

Philippine lecture notes

  • Prevalence stated as roughly 32%–60%, varying by region, socioeconomic status, and sanitation.
  • Class I carcinogen and a critical gastric-cancer risk factor.
  • Approximately 32% of H. pylori-positive patients are stated to present with peptic ulcer disease.

Lecture-labeled “foods that prevent colonization”

The lecture lists red wine, red-grape flavonoids, green tea, broccoli sprouts, garlic, and probiotics. These are presented as lecture content only; the lecture treatment section still specifies combination antibiotics plus a PPI.

Secretory diarrhea through cAMP

Vibrio cholerae

The core mechanism is an A-B enterotoxin that raises cAMP, drives chloride secretion, and causes rapid water and electrolyte loss.

PDF p. 9–10

Classification by O antigen

  • O1: includes classical and El Tor biotypes.
  • O139: Bengal strain.
  • Non-O groups include other vibrios such as V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, and V. alginolyticus.
  • Further differentiation may use Vibrio phage and polymyxin B resistance.

Epidemic strains

  • O1 El Tor N16961: associated with the ongoing 7th pandemic; described as hardier and often milder.
  • O139 Bengal: emerged in South Asia in the 1990s, has epidemic potential similar to O1, and is rarely reported today.
  • Both are described as showing multidrug resistance.

Biotype and history anchors

  • First through sixth pandemics: classical biotype, 1817–1923.
  • 1905: a new strain identified in El Tor, Egypt.
  • El Tor spread beginning in 1961 and is associated with more mild disease and more asymptomatic carriers.
  • 1992–1993: O139 epidemic in the Indian subcontinent.
  • Habitat of O139 is described as brackish water at river mouths, pH 6–10.

The lecture's biotype diagrams show serotypes Ogawa, Inaba, and Hikojima based on O-antigen differences.

Virulence architecture

FactorParts / function
Cholera toxinSubunit A: A1 has toxic activity; A2 links A to B. Subunit B performs cellular binding.
Toxin-coregulated pilus (TCP)Acts as receptor for CTX in the lecture.
MucinasePenetrates the mucus lining of the small intestine.

Five-step pathogenesis

  1. The A-B toxin is secreted.
  2. B subunits bind monosialosyl ganglioside.
  3. Subunit A stimulates formation of cAMP.
  4. cAMP activates mucosal membrane pumps that push chloride ions.
  5. Large amounts of water and electrolytes are expelled.

What this flow means: the major injury is rapid secretory fluid loss, not merely mucosal destruction.

Clinical pattern and dehydration

The lecture notes that not all patients manifest rice-water stool.

  • Vomiting and profuse diarrhea may occur after contaminated food or water.
  • Within 2–3 hours, fluid/electrolyte loss may cause tachycardia, low blood pressure, reduced skin elasticity, sunken eyes, dry mucosa, thirst, and muscle cramps.

Risk factors

  • Hypochlorhydria or achlorhydria.
  • Poor sanitation.
  • Antacid or H₂-blocker use.
  • Blood group O.

The lecture links these risks to an organism that lacks acid-resistance genes and therefore requires a relatively high infective dose.

Lecture gives several infective-dose values

The lecture states 10³–10⁸ in the main infection-dose section, later says 10⁶ bacterial cells, and elsewhere compares V. cholerae as 10³–10⁶. These values are preserved as an internal lecture inconsistency.

Treatment

  • Aggressive rehydration.
  • Administer antibiotics.
  • Treat complications.

Prevention and Philippine history

  • Discourage open-air defecation.
  • Use cresol or lime to disinfect stools and vomitus.
  • Provide clean drinking water.
  • First O1 biotype case in Manila: 1961.
  • Lecture states cholera is endemic in the Philippines and gives a 2025 cumulative total of 1,451 cases and 27 deaths.

Seafood and wound-associated vibrios

V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, V. alginolyticus

The key split is diarrheal seafood disease versus severe wound/septicemic disease in a vulnerable host.

PDF p. 10–11

Vibrio parahaemolyticus

A halophilic, oxidase-positive organism associated with brackish or fresh water and seafood, especially in summer.

Identity and transmission

  • Classified by K (capsular) antigens K1–K71 and O (somatic) antigens O1–O11.
  • The lecture notes the O3:K6 pandemic strain through 2006.
  • Historic foodborne transmission in Japan involved semidried juvenile sardines.

Clinical pattern

  • After 96 hours: gastroenteritis, cramps, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills.
  • Self-limiting in immunocompetent patients, resolving within 3 days.
  • May also cause septicemia.
  • Infective dose: 10⁷–10⁹.

Virulence and lab

  • Thermostable direct hemolysin (TDH) and TDH-related hemolysin (TRH).
  • Hemolysin produces reddish “meat-wash” watery stool, called the Kanagawa phenomenon.
  • Wagatsuma agar demonstrates hemolysis around colonies.
  • Urease is also listed.

TCBS distinction

Thiosulfate Citrate Bile Sucrose agar separates the two organisms by sucrose fermentation:

  • V. cholerae: ferments sucrose, so green medium turns yellow.
  • V. parahaemolyticus: no sucrose fermentation, so no yellow change.

This is a high-yield visual differentiator because both are vibrios but their carbohydrate reaction differs.

Vibrio vulnificus

  • Oxidase-positive fermenter and the most virulent non-cholera vibrio in the lecture.
  • Lactose-positive, capsulated, halophilic.
  • Naturally present in coastal and estuarine waters.
  • Transmission through raw/undercooked seafood or wound contact with contaminated seawater, including barefoot exposure and puncture wounds.
  • After 1–7 days: diarrhea and abdominal pain.
  • May cause septicemia, especially with underlying medical conditions, particularly liver disease.
  • Causes cellulitis and soft-tissue infection.

Vibrio alginolyticus: lecture coverage limit

The organism is included in the four-species Vibrio list and in the non-O-antigen group, but the lecture provides no separate characteristics, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, or treatment.

Do Not Confuse: emetic vs cholera-like diarrhea

B. cereus emetic form is the early 1–6-hour syndrome linked to heat-stable toxin and fried rice. Its diarrheal form is later and called cholera-like in the lecture; actual V. cholerae uses an A-B toxin and cAMP-driven secretion.

Do Not Confuse: erysipeloid vs erysipelas

Erysipeloid follows infected fish/pig exposure and is linked to Erysipelothrix. The lecture assigns erysipelas to Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus.

Cram Sheet

Short, lecture-supported anchors for the last pass. No new information is introduced here.

PDF p. 1–11

Spore biology

  • Favorable: vegetative cell.
  • Unfavorable: sporulation → dormant resistant endospore.
  • Germination: moist, nutrient-rich surface at 37°C.
  • Autoclave point: 120°C × 15 min.
  • 72-hour culture may show spores; 24-hour culture may not.

B. anthracis

  • Soil/herbivore/animal-product exposure.
  • pXO2 capsule: D-glutamic acid, antiphagocytic.
  • pXO1 toxin: PA attachment, EF cAMP/edema, LF macrophage death.
  • Skin → painless black eschar; inhalation → Woolsorter’s / mediastinal nodes.
  • Bicarbonate mucoid; BAP Medusa head; gamma-phage lysis.

B. cereus / thuringiensis

  • Spore survives heating → germination → enterotoxin.
  • Emetic: 1–6 h, heat-stable, fried rice/milk/pasta.
  • Diarrheal: 6–24 h, heat-labile, meat/sauce.
  • Eye trauma with soil → ophthalmitis.
  • B. thuringiensis = larvicide protein.

C. diphtheriae

  • Clubbed ends, metachromatic granules, Chinese letters.
  • Beta prophage tox gene.
  • B binds; A ADP-ribosylates → EF-2 stops.
  • Pseudomembrane, bull neck, airway obstruction, myocarditis, paralysis.
  • Loeffler, tellurite, Elek; antitoxin + penicillin; toxoid/Tdap.

L. monocytogenes

  • Refrigerated/unpasteurized foods; pregnancy/newborn/immunocompromised risk.
  • Short G+ rod, intracellular, tumbling at 25°C, catalase+, narrow beta hemolysis.
  • InlA/B entry → LLO/PLC escape → ActA movement → listeriapod spread.
  • Gut → blood → brain/placenta/liver/spleen.
  • Ampicillin, vancomycin, ciprofloxacin listed.

Minor Gram-positive rods

  • Erysipelothrix: fish/pig, seal/whale finger, H₂S, penicillin.
  • Lactobacilli: normal flora, lactic acid, MRS creamy colonies, probiotic.
  • Streptomyces: colorful soil colonies, antibiotics, black-granule mycetoma.
  • Nocardia: branching weak acid-fast, BCYE breadcrumb colonies.

C. jejuni

  • Undercooked chicken, milk, water.
  • Seagull-shaped, microaerophilic, oxidase+, 37–42°C.
  • Adhesin + vesicles + enterotoxin/endotoxin/cytotoxin.
  • Watery/bloody diarrhea, fever, pus, cramps.
  • Antecedent to Guillain–Barré; CAMPY agar.

H. pylori

  • Curved, motile, microaerophilic; urease/oxidase/catalase positive.
  • Urease neutralizes → flagella penetrate → BabA attaches → VacA/CagA injure.
  • Rapid urease, histology, breath, stool antigen; IgG cannot separate old from active.
  • 14 days: two antibiotics + PPI.
  • Check eradication after 4 weeks with breath or stool antigen.

V. cholerae

  • O1 / O139 epidemic serogroups; classical and El Tor.
  • CT: B binds; A raises cAMP.
  • cAMP ↑ → chloride secretion → water/electrolyte loss.
  • Not all have rice-water stool.
  • Aggressive rehydration; safe water and sanitation.

Other vibrios

  • V. parahaemolyticus: seafood, 96 h, TDH/TRH, Kanagawa; TCBS green.
  • V. cholerae: sucrose fermentation; TCBS yellow.
  • V. vulnificus: raw seafood/seawater wound; liver disease → septicemia/soft-tissue infection.
  • V. alginolyticus: listed but not further discussed.

One-line ID cues

  • Black eschar → anthrax.
  • Fried rice, 1–6 h → B. cereus emetic.
  • Chinese letters + bull neck → diphtheria.
  • Tumbling at 25°C + pregnancy risk → Listeria.
  • Weak acid-fast branching rod → Nocardia.
  • Chicken + Guillain–Barré → Campylobacter.
  • Urease + gastric ulcer → H. pylori.
  • Seawater wound + liver disease → V. vulnificus.

Lecture inconsistencies to remember

  • Anthrax treatment: detailed section vs summary table differ.
  • Campylobacter incubation is stated as 9–39 days.
  • V. cholerae infective dose is given as multiple ranges.
  • Listeria is listed as (+) endotoxin.
  • V. alginolyticus is listed without a full section.
