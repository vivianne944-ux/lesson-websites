Study-use warning: These are exam-practice prescriptions, not patient-specific medical advice. In real care, confirm the exact product concentration, allergies, hydration, kidney/liver function, pregnancy status, comorbidities, and interacting medicines.

1. Read the stem before choosing the drug

The best prescription is not just a dose calculation. It must match the patient's problem and the requested dosage form.

Amber Seen • 7/F • 20 kg

Case snapshot

One day of fever and chills, Tmax 39°C, mild headache and nasal congestion, clear nasal discharge, clear lungs, and no GI or urinary symptoms. Impression: probably viral upper respiratory tract infection.

FeverMild headacheNo clear inflammatory diseaseNo dehydration reported
Best P-drug for this exact case: ParacetamolIt treats fever and mild headache without adding unnecessary peripheral anti-inflammatory action.
Reasonable alternative: IbuprofenUse only when appropriate—for example, if paracetamol cannot be used or the examiner asks for an NSAID. Check hydration, renal/GI history, NSAID allergy, and asthma sensitivity.
Not the Amber answer: CelecoxibCelecoxib is an anti-inflammatory COX-2 inhibitor used for conditions such as OA, RA, JIA/JRA, and selected pain syndromes. Amber has uncomplicated viral fever and mild headache, so celecoxib is not the rational first prescription.

4. Dose and quantity math

Write the math before the prescription. It catches most concentration and dispense errors.

Liquid formula

Dose (mg) = weight (kg) × ordered mg/kg/dose

Volume per dose (mL) = desired dose (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL)

Total volume = mL/dose × doses/day × days

Solid formula

Units per dose = ordered dose ÷ strength per tablet/capsule

Dispense quantity = units/dose × doses/day × days

5. Exam traps to avoid

These are the tiny things that turn a clinically reasonable answer into a prescription-writing deduction.

1

PRN is not “round the clock”

Every 6 hours as needed means give only when the symptom is present. Every 6 hours round the clock means scheduled dosing for 24 hours. Do not write both unless the stem is bizarrely explicit.

2

Strength controls volume

Ibuprofen 5 mL is correct for 200 mg/5 mL—not for 100 mg/5 mL. Always rewrite the fraction before solving.

3

Do not give aspirin to Amber

Aspirin is avoided in children/adolescents with viral illness because of Reye syndrome risk.

4

Celecoxib needs an inflammatory indication

It is not a routine antipyretic for uncomplicated viral fever. Use the 50 mg BID pediatric model only for a JIA/JRA-type stem.

5

Three 120 mg chewables is too much here

360 mg ÷ 20 kg = 18 mg/kg/dose, above the lecture's 10–15 mg/kg/dose range. Two 120 mg tablets = 240 mg = 12 mg/kg.

6

Do not invent the duration or PRN status

If the stem says scheduled BID for five days, write scheduled BID. If it says PRN, include the symptom trigger and a maximum.

7

Use the generic name

Write paracetamol, ibuprofen, or celecoxib—not a brand name as the main drug name.

8

Complete the prescriber block

Signature, name followed by M.D., address, PRC license number, and PTR number. Use a complete date and include “y/o” for children.
