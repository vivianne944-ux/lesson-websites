Overview

The whole lecture in one idea

Medical microbiology follows the journey from a microorganism to a possible infectious disease: identify what kind of microbe it is, determine where it comes from and how it reaches the host, follow its interaction with host defenses, then explain the eventual outcome.

Central question

How does a microscopic agent move from simply existing in the environment to colonizing, invading, damaging, and possibly leaving or remaining in a human host? The lecture answers this by connecting microbial characteristics, epidemiology, host defenses, virulence factors, clinical disease, diagnosis, treatment, and transmission. PDF p. 1

How the topics connect

A microbe's structure and classification help explain its behavior. Epidemiology describes its source and route. The host–microbe stages show whether contact remains harmless or progresses to disease. The final sections explain signs, symptoms, disease course, prevention, and proof of causation.

Lesson 2

Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes

The most important distinction is whether the cell has a membrane-bound nucleus. That single structural difference explains several downstream differences in DNA organization and gene expression.

Prokaryotes

  • Include bacteria and archaea; unicellular
  • About 1 µm in diameter in the lecture description
  • No nuclear membrane
  • No membrane-bound organelles
  • Functions occur in the cytoplasm or cytoplasmic membrane
  • Most bacteria have a peptidoglycan cell wall; archaea are the exception stated by the lecture
  • DNA is usually a single circular chromosome
  • Transcription and translation can occur simultaneously
PDF pp. 2–3

Eukaryotes

  • Include protozoa, algae, fungi, and helminths
  • About 10–100 µm in diameter
  • Contain membrane-enclosed organelles
  • Have a cytoskeleton for support, organization, and movement
  • DNA is on several chromosomes inside a membrane-bound nucleus
  • Transcription and translation are separated: mRNA leaves the nucleus before ribosomal translation in the cytoplasm
PDF pp. 2–3

Why transcription and translation differ

Prokaryotes have no nuclear membrane, so ribosomes can begin translation while mRNA is still being synthesized. Eukaryotes separate the two processes: transcription occurs in the nucleus, then mRNA exits into the cytoplasm for translation.

The major pattern is simpler, smaller, no nucleus versus larger, compartmentalized, nucleus present.

Structures to recognize

Prokaryotic essentials

Nucleoid stores DNA; ribosomes synthesize protein; the cell membrane separates the cell from the environment; the cell wall provides structure and protection.

Attachment and movement

Capsule helps attachment and protection; pili aid attachment and DNA transfer; flagella assist movement.

Eukaryotic organelles

Nucleus, nucleolus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi body, lysosomes, vesicles/vacuoles, cytoskeleton, and ribosomes have specialized functions.

Lecture-specific note

The lecture’s eukaryote structure table lists pili among eukaryotic structures but does not further explain this listing. The reviewer preserves the lecture’s core comparison without adding an outside correction. PDF p. 2

Do Not Confuse

All cells share DNA, plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and ribosomes. The presence of ribosomes does not distinguish prokaryotes from eukaryotes; the membrane-bound nucleus does.

Lock It In

Core concept: nucleus determines the basic cell plan. Differentiator: coupled versus separated transcription/translation. Sequence: nucleus present → mRNA must exit → translation in cytoplasm. Exam cue: bacteria are prokaryotes.

Viruses

Simple ideaA virus cannot reproduce by itself. It must enter a living cell.
Complete explanationViruses are submicroscopic obligate intracellular parasites. They infect animal, plant, or bacterial cells, but most have a restricted tropism for specific cell types of one host species. A virus lacks independent replication and acquires the reproductive attribute of a living system only after infecting a cell.
High-yield takeawayVirus = nucleic acid + capsid ± envelope.

Virion

A complete virus particle with an inner RNA or DNA core, a protective capsid, and in some viruses a lipid-containing envelope.

Nucleocapsid

The complete unit of nucleic acid plus capsid. Its symmetry may be icosahedral, helical, or complex.

Envelope

A host-derived lipid bilayer containing viral proteins and glycoproteins. It surrounds the nucleocapsid in enveloped viruses.

Lesson 4

Infectious Disease Epidemiology

Epidemiology asks how and why infectious diseases emerge and spread in populations over time, then uses that information to prevent or contain disease.

Incidence

The number of new cases or newly infected persons in a population.

Prevalence

The percentage of persons with disease, including old and new cases, in a population at a particular time.

Morbidity

The state of disease and its associated effects. The morbidity rate is the incidence of a particular disease state.

Mortality

Deaths resulting from disease. The mortality rate is the incidence in which disease results in death.

Endemic, epidemic, outbreak, pandemic

TermLecture definitionKey recognition cue
EndemicDisease constantly present at some rate in a particular locationExpected ongoing presence
EpidemicLarger than normal number of diseased or infected individuals in a locationAbove normal
OutbreakLarger than normal number over a relatively short periodShort, concentrated increase
PandemicAn epidemic that spans the worldWorldwide spread

Health care-associated vs nosocomial

Health care-associated infection

Acquired because of medical procedures, such as insertion of a central line, catheter, or ventilator, or because of participation in or admission to a health care facility.

Nosocomial infection

The etiologic agent was acquired in a hospital or long-term health care facility.

Outbreak investigation tools

Strain typing

Laboratory characterization of etiologic agents to determine how closely related they are during an outbreak or epidemic.

Surveillance

Epidemiologic data collection used to characterize the circumstances around disease incidence or prevalence.

Stages 1–2: Encounter and Colonization

The lecture organizes host–microbe interaction into a sequence. Contact is not yet disease. Each stage is a checkpoint that determines whether the microbe advances.

Stage 1: physical encounter

Simple ideaThe host and the microorganism must first meet.
Complete explanationHumans encounter microbes by entering the same environment as the microbial reservoir or through indirect carriage. Reservoirs include humans, animals, food, water, air, and soil. Direct transmission means direct contact with the reservoir; indirect transmission uses a living vector or a non-living vehicle/fomite.
High-yield takeawayNo encounter, no colonization.

Stage 2: colonization

Colonization means that a microbe establishes itself and multiplies on a body surface. If the relationship is parasitic, the lecture allows the term infection. However, infection does not always cause noticeable illness.

Subclinical infection

Symptoms are absent or mild enough to go unnoticed.

Infectious disease

An infection that produces a noticeable impairment of body function.

Primary vs secondary

Primary is the initial infection; secondary is an additional infection arising because of the primary infection.

Lesson 7

Stage 3: Entry, Invasion, Dissemination, and Host Defense

After colonization, a microorganism must cross or circumvent the host’s physical barriers. Whether it succeeds depends on both host factors and microbial factors.

What disrupts surface barriers?

Trauma

Penetrating wounds, abrasions, burns, surgical wounds, and needle sticks.

Environmental and medical disruption

Noxious gases, particulate matter, smoking, implantation of medical devices, childbirth, and overuse of antibiotics.

Host disease states

Malignancy, diabetes, previous or simultaneous infections, alcoholism, and other chemical dependencies.

Important connection

Antibiotics may remove normal protective colonizing microorganisms. This links the earlier microbiome section to invasion: disturbed normal microbiota can create space for other organisms to colonize and proliferate.

Non-specific defenses in deeper tissues

Biochemical response

Removes essential nutrients, such as iron, from tissues so invading microorganisms cannot use them.

Cellular response

Phagocytes—neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells—ingest and destroy bacteria and foreign particles.

Inflammatory response

A major defense against microbial survival and proliferation, integrating cellular and biochemical components.

Lesson 8

Virulence Factors, Toxins, and Biofilms

Pathogenicity vs virulence

Pathogenicity

The organism’s ability to cause disease. High pathogenicity means it is very likely to cause disease.

Virulence

The measure or degree of pathogenicity. High virulence means more severe damage to the human host.

Do Not Confuse

Pathogenicity asks “Can it cause disease?” Virulence asks “How severe is its disease-causing capacity?”

Opportunistic pathogens

These organisms cause infection when one or more host defense mechanisms are disrupted or malfunction. Their infections are called opportunistic infections. PDF p. 9

Major virulence strategies

Attachment
Invasion
Survive inflammation
Survive immune response
Toxin production / biofilm

Attachment

Pathogens may use the same mechanisms as non-pathogenic colonizers. Pili and adhesins allow attachment to host receptors, often while competing with the normal microbiota for surface space.

Invasion

Microbes enter through disrupted barriers or through virulence factors. The lecture uses Shigella as an example of using M cells to cross epithelium, then escaping macrophages to infect other cells.

Extracellular enzymes

Hyaluronidases, nucleases, and collagenases hydrolyze host proteins and nucleic acids. The lecture explains that tissue destruction opens a path for organisms to move through surface barriers into deeper tissues. PDF p. 10

How pathogens survive inflammation

ThreatMicrobial strategy
Phagocytic ingestionProduce a capsule that inhibits phagocytosis
Phagocyte-mediated killingBlock phagosome–lysosome fusion, resist lysosomal agents, multiply inside phagocytes, or release phagocyte-damaging toxins and enzymes
ComplementHide activating surface molecules with capsule or biofilm, inhibit complement activation, or destroy complement proteins

How pathogens survive specific immunity

  • Invade and multiply before the immune response is fully activated
  • Destroy cells involved in the immune response
  • Survive inside host cells and avoid detection
  • Mask antigens with a capsule or biofilm
  • Alter antigen expression so the immune system repeatedly faces what resembles a primary encounter
  • Produce proteases that destroy or inactivate antibodies

Biofilm formation

Simple ideaMicrobes form a protected community attached to a surface.
Complete explanationA biofilm is an accumulation of microorganisms embedded in a polysaccharide matrix. Early formation involves synthesis of an extracellular polymer matrix with polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA, followed by aggregation, maturation, and eventual dispersion.
High-yield takeawayBiofilm protects against desiccation, toxic compounds, and loss of protective molecules.

The lecture states that initial biofilm development takes approximately 4–6 hours, depending on microbial growth rate. Pathogens use biofilms to adhere to implants and prosthetic devices. Listed organisms include Candida albicans, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Enterococcus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus species. PDF pp. 10–11

Lock It In

Core concept: virulence factors help a pathogen persist and damage. Differentiator: pathogenicity is ability; virulence is degree. Mechanism: attach → invade → evade → damage. Exam cue: endotoxin is gram-negative LPS released with cell destruction; exotoxin is produced by living bacteria.

Stage 4: Outcomes and the Course of Infectious Disease

Possible outcomes

Pathogen outcome

The pathogen may leave the host, be destroyed, remain latent, or destroy the host.

What determines the result?

The interplay of host health, pathogen virulence, and the host’s ability to clear the pathogen before irreversible harm.

Favorable outcome: host factors overcome microbial factors, allowing complete recovery. Less favorable outcome: microbial factors outperform host defenses, potentially ending in death. PDF p. 11

Lesson 10

Prevention and Establishing Disease Causation

Three prevention strategies

1. Prevent transmission

Avoid direct contact, use protective measures such as gloves or condoms, use masks or isolation for airborne spread, and use sterile medical techniques.

2. Control reservoirs

Sanitation, disinfection, sewage treatment, food preservation, water treatment, and control of pests and insect vectors.

3. Minimize risk near exposure

Immunization or vaccination, cleansing and antiseptics, and prophylactic antimicrobial use.

Koch’s postulates

1. Organism is found in all cases, with distribution matching lesions
2. Organism is grown in pure culture in vitro
3. Pure culture causes typical disease in a susceptible animal
4. Organism is re-isolated from experimentally produced lesions

What this flow means: Koch’s postulates create a logical chain from association, to isolation, to reproduction of disease, to re-isolation. PDF p. 12

Limitations of Koch’s postulates

Culture problem

Some organisms cannot be grown in laboratory media, such as Treponema pallidum.

Asymptomatic hosts

Some infected persons do not have symptoms, as in the lecture examples of cholera and polio.

Polymicrobial disease

Some diseases involve more than one microorganism, such as chronic periodontal disease.

Animal model problem

Suitable experimental animals may not exist, and human testing may be unethical.

Molecular Koch’s postulates

Association
virulence property is linked to pathogenic strains
Inactivation
disabling the gene decreases pathogenicity or virulence
Restoration
replacing the wild-type gene restores pathogenicity or virulence

Molecular guidelines for difficult-to-culture pathogens

The lecture explains that PCR can amplify microorganism-specific nucleic acid sequences from host tissues or fluids. These sequences are then used to identify infecting organisms when culture-based postulates are not practical. Examples listed include Whipple’s disease, bacillary angiomatosis, human monocytic ehrlichiosis, hantavirus pulmonary syndrome, and Kaposi sarcoma. PDF p. 12

Do Not Confuse

Classic Koch: whole organism, culture, animal disease, re-isolation. Molecular Koch: virulence genes and change in pathogenicity. Molecular guidelines: pathogen-specific nucleic acid sequences detected directly in host material.

Lock It In

Core concept: prevention interrupts transmission or reduces host risk. Differentiator: classic postulates test the organism; molecular postulates test virulence genes. Sequence: association → isolation/inactivation → disease change → confirmation. Exam cue: PCR is useful when culture is difficult or impossible.
