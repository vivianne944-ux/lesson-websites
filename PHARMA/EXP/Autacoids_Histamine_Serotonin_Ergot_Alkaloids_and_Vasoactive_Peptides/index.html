Whole-lesson orientation

One lecture, one organizing idea: local signals and the drugs that modify them

This lecture groups several endogenous mediators that act powerfully in the body, then shows how drugs can block, imitate, preserve, or amplify those signals. The recurring exam question is: Which mediator or receptor is being targeted, and what physiologic effect follows?

pp. 1–10

Central question

How can a drug change vascular tone, smooth-muscle activity, secretion, inflammation, nausea, migraine, or blood pressure by acting on a local signaling molecule?

Main pattern

Start with the signal, identify its receptor or synthetic pathway, then predict whether the drug is an agonist, antagonist, enzyme inhibitor, or degradation blocker.

Exam anchor

The lecture repeatedly contrasts vasoconstrictors with vasodilators, and asks when blocking or enhancing one side is clinically useful.

Autacoids and histamine foundations

Build the framework first: what an autacoid is, where histamine comes from, where it is stored, and how its four receptor subtypes differ.

pp. 1–2
What it is

Autacoid

The term comes from Greek words meaning “self” and “cure/remedy.” In the lecture, this captures two ideas:

  • Self: endogenous molecules with complex physiologic and pathophysiologic functions.
  • Cure/remedy: the same kinds of molecules can have powerful pharmacologic effects when administered as drugs.
Four groups

How the lecture classifies autacoids

  • Amines: histamine and serotonin.
  • Vasoactive peptides: peptide signals that strongly affect vessels and other tissues.
  • Eicosanoids: products derived from fatty acids.
  • Nitric oxide: a gaseous signaling molecule.

Lecture wording on page 1 labels amines and vasoactive peptides as proteins; the reviewer preserves the lecture’s framing.

Mast cells and basophils

Histamine is stored in granules, especially at sites vulnerable to tissue injury such as the nose, mouth, feet, internal body surfaces, and blood vessels. It is an important mediator of immediate allergic and inflammatory reactions.

Stomach

In enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells, histamine activates gastric acid-producing parietal cells. This explains why blocking H2 receptors decreases acid secretion.

Brain

Histamine acts as a neurotransmitter involved in neuroendocrine control, body-weight regulation, and sleep/arousal. This connects to the H3 inverse agonist pitolisant, which promotes wakefulness.

Why it matters. Histamine itself has no clinical application in this lecture. The useful drugs are mainly receptor blockers, release inhibitors, or a physiologic antagonist such as epinephrine in anaphylaxis.

Triple response

After intradermal histamine injection, the lecture describes a red spot + edema + flare response. This is a compact way to recognize histamine’s vascular and inflammatory effects.

Dose-related adverse effects

Larger histamine doses can cause flushing, urticaria, hypotension, tachycardia, headache, bronchoconstriction, and GI upset. The lecture links this pattern to scombroid fish poisoning, in which spoiled fish contains large amounts of histamine.

Blocking histamine

Histamine antagonists: choose the receptor, then predict the use and adverse effects

The key separation is between H1 blockers for allergic or motion-related conditions and H2 blockers for acid peptic disease.

p. 2

Physiologic antagonist

Epinephrine opposes histamine through adrenergic receptors and is used for anaphylaxis.

Release inhibitors

Cromolyn, nedocromil, and β2 agonists are listed for asthma.

H1 receptor antagonists

Allergic rhinitis, chronic urticaria, and motion sickness.

H2/H3/H4

H2: acid peptic disease. H3: narcolepsy. H4: no clinical drug.

H1 receptor antagonists

Mechanism. H1 blockers compete with histamine by reversibly binding H1 receptors. Because several first-generation drugs also interact with autonomic or other receptors, they gain both additional uses and additional adverse effects.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Rapidly absorbed; peak in 1–2 hours.
  • Widely distributed; first generation enters CNS more readily.
  • Metabolized in the liver.
  • Typical duration 4–6 hours; second generation lasts longer and may be taken once daily.

Clinical positioning

  • Drugs of choice for chronic urticaria.
  • Approved for allergic rhinitis but second line after intranasal glucocorticoids.
  • Not effective for asthma because asthma involves several mediators other than histamine.

Additional actions with therapeutic value

  • Antiparkinsonism: diphenhydramine
  • Motion sickness: meclizine and diphenhydramine
  • Antiserotonin: cyproheptadine
  • Local anesthesia: diphenhydramine and promethazine

Undesirable effects

  • Sedation, strongest in first generation
  • Seizures at very high doses
  • Antimuscarinic effects: blurred vision, urinary retention, dry secretions
  • Orthostatic hypotension: promethazine
Lecture-supported recall pattern: first-generation H1 blockers are the ones to associate with CNS entry, sedation, and autonomic side effects. Second-generation drugs are less sedating because distribution into the CNS is reduced.

Pharmacokinetics

Rapid absorption, first-pass hepatic metabolism, about 50% bioavailability, half-life 1–4 hours, and action lasting 6–10 hours.

Clinical uses

Oral or IV use. IV administration may be used in surgical or ICU patients to prevent gastric erosions or hemorrhage. They are less effective than PPIs for GERD but have very low toxicity.

Adverse effects

Usually rare and mild: headache, fatigue, myalgia, diarrhea, constipation; CNS effects may occur with IV use.

Serotonin foundations: where it comes from, where it is stored, and how its receptors are organized

Serotonin receptor pharmacology becomes easier when you first separate the synthetic pathway, major storage sites, and receptor families.

Clinical meaning. Urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) is used as a diagnostic test for carcinoid tumors that synthesize excessive serotonin.

GIT

More than 90% is stored in enterochromaffin-like cells of the gastrointestinal tract.

Platelets

Serotonin is present as a component of the clotting process.

Brain and pineal gland

In raphe nuclei, serotonergic neurons regulate mood, sleep, appetite, temperature, blood pressure, pain perception, and vomiting. In the pineal gland, serotonin is a precursor of melatonin.

Lecture conclusion: serotonin itself has no clinical application. Therapeutic drugs act on serotonin synthesis, storage, receptors, or excess signaling.

Serotonin drugs and serotonin syndrome

This section moves from receptor pattern to clinical use: migraine, anxiety, constipation, nausea/vomiting, carcinoid-related diarrhea, and treatment of serotonin excess.

pp. 3–4

5-HT1B/1D agonists: triptans

TriggerAcute severe migraine
→
Trigeminal actionInhibit release of vasodilating peptides such as CGRP
→
Vascular actionConstrict intracranial arteries
→
Clinical roleFirst-line therapy in the lecture

Adverse effects

Altered sensations such as tingling or warmth, dizziness, muscle weakness, neck pain, and chest pain. Chest pain is connected to possible coronary vasoconstriction.

Contraindications

Coronary artery disease or angina, severe hepatic/renal impairment, peripheral vascular disease, and Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome.

Disadvantages

Most have short action, commonly 2–3 hours, while migraine may last longer. Frovatriptan is the exception with a 27-hour half-life. The lecture also emphasizes high cost.

Do Not Confuse. Triptans are agonists, not antagonists. Their benefit comes from suppressing vasodilating peptide release and constricting intracranial arteries, but this same vasoconstrictor property explains cardiovascular caution.

Serotonin syndrome

Recognition

Excess synaptic serotonin

A serious, potentially fatal syndrome. The lecture’s three major signs are hypertension, hyperreflexia, and hyperthermia.

Other findings include tremor, clonus, hyperactive bowel sounds, diarrhea, mydriasis, agitation, and coma, often with onset within hours.

Clinical diagnosis

Recent serotonergic drug exposure + physical findings

Precipitating drugs listed include SSRIs, second-generation antidepressants, MAOIs, linezolid, tramadol, meperidine, fentanyl, ondansetron, sumatriptan, MDMA, LSD, St. John’s wort, and ginseng.

Differentials in the lecture: malignant hyperthermia after anesthetic overdose and neuroleptic malignant syndrome after antipsychotic overdose.

Why 5-HT2 blockade? The lecture states that blocking 5-HT2 may reduce skeletal-muscle contractions. Cyproheptadine is useful because it is an H1 blocker with anti-serotonin activity.

Serotonin antagonists

Synthesis inhibitors

P-chlorophenylalanine and p-chloroamphetamine are too toxic for general use. Telotristat ethyl is approved for diarrhea due to carcinoid tumor.

Storage inhibitor

Reserpine inhibits vesicular monoamine transport but also depletes norepinephrine and dopamine; it is rarely used because of toxicity.

Receptor antagonists

The major therapeutic approach is blockade of 5-HT2 or 5-HT3 receptors.

Fungal receptor-active drugs

Ergot alkaloids: clinically useful mixed receptor actions, limited by prolonged vasoconstriction

Ergot alkaloids are not autacoids. They are complex products of Claviceps purpurea that act on serotonin, alpha-adrenergic, and dopamine receptors.

pp. 4–5

Origin and historical toxicity

Claviceps purpurea infects wet grains. Ergotism or St. Anthony’s fire produced fiery pain, prolonged vasospasm with gangrene, dementia with hallucinations and convulsions, and uterine contractions leading to abortion.

Receptor profile

Ergot alkaloids can act as agonists, partial agonists, or antagonists at 5-HT1A/1B/1D, 5-HT2, α-adrenoceptors, and dopamine D2 receptors. Their important actions occur in blood vessels, uterus, and CNS.

Pharmacokinetics

Oral absorption

Variable from the GIT. The lecture states that an oral dose may be 10 times the IM dose.

Caffeine effect

Oral absorption improves with caffeine.

IM and metabolism

IM absorption is slow but reliable. The drugs are extensively metabolized.

Vasoconstrictor side

Vasoactive peptides I: RAS, endothelin, vasopressin, NPY, and neurotensin

These peptides affect vascular smooth muscle and other tissues. A major therapeutic strategy is to block vasoconstrictors in hypertension or pulmonary arterial hypertension.

pp. 5–7

Vasoconstrictors emphasized

Angiotensin, vasopressin, and endothelin. For hypertension, the goal is to decrease vasoconstriction and therefore lower blood pressure.

Vasodilator bridge

Bradykinin, substance P, CGRP, and natriuretic peptides sit on the vasodilator side and are developed in the next tab.

ACE has two important effects: it creates the vasoconstrictor ANG II and inactivates the vasodilator bradykinin. Both actions shift the balance toward vasoconstriction.

ACE2 connection

  • Found in kidneys, lungs, heart, and testes.
  • Converts ANG II to ANG 1–7.
  • Binds SARS-CoV-2 and facilitates entry into host cells.
  • The lecture states that downregulation of ACE2 and increased ANG II may partly explain poor outcomes in severe COVID among patients with hypertension.

Why chronic RAS inhibition matters

  • RAS overactivity contributes to hypertension.
  • RAS inhibition slows or prevents cardiac remodeling after myocardial infarction.
  • The lecture frames long-term RAS inhibition as reducing cardiac mortality in hypertension or prior MI.

Angiotensin II effects

Pressor action

A potent pressor agent, described as 40 times more potent than norepinephrine. It rises in vasodilatory shock and hypotension during anesthesia as compensation.

Renal effects

Renal vasoconstriction, increased sodium reabsorption, and decreased renin release.

Hormone release

Stimulates catecholamines, aldosterone, vasopressin, and ACTH.

Key connection: Kininase II is identical to ACE. Therefore, ACE inhibition not only reduces ANG II formation but also blocks bradykinin degradation.

Effects of kinins

  • Vasodilation in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, liver, and intestine; 10 times more potent than histamine.
  • Direct arteriolar smooth-muscle inhibition or mediation by PGE2 and NO.
  • Venoconstriction directly or through PGF2α.
  • Edema from increased vascular permeability.
  • Classic inflammation: heat, redness, swelling, pain, loss of function.

Hereditary angioedema

Deficiency or dysfunction of C1 esterase inhibitor disinhibits kallikrein, increasing bradykinin. Angioedema may affect airways, GIT, genitalia, or extremities.

Icatibant, a B2 receptor antagonist, is given subcutaneously for acute attacks. The lecture reminds: “B” means bradykinin, not beta.

Substance P and emesis

Substance P is the most important neurokinin-family peptide in the lecture. It is widely distributed in CNS/PNS, cardiovascular system, and GIT, and is implicated in stress, anxiety, depression, nausea, emesis, nociception, and inflammation.

It can contract venous, intestinal, and bronchial smooth muscle, stimulate salivary secretion, and promote renal natriuresis/diuresis.

NK1 blockade

Aprepitant competes with substance P at NK1 receptors in the vomiting center and is approved for chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting. Fosaprepitant is the IV prodrug alternative.

Lecture mnemonic: Substance P → Aprepitant → Puking

Natriuretic peptides and heart failure

Physiologic response

Released as compensation in heart failure, causing vasodilation, natriuresis, and diuresis.

BNP as marker

BNP is found in cardiac ventricles. A negative test makes heart failure highly unlikely; higher levels indicate more severe disease. The lecture describes BNP as both diagnostic and prognostic.

Clearance

Short half-life; metabolized by neprilysin in kidneys, liver, and lungs.

Arachidonic-acid products

Eicosanoids: one precursor, two oxygenase pathways, many organ effects

Leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and thromboxanes all arise from arachidonic acid. The exam logic is to identify the branch, then match the agonist or inhibitor to its organ effect.

Block PLA2 / COX synthesis

Glucocorticoids inhibit PLA2 and stimulate annexin synthesis; the lecture states annexins inhibit COX.

Block lipoxygenase

Zileuton inhibits 5-lipoxygenase, reducing leukotriene production.

Block receptors or COX

“-lukasts” such as montelukast and zafirlukast block leukotriene receptors. NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase.

PGI2 vs TXA2

Prostacyclin (PGI2) promotes vasodilation and decreases platelet aggregation. Thromboxane A2 promotes platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction.

Aspirin blocks platelet COX-1 and therefore TXA2 formation. The lecture states that no thromboxane antagonist has superior complete antiplatelet activity, so aspirin remains the antiplatelet choice for this mechanism.

Leukotrienes in asthma

LTC4 and LTD4 are potent bronchoconstrictors secreted in asthma. Montelukast and related leukotriene-receptor antagonists are used for chronic asthma treatment.

This is not an acute bronchodilation story: the lecture frames them as chronic treatment.

Do Not Confuse PDA directions. Alprostadil maintains a patent ductus arteriosus. Indomethacin or ibuprofen closes it by inhibiting COX.
Pain and inflammation connection. Prostaglandin E and prostacyclin are vasodilators that mediate pain and inflammation; glucocorticoids and NSAIDs act upstream by blocking eicosanoid production.

Gaseous signaling

Nitric oxide: L-arginine to cGMP to smooth-muscle relaxation

Nitric oxide readily diffuses across membranes and regulates cardiovascular, inflammatory, and neuronal functions. Its therapeutic story is about donating NO or preserving/amplifying cGMP signaling.

pp. 9–10
Do Not Confuse. Nitric oxide (NO) is not nitrous oxide (N2O, an anesthetic gas) and not nitrogen dioxide (NO2, a toxic pulmonary irritant).
SubstrateL-arginine
→
EnzymeNO synthase, requiring O2 and NADPH
→
SignalNO activates guanylyl cyclase
→
Second messenger↑ cGMP → protein kinase G
→
Endpoint↓ cytosolic Ca2+ → smooth-muscle relaxation

Antithrombotic

In platelets, increased cGMP decreases aggregation and clot formation.

Antiatherogenic / antioxidant

Reduces monocyte/leukocyte adhesion to endothelium, blocks LDL oxidation, and decreases foam-cell formation.

Oxidant damage

NO can inactivate metalloproteins and contribute to tissue damage. Overproduction in septic shock causes hypotension, shock, and death.
