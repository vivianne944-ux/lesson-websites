Family Enterobacteriaceae

One family, six major organisms, a handful of decisive patterns

The lecture organizes enteric Gram-negative rods by lactose fermentation, motility, indole/citrate behavior, antigenic structures, and the diseases produced by their virulence factors. The central task is to connect a laboratory pattern to a mechanism, then connect that mechanism to a clinical syndrome.

Central question

How do you identify each organism—and why does its structure produce its characteristic disease?

Shared family pattern

What most Enterobacteriaceae have in common

  • Gram-negative rods
  • Catalase-positive, oxidase-negative
  • Nitrate-positive and able to ferment glucose
  • O antigen as the somatic LPS antigen and endotoxin-related structure

The lecture separates opportunists—Escherichia, Klebsiella, Proteus, and Citrobacter—from the true pathogens Salmonella and Shigella. PDF p. 1–2

The six organism anchors

First-pass recognition

E. coli: pink MacConkey, metallic EMB sheen, UTI and diarrheagenic strains.

Salmonella: motile, H₂S-positive, black-centered colonies, enteric fever or food poisoning.

Shigella: non-motile, no H or K antigen, low infectious dose, dysentery.

Klebsiella: thick capsule, mucoid lactose fermenter, non-motile, pneumonia and device-associated infection.

Proteus: swarming, strong urease, alkaline urine, struvite/staghorn stones.

Citrobacter: opportunistic nosocomial pathogen; C. koseri is linked to neonatal meningitis and brain abscess.

Enterobacteriaceae foundations

Before memorizing organisms, learn the family’s common language: opportunist versus true pathogen, and the O, H, and K antigens.

Nature

Opportunistic pathogens

Escherichia, Klebsiella, Proteus, and Citrobacter may be part of normal gut flora. In the lecture’s framing, they produce disease when they are transformed or leave their usual habitat.

Normal location→Displacement or altered behavior→Opportunistic infection
Nature

True pathogens

Salmonella and Shigella are presented as organisms that directly cause disease rather than primarily behaving as normal flora opportunists.

Exposure→Pathogenic invasion or toxin action→Clinical disease

Why classification matters

A test becomes memorable when it separates close look-alikes. Lactose fermentation divides pink from colorless MacConkey colonies; motility separates Shigella and Klebsiella from motile organisms; urease and swarming point strongly to Proteus; metallic sheen points to E. coli.

Escherichia

E. coli: normal-flora member with major urinary and diarrheal disease roles

The organism’s identity is built from three linked ideas: adhesion, strain-specific toxins or A/E lesions, and a highly recognizable lactose-fermenting laboratory pattern.

PDF p. 1–3
Medical importance

Why it matters

E. coli is both a beneficial normal-gut organism and a major pathogen. The lecture identifies it as a leading cause of community- and hospital-acquired UTI, and a cause of sepsis, pneumonia, neonatal meningitis, foodborne gastroenteritis, and HUS.

Philippine distribution

Who is most affected

Reported disease is undercounted. The lecture highlights rainy-season diarrheal disease, contamination of wells or community water, tertiary-hospital UTI/bacteremia, children under 5 with dehydration, older adults with UTI-related sepsis, people with poor WASH access, chronic illness, and catheter use.

Lecture figures: 75% of UTI and >20% of bacteremia in tertiary hospitals; bacteremia mortality listed as 37.0% from untreated urinary or abdominal infection. PDF p. 2

What this flow means: adherence prevents washout. After attachment, some strains damage absorptive surfaces, while others primarily alter water/salt movement or shut down protein synthesis. PDF p. 2

Diarrheagenic patterns

Do not merge the strains

ETEC: LT and ST enterotoxins drive acute watery traveler’s diarrhea.

EPEC: A/E lesion; associated with infantile watery diarrhea.

EHEC O157:H7: Shiga toxin → hemorrhagic colitis and HUS; high fever is notably absent in the lecture’s comparison.

UPEC: fimbrial adherence in the urinary tract → cystitis, pyelonephritis, and possible urosepsis.

Host response

Mechanical, innate, then adaptive

  1. Physical flushing: diarrhea or urinary frequency attempts to remove bacteria.
  2. TLR4 sensing: O antigen/LPS triggers TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, producing inflammation, fever, and vasodilation.
  3. Neutrophils: phagocytosis produces pyuria in urinary infection.
  4. Antibodies: target O, H, and K antigens, neutralize toxins, limit flagellar movement, and opsonize the capsule.

Medical emergency: HUS

In the lecture, 5%–10% of STEC infections develop HUS, commonly 5–10 days after diarrhea, sometimes as gastrointestinal symptoms seem to improve. The classic triad is hemolytic anemia (fatigue/pallor), thrombocytopenia (petechiae/low platelets), and acute kidney failure (oliguria or anuria, edema). PDF p. 3

Understand It Better: diagnosis, strain confirmation, and automated methods

Specimens: midstream urine for UTI, stool for diarrhea, blood cultures for sepsis. UTI dipstick may show nitrites + and leukocyte esterase +.

Pathogenic-strain tests: SMAC for O157:H7, latex agglutination for O/H antigens, ELISA for Stx1/Stx2, and PCR for stx1/stx2, lt/st, or eae.

Automated identification: VITEK 2 or MicroScan reads biochemical and resistance patterns; MALDI-TOF uses a protein fingerprint.

Management and prevention

Lecture-stated approach

Intestinal infection

ORS for mild–moderate disease and IV Lactated Ringer’s when severe or oral intake is not tolerated. The lecture strictly contraindicates loperamide when a toxin-producing strain is suspected and restricts antibiotics in EHEC/STEC because of concern for HUS.

Extraintestinal infection

The lecture lists nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, or TMP-SMX for uncomplicated cystitis; ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin for complicated UTI/pyelonephritis; carbapenems or piperacillin-tazobactam for ESBL sepsis; and high-dose IV cephalosporin combinations for neonatal meningitis.

Prevention: hand hygiene, thorough cooking, cross-contamination prevention, produce washing, safe water, chlorination/boiling, antimicrobial stewardship, and hospital infection control. PDF p. 3

Salmonella

One genus, two clinical paths: localized food poisoning or systemic enteric fever

The decisive branch is whether the strain remains in the intestine as non-typhoidal Salmonella or survives inside macrophages and disseminates as typhoidal Salmonella.

PDF p. 3–5
Non-typhoidal

Localized gastroenteritis

S. enteritidis and S. typhimurium remain mainly in the intestinal lining, generating intense inflammation, fluid leakage, cramping, and watery or bloody diarrhea.

Typhoidal

Systemic enteric fever

S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi use the Vi capsule and intracellular macrophage survival to reach the liver, spleen, and bone marrow.

Antigens

O, diphasic H, and the Vi shield

O antigen

Somatic LPS component used for serotype identification and innate immune sensing.

H antigen

Peritrichous flagellar protein. Salmonella can switch between Phase 1 and Phase 2 flagellin.

Vi antigen

A K-type capsular polysaccharide, especially associated with S. Typhi, that hides the organism from immune recognition.

What this flow means: SPI-1 gets Salmonella into the cell; SPI-2 helps it survive after entry. The strain’s behavior after that determines gastroenteritis versus systemic typhoid fever. PDF p. 4

Host response

Inflammatory death versus intracellular control

TLR4 senses O/LPS and TLR5 senses flagella. Caspase-1 activation produces pyroptosis with IL-1β and IL-18 release. In gastroenteritis, neutrophil proteolysis damages the gut while attacking bacteria. In typhoid, macrophages become transport vehicles.

Th1 immunity supplies IFN-γ to reactivate macrophage killing; antibodies opsonize O and H antigens.

Philippine burden

Lecture-stated figures

The lecture lists roughly 13,000 Salmonella cases with a 43% surge linked to contaminated food/livestock lines, around 21,000 S. Typhi cases annually, and 50 deaths/year. Intestinal perforation is listed with 15.29% mortality.

Understand It Better: which specimen is best at each stage?

Gastroenteritis: stool or rectal swab during acute diarrhea.

Typhoid week 1: blood culture is called the absolute gold standard.

Weeks 2–3: stool and urine cultures become useful.

Most sensitive: bone marrow culture, even after antibiotics have begun.

Isolation may use Selenite F or Tetrathionate enrichment before SS, HE, or XLD agar. Serotyping uses O, H, and Vi antigens; the lecture notes that boiling can remove the heat-labile Vi mask before repeating O-antigen testing. The Widal test is limited by false positives and cross-reactivity.

Management and prevention

Lecture-stated approach

Uncomplicated non-typhoidal disease: fluids/electrolytes; the lecture says no antibiotics for healthy people with standard self-limited food poisoning and contraindicates anti-diarrheals. Antibiotics are listed for bacteremia or high-risk groups.

Enteric fever: ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin are listed, with azithromycin for MDR, dexamethasone as an adjunct in severe disease, and emergency surgery for perforation.

Prevention: handwashing, thorough cooking of poultry/meat/eggs, refrigeration, water purification, sanitary disposal, and typhoid vaccination for relevant risk groups. PDF p. 5

Shigella

Low infectious dose, intracellular lateral spread, and inflammatory dysentery

Shigella is defined by what it lacks—motility, H antigen, and K antigen—and by what it does unusually well: enter through M cells, rupture macrophages, and move directly from cell to cell using host actin.

PDF p. 5–7
Medical importance

Bacillary dysentery

Shigella is a major human pathogen causing shigellosis, especially serious among children under five and in crowded settings with poor sanitation or unsafe water. The lecture places it among the true pathogens.

Infectious dose: as few as 10–100 organisms, attributed in the lecture to survival in acidic gastric conditions. Symptoms usually begin within 1–3 days. PDF p. 6

What this flow means: Shigella avoids repeated exposure to extracellular defenses by moving directly between epithelial cells. Tissue destruction plus the neutrophil response produces raw, bleeding ulcers and dysentery. PDF p. 6

Host response

Why the stool contains blood, mucus, and pus

Pyroptotic cells release IL-1β and IL-18, bringing neutrophils into the mucosa. Shigella kills cells from within while neutrophils disrupt tight junctions. The injured mucosa sloughs, water absorption fails, and the stool becomes frequent, painful, and rich in blood, mucus, and pus.

Secretory IgA eventually targets O antigen and T3SS proteins to block renewed attachment and invasion.

Species pattern

Mild versus severe

S. sonnei is linked to milder watery diarrhea and may ferment lactose slowly. S. dysenteriae type 1 produces Shiga toxin and is linked to severe dysentery and HUS.

PDF p. 2, 6
Complications and emergencies

Febrile seizures/encephalopathy may occur in young children. HUS from S. dysenteriae type 1 involves RBC destruction and acute kidney failure. Toxic megacolon results when severe inflammation paralyzes and dilates the colon, risking rupture. Weeks later, reactive arthritis may present with arthritis, urethritis, and conjunctivitis in HLA-B27-positive patients. PDF p. 6

Understand It Better: diagnosis and serogrouping

Fresh stool is critical: use visibly bloody, mucous, or purulent portions. If plating is delayed beyond 1–2 hours, the lecture recommends Cary-Blair or buffered glycerol saline and cooling.

Microscopy: abundant neutrophils and RBCs. Culture: pale MacConkey, colorless SS without H₂S, red/pink XLD. PCR: ipaH. Shiga toxin: ELISA or PCR.

Serogroups: A = S. dysenteriae, B = S. flexneri, C = S. boydii, D = S. sonnei.

PDF p. 6–7
Management and prevention

Lecture-stated approach

Use oral or IV rehydration. The lecture lists azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, or IV ceftriaxone as targeted options and notes multidrug resistance. It strictly contraindicates anti-diarrheals because invasive bacteria and Shiga toxin may be trapped, increasing risk of toxic megacolon or systemic complications.

Prevention centers on rigorous handwashing, isolation/contact precautions, disinfection, secure water, and fly control. PDF p. 7

Klebsiella

The capsule explains the colony, the immune evasion, and the disease severity

Klebsiella’s defining feature is its thick K polysaccharide capsule. That single structure links the organism’s mucoid colonies, resistance to phagocytosis, hypermucoviscosity, and severe pneumonia or invasive disease.

PDF p. 7–8
Philippine hospital burden

A healthcare-associated organism

The lecture describes Klebsiella as one of the top Gram-negative isolates in Philippine tertiary hospitals, accounting for 17%–31% of healthcare-associated infections. Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae is listed as exceeding 15%, with especially high mortality in drug-resistant infections.

High-risk groups include neonates in NICUs, ICU patients, and people with CKD, diabetes, hemodialysis, or other severe comorbidities. PDF p. 7

Pathogenesis

Four mutually reinforcing virulence systems

Capsule

Blocks macrophage/neutrophil phagocytosis and keeps complement away from the cell membrane.

LPS / O antigen

Acts as endotoxin and may drive endotoxic septic shock.

Siderophores

Enterobactin and yersiniabactin acquire iron from host storage proteins.

Fimbriae

Type 1 and 3 adhesins bind tissue and create biofilm on catheters or breathing tubes.

Clinical manifestations

Lobar pneumonia and UTI

Pneumonia: sudden high fever/chills, productive cough, currant-jelly sputum, pleurisy, dyspnea, cyanosis, abscesses, and cavitation. Risk groups include chronic alcohol users, diabetics, and ventilated patients.

UTI: dysuria, urgency, frequency, cloudy alkaline urine, and pyelonephritis, especially with urinary catheters.

Emergencies

Hypervirulent and bloodstream disease

Hypervirulent K. pneumoniae may infect otherwise healthy people and produce pyogenic liver abscess with metastatic endophthalmitis or meningitis. Bloodstream spread can produce endotoxic shock and multi-organ failure.

Understand It Better: laboratory confirmation and resistance profiling

Specimens: sputum, urine, blood, or CSF. Gram stain shows short, plump Gram-negative rods. India ink may visualize the capsule.

String test: a thread longer than 5 mm supports hypermucoviscosity in the lecture.

Resistance workup: AST plus PCR for carbapenemase genes such as blaKPC, blaNDM, blaOXA-48, and blaVIM; MALDI-TOF or automated systems identify the organism.

PDF p. 8
Management and prevention

Lecture-stated approach

The lecture lists ceftriaxone, piperacillin-tazobactam, or levofloxacin for susceptible strains; carbapenems for ESBL producers; and ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, polymyxins, or tigecycline for carbapenem-resistant disease. Supportive pneumonia care includes oxygen/ventilation and mucolytics.

Prevention emphasizes strict hand hygiene, contact isolation, dedicated equipment, environmental disinfection, catheter minimization, ventilator protocols, comorbidity control, and antimicrobial stewardship. PDF p. 8

Proteus

Swarming gets it there; urease changes the urine; crystals make the infection persist

Proteus is easiest to remember as a linked chain: hyperflagellated swarming permits ascent, urease alkalinizes urine, and alkaline urine precipitates struvite and carbonate apatite into stones and crystalline biofilm.

PDF p. 8–10
Medical importance

Complicated urinary infection and stones

P. mirabilis and P. vulgaris are opportunistic pathogens strongly associated with complicated UTIs, long-term catheters, urinary structural abnormalities, surgical wounds, and mineral stone formation.

The lecture lists urosepsis mortality of 15%–30%, especially with ESBL-producing strains. PDF p. 9

Host response

Inflammation cannot remove a stone

TLR4 recognition of LPS drives IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α release; IL-8 recruits neutrophils, producing pyuria. Stones cannot be phagocytosed and continue scraping urinary tissue, causing chronic inflammation and hematuria. Antibodies may target flagella and urease during invasive disease.

Clinical manifestations

The urinary clues

Complicated UTI: dysuria, urgency/frequency, suprapubic pain, pyuria, hematuria, and a strong ammonia odor.

Stones: renal colic, persistent infection, struvite or staghorn calculi.

Pyelonephritis: fever/chills, CVA tenderness, nausea/vomiting.

Catheter and systemic emergencies

Crystalline biofilm may block urinary catheters and is associated in the lecture with autonomic dysreflexia. Proteus may also infect diabetic foot ulcers, pressure sores, and burns, then progress to bacteremia or septic shock. PDF p. 10

Understand It Better: definitive tests and the Weil-Felix connection

Urinalysis often shows alkaline pH 7.5–8.5 or higher, nitrites, and leukocyte esterase. Culture may have a strong ammonia odor.

Definitive biochemical clues are rapid urease +, TSI K/A with heavy H₂S, PAD +, and gelatin liquefaction +.

In the legacy Weil-Felix test, Proteus OX-19, OX-2, and OX-K antigens cross-react with antibodies in some rickettsial infections, producing visible agglutination.

PDF p. 10
Management and prevention

Lecture-stated approach

The lecture lists ampicillin, cephalexin, or ciprofloxacin as first-line oral choices; carbapenems or piperacillin-tazobactam for ESBL strains; and emphasizes complete stone removal by PCNL or ESWL. Acetohydroxamic acid is listed as a urease inhibitor for chronic unresectable stones.

Prevention: closed catheter drainage, catheter care, hydration, urine-acidification measures listed in the lecture, and hospital hand hygiene. PDF p. 10

Citrobacter

A variable opportunist: hospital infection in adults, neonatal CNS invasion in infants

Citrobacter can resemble E. coli or Salmonella in culture, so the lecture stresses laboratory confirmation. The most memorable species split is C. freundii with variable lactose/H₂S behavior and AmpC resistance versus C. koseri with neonatal meningitis and brain abscess.

PDF p. 10–12
Medical importance

Emerging healthcare-associated pathogen

Citrobacter is framed as a gut-associated opportunist that becomes dangerous in neonates, immunosuppressed patients, debilitated older adults, and people with prolonged hospitalization.

The lecture lists Citrobacter as under 5% of Philippine healthcare-associated infections, but gives mortality above 30% for bacteremia or neonatal meningitis because of brain-abscess risk. PDF p. 11

Structural logic

How it persists and invades

Some strains carry a Vi-like capsule; LPS contributes endotoxin; the periplasm may contain AmpC beta-lactamase; fimbriae promote adhesion; peritrichous flagella promote motility; plasmids/transposons may carry ESBL or carbapenemase genes.

Pathogenesis

Three disease routes

Gut A/E lesion

LEE-encoded T3SS rearranges actin and forms a pedestal, destroys microvilli, and reduces water/nutrient absorption → watery diarrhea.

Shiga toxin

Select C. freundii strains may carry Stx1/Stx2, halting protein production → bloody diarrhea and possible kidney failure.

BBB crossing

C. koseri travels in macrophages and uses outer-membrane proteins to cross brain capillary endothelium → meningitis and brain abscess.

Host response

Inflammation clears bacteria but damages intestine and brain

TLR4 senses O antigen and TLR5 senses H antigen, triggering IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and recruiting neutrophils. In the lecture, the same inflammatory process contributes to intestinal injury and to liquefactive brain-tissue destruction with abscess formation. Antibodies later target O, H, and Vi-like capsular antigens for opsonization.

PDF p. 11
Clinical manifestations

Hospital syndromes

UTI: cystitis, dysuria, pyelonephritis, pyuria, hematuria. Gastroenteritis: watery diarrhea or Shiga-toxin-mediated dysentery. Intra-abdominal/wound disease: cholecystitis, cholangitis, surgical-site and soft-tissue infection. Sepsis: bacteremia and endotoxic shock.

Neonatal emergency

C. koseri CNS disease

Newborns and premature infants under two months may develop meningitis and sepsis with bulging fontanelle, seizures, rapid deterioration, and brain abscesses, followed by developmental delay or death.

Understand It Better: laboratory differentiation and resistance

Specimens: urine, wound material, blood, or CSF. Urinalysis may show nitrites and leukocyte esterase.

Key bench combination: citrate + and ONPG +. C. freundii may form black-centered colonies because of H₂S; C. koseri does not.

Resistance: C. freundii may express AmpC beta-lactamase; the lecture lists AST, AmpC/ESBL detection, MALDI-TOF, VITEK/MicroScan, and molecular panels.

Management and prevention

Lecture-stated approach

The lecture warns about the AmpC beta-lactamase trap and lists cefepime or carbapenems for severe infection. Neonatal meningitis/brain abscess management includes high-dose IV meropenem or cefepime plus surgical drainage. For cystitis, it lists ciprofloxacin or TMP-SMX; gastroenteritis care emphasizes ORS and avoidance of loperamide when Shiga toxin is a concern.

Prevention emphasizes hand hygiene, contact isolation, NICU surveillance, removing device/biofilm targets, and safe water/kitchen practices. PDF p. 11–12

Do Not Confuse: Salmonella vs Shigella

Both: non-lactose fermenters with K/A TSI. Salmonella: motile, gas/H₂S, black centers. Shigella: non-motile, no gas, no H₂S, no H antigen, very low infectious dose.

Do Not Confuse: E. coli vs Klebsiella

Both: lactose fermenters. E. coli: motile, indole +, metallic EMB sheen, IMViC ++−−. Klebsiella: non-motile, mucoid capsule, urease +, no sheen, IMViC −−++.

Mechanism mini-flows

Sequences worth reciting

EHEC: Shiga toxin → protein synthesis stops → colonic injury → bloody diarrhea → HUS.
Salmonella: M cell → SPI-1 entry → SCV → SPI-2 survival → intestinal or systemic branch.
Shigella: M cell → macrophage pyroptosis → basolateral invasion → IcsA actin tail → dysentery.
Klebsiella: capsule + siderophores + fimbriae → immune evasion/biofilm → necrosis or invasive infection.
Proteus: urease → NH₃ → OH⁻ → alkaline urine → struvite/apatite → stones + biofilm.
Citrobacter koseri: intestinal entry → macrophage transport → BBB crossing → meningitis/brain abscess.
Emergencies

HUS: anemia + thrombocytopenia + AKI. Typhoid: intestinal perforation/hemorrhage. Shigella: toxic megacolon. Klebsiella: septic shock or metastatic hypervirulent disease. Proteus: obstructed catheter, pyelonephritis, urosepsis. Citrobacter: neonatal CNS abscess and sepsis.

Lecture-stated “do not” rules

Do not use anti-diarrheals when invasive or toxin-producing E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, or Shiga-toxin Citrobacter disease is suspected in the lecture. EHEC/STEC antibiotics are restricted because the lecture links antibiotic stress to increased HUS risk.
