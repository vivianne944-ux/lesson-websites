Identity and morphology

Begin with the simplest observable clues: whether the organism is a prokaryote, its overall shape, and the arrangement created after cell division.

PDF p. 1, 2, 6
Simple idea

Prokaryotes package DNA without a true nucleus

Prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. Their DNA is concentrated in a nucleoid. The lecture divides prokaryotic organisms into eubacteria and archaebacteria.

High-yield takeaway

Classic medical bacteriology centers on eubacteria

Eubacteria are the classic bacteria: unicellular, asexually reproducing organisms, usually with a peptidoglycan cell wall, except Mycoplasma.

Morphology: shape first, arrangement second PDF p. 1, 2

Morphology describes what the bacterial cell looks like. Arrangement describes how cells remain grouped after division. The two clues must be read together: for example, “cocci in clusters” is more informative than “cocci” alone.

Cocci: spherical cells

  • Diplococci - pairs; example: Neisseria gonorrhoeae
  • Streptococci - chains; example: Streptococcus pyogenes
  • Staphylococci - clusters; example: Staphylococcus aureus
  • Tetrad - packets of four; example: Micrococcus luteus
  • Octad - packets of eight; example: Sarcina ventriculi

Bacilli: rod-shaped cells

  • Bacillus - single rod
  • Diplobacilli - pairs; example: Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis
  • Streptobacilli - chains; example: Streptobacillus moniliformis
  • Coccobacilli - short rods resembling elongated cocci; example: Haemophilus influenzae

Spirochetes

Spiral-shaped bacteria that move by spinning around the long axis. Examples: Treponema pallidum and Borellia as written in the lecture.

Vibrio

Curved or comma-shaped bacilli. Example: Vibrio cholerae. The lecture also notes paired “gulfing” morphology in Campylobacter.

Mycoplasma

Cell wall-deficient and therefore pleomorphic, meaning it can exist in a variety of shapes. Example: Mycoplasma pneumoniae.

Key connectionIf daughter cells remain attached after cell division, the plane and number of divisions produce characteristic groupings such as chains, pairs, cubical bundles, or grape-like clusters. PDF p. 6

Cell envelope and cell walls

The envelope protects bacteria from osmotic stress, harsh chemicals, and antibiotics. Its architecture explains staining behavior and several differences in susceptibility.

PDF p. 2, 4
Cell membrane

Selective boundary

A flexible, thin layer that encloses the cell interior and controls transport through membrane proteins.

Cell wall

Rigid support

Provides rigidity, strength, protection, and defense against osmotic bursting. Its structure differs in Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.

Capsule / glycocalyx

External protective material

Gelatinous material secreted outside the wall. It may support survival, attachment, gliding, subtype identification, and resistance to phagocytosis.

Peptidoglycan: the rigid backbone PDF p. 3

Simple idea

A sugar backbone held together by peptide links

Murein is peptidoglycan. It consists of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) chains. Tetrapeptide side chains attach to NAM, and cross-links reinforce the wall.

Why it matters

Two vulnerabilities are emphasized

Lysozyme hydrolyzes the peptidoglycan backbone. Penicillin prevents cross-linking of the glycan chains. Both interfere with the wall's mechanical integrity.

Understand the Gram-negative outer layers

Outer membrane: excludes hydrophobic molecules, helps protect enteric bacteria from substances such as bile salts, and slows penetration of large antibiotic molecules. Porins provide channels for selected hydrophilic molecules.

Lipopolysaccharide: consists of Lipid A with attached long-chain fatty acids and repeating sugar units called O antigens. It is also called endotoxin and is released when the cell lyses.

Lipoprotein and periplasm: lipoprotein secures the outer membrane to peptidoglycan. The periplasm contains peptidoglycan, nutrient-binding proteins, hydrolytic enzymes, detoxifying enzymes such as beta-lactamase, and membrane-derived oligosaccharides involved in osmoregulation.

The acid-fast cell wall PDF p. 4

What makes it different

Mycobacterium tuberculosis and relatives contain large amounts of wax called mycolic acid. Their wall includes peptidoglycan plus an external asymmetric lipid bilayer, creating a highly hydrophobic structure resistant to harsh chemicals and strong acids.

How the staining clue appears

When carbol fuchsin is driven into the cells by heating or detergent, dilute hydrochloric acid cannot remove it. This is the basis of acid-fast retention described in the lecture.

Do not confuseAcid-fast red/pink rods are not automatically Gram-negative rods. The lecture's microscopy section explicitly identifies the stain as Ziehl-Neelsen rather than Gram staining. PDF p. 15

DifferentiatorThick PG = Gram-positive; thin PG + outer membrane = Gram-negative.

Internal structures and appendages

Because bacteria lack classic membrane-bound organelles, several essential functions are reassigned to the plasma membrane, ribosomes, surface appendages, and specialized survival structures.

PDF p. 4, 5

Plasma or cytoplasmic membrane PDF p. 4

Simple idea

The membrane is more than a boundary

It performs transport, energy production, secretion, wall assembly, lipid synthesis, and chemotactic sensing.

Functional analogy

Respiratory chain lives here

Because bacteria lack mitochondria, respiratory-chain components are located in the cytoplasmic membrane, making it a functional analog of the mitochondrion.

Membrane functionLecture-supported explanation
TransportMoves nutrients into the cell and wastes out; can work against a concentration gradient to increase internal nutrient concentration.
Energy productionContains respiratory-chain components.
Hydrolytic enzyme secretionEnzymes break proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids into smaller subunits. Gram-positive bacteria secrete them outside; Gram-negative bacteria release them into the periplasm.
Cell-wall constructionContains carrier lipids and enzymes involved in wall biosynthesis.
Phospholipid synthesisIncludes relevant synthetic enzymes.
ChemotaxisAttractants and repellents bind specific membrane receptors.

Nucleoid and cytoplasmic structures PDF p. 4

Nucleoid

Prokaryotic DNA is not enclosed in a true nucleus. The nucleoid contains circular DNA along with RNA and proteins.

Ribosomes

The most numerous intracellular structures in many bacteria. They are the site of protein synthesis. All prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes composed of 50S and 30S subunits.

Inclusion bodies and glycogen

Inclusion bodies store reserve materials. Glycogen is formed when carbon is excessive and later serves as a carbon source for protein and nucleic-acid synthesis.

Gas vesicles

Found almost exclusively in aquatic microorganisms and provide buoyancy.

Flagella: locomotion and antigenicity PDF p. 4

Flagella are thread-like appendages made from many molecules of flagellin. Flagellin is highly antigenic and constitutes the H antigen.

Pili and fimbriae: attachment, conjugation, and twitching PDF p. 5

STRUCTUREPili are rigid surface appendages

Many Gram-negative bacteria possess them; they are made of pillin subunits.

TIPAdhesins mediate attachment

Ordinary pili help pathogenic bacteria adhere to host cells.

TRANSFERSex pili join cells

They attach donor and recipient cells during conjugation, allowing plasmid transfer.

MOTIONRetraction produces twitching

The cell moves toward the tip adhered to a surface.

What this flow means: pili can influence both virulence and genetic exchange, not merely locomotion.

Clinical meaningThe lecture states that piliated strains are pathogenic and that pili, like capsules, can inhibit phagocytic activity of leukocytes.

Plasmids: transferable extra-chromosomal DNA PDF p. 5

A plasmid is a small, circular, double-stranded DNA molecule separate from the chromosome. It may contain ten to several hundred genes, occur as one or multiple copies, and move through a sex pilus. The lecture emphasizes that antibiotic resistance traits can be carried on plasmids.

Endospores: survival during nutrient depletion PDF p. 5

Sporulation

Near depletion of a nutrient triggers genera such as Bacillus and Clostridium to form one internal spore. The mother cell later undergoes autolysis and releases it.

Germination

The dormant spore resists desiccation, heat, and chemicals. When it reaches favorable nutritional conditions, it germinates into one vegetative cell.

Exam trapAn endospore is a dormant survival form, not a reproductive strategy that multiplies the number of cells: the lecture describes one cell forming one internal spore, then one vegetative cell after germination.

Core conceptThe membrane carries many organelle-like functions.
DifferentiatorFlagella move; pili attach, transfer, and twitch.
SequenceNutrient depletion → sporulation → release → germination.
Exam cuePlasmid + sex pilus = transferable resistance trait.

Staining, division, and cell grouping

Stains reveal structures and wall behavior; binary fission explains population growth and the arrangements seen under the microscope.

PDF p. 5, 6

How stains interact with bacterial cells PDF p. 5

Basic stains

Positively charged dyes combine with negatively charged phosphate groups in bacterial nucleic acid. Example: methylene blue.

Acidic dyes

Negatively charged dyes do not stain bacterial cells. They stain the background in a contrasting color. Example: eosin.

Core ideaThe Gram stain is a differential stain: it separates groups because the wall determines whether the crystal violet-iodine complex remains after alcohol exposure.

Gram staining: the four-step sequence PDF p. 5

1Crystal violet

All cells appear purple.

2Gram's iodine

Forms a complex with crystal violet; lecture table states all bacteria appear blue at this point.

3Alcohol

Gram-positive cells retain the complex; Gram-negative cells are completely decolorized.

4Safranin

Gram-positive remain purple; Gram-negative become pink.

What this flow means: the decisive step is the alcohol decolorization, because it exposes the structural difference between the two walls.

Taxonomy and identification

Laboratory identification moves from a rapid broad clue to progressively more specific evidence: stain, culture, biochemical properties, antigen recognition, or genetic analysis.

PDF p. 6, 7
Identification

Isolation and distinction of a specific organism from mixed flora.

Classification

Where does it belong?

Placement into related taxonomic groups using biochemical, physiologic, genetic, and morphologic properties.

Nomenclature

What is it called?

Formal naming by established scientific and medical groups.

Identification: from fast clue to exact distinction PDF p. 6, 7

1Gram-stained microscopy

Rapidly separates bacteria by wall reaction and morphology.

2Growth on media

Colony appearance and media response provide further clues.

3Biochemical testing

Presence or absence of a specific enzyme separates look-alike organisms.

Nomenclature: the binomial system PDF p. 7

The lecture lists eight ranks: Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species, and Subtype. In binomial nomenclature, the first letter of the genus is capitalized, and the genus and specific epithet are italicized or underlined: Escherichia coli.

  • Genus + specific epithet: Escherichia coli
  • Subspecific epithet / serovar: may follow the species, as shown in H. influenzae ssp. Aegyptus.
  • Some names reflect disease associations: Bacillus anthracis, Vibrio cholerae, Mycobacterium leprae, Clostridium tetani.

Microbial growth and biofilms

Growth can be measured, plotted, and interpreted. The same cells can also shift from free-living planktonic forms into protected surface communities.

PDF p. 7, 8, 15, 16

How microbial concentration is measured PDF p. 7

Viable cell count

Counts actively growing or dividing cells. In the plate or spread-plate method, visible colonies are counted on nutrient media.

Turbidity

The cloudiness of a fluid caused by many microbial particles that are individually invisible to the naked eye.

Biomass

Determined from the dry weight of a washed microbial culture.

Generation time and population doubling PDF p. 8, 15, 16

Generation time or doubling time is the average time required for a bacterial population to double. A shorter time means a higher growth rate, although the rate depends on the species and growth conditions.

E. coli

The main growth section states 20 minutes. The microscopy recap later states 15 minutes and links this rapid growth with acute infection.

Mycobacteria / M. tuberculosis

The main section states 24 hours; the recap says one day and links this slow generation time with slowly appearing signs and symptoms.

Lecture-specific inconsistencyThe lecture gives two E. coli generation times: 20 minutes on page 8 and 15 minutes on page 15. Both are preserved here rather than silently reconciled.

Cultivation and growth requirements

To multiply, bacteria must obtain carbon, nitrogen, minerals, growth factors, and usable energy while remaining within suitable pH, temperature, oxygen, and osmotic conditions.

Nutritional classes PDF p. 10

Autotrophs

Produce their own food using light, water, carbon dioxide, and other chemicals. They do not require organic nutrients for growth.

Chemolithotrophs

Use inorganic substrates such as hydrogen or thiosulfate as reductants and use carbon dioxide as the carbon source.

Heterotrophs

Use organic compounds other than carbon dioxide as the carbon source. The lecture states that most bacteria are heterotrophs.

Reactive oxygen species and osmotic conditions PDF p. 11

ROS protection

Aerobic metabolism produces hydrogen peroxide and superoxide. With iron, these may form hydroxyl radicals that damage macromolecules. Many aerobes and aerotolerant anaerobes use superoxide dismutase and catalase for protection.

Osmotic specialists

Halophiles grow in high salt. Osmophiles grow in high sugar. Most bacteria can regulate internal osmolality and ion concentration.

Assemble building blocks into proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, polysaccharides, and LPS.

4Assembly

Build complete structures such as membranes, wall components, pili, flagella, and nucleoid.

Two mechanisms of ATP production

Substrate-level phosphorylation

A high-energy phosphate formed during central metabolism is transferred directly to ADP, forming ATP.

Oxidative phosphorylation

Electrons pass through the electron-transport system to a terminal acceptor; released energy generates ATP from ADP.

Precursor metabolites to complete structures PDF p. 12

Imported nutrients become raw materials for precursor metabolites. Biosynthesis generates amino acids, fatty acids, sugars, and nucleotides. Anabolic reactions then polymerize these into proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, lipopolysaccharides, and polysaccharides. The final cellular structures are products of all genetic and metabolic processes.

Choose the medium according to the laboratory goal PDF p. 12

GoalHow the culture plan changes
Amplify a defined bacterial cloneUse conditions supporting the organism's specific requirements to increase a desired product such as nucleic acid or protein. Fastidious organisms need conditions resembling their natural environment.

Selective media

Contains additives that inhibit organisms other than the target. Example: Thayer-Martin agar contains antibiotics that inhibit Gram-positive bacteria.

Differential media

Contains substances that selected bacteria alter recognizably. Example: MacConkey agar turns pink with lactose fermentation.

Pure culture: only one type of organism PDF p. 13

A pure culture contains only one organism type and is free of all other organism types. Isolation methods separate bacteria sufficiently for individual cells to generate distinct colonies.

Cram Sheet

Rapid retrieval of the complete lecture. No new information is introduced here.

PDF p. 1, 16

Shapes and arrangements

  • Cocci: pairs = diplococci; chains = streptococci; clusters = staphylococci; 4 = tetrad; 8 = octad.
  • Bacilli: single, diplobacilli, streptobacilli; short rods = coccobacilli.
  • Spirochete: spiral, spins along long axis.
  • Vibrio: curved/comma rod.
  • Mycoplasma: wall-deficient, pleomorphic.

Cell envelope

  • Membrane: transport, respiration, secretion, synthesis, chemotaxis.
  • Wall: rigidity, form, division, osmotic protection.
  • Capsule: defined, anti-phagocytic, excludes particles.
  • Slime: loose, no particle exclusion, gliding.
  • Peptidoglycan: alternating NAG-NAM + peptide cross-links.

Walls and staining

Gram+ = thick PG + teichoic acids → purple
Gram- = thin PG + outer membrane/LPS/porins/periplasm → pink
Acid-fast = mycolic acid → retains carbol fuchsin
Mycoplasma = no wall → pleomorphic

Gram stain order

Crystal violet → iodine → alcohol → safranin
After alcohol: Gram+ retains; Gram- decolorizes
Final: Gram+ purple; Gram- pink

Internal structures

  • Nucleoid: circular DNA + RNA/proteins.
  • Ribosome: 70S = 50S + 30S.
  • Inclusions: reserves; glycogen stores carbon.
  • Gas vesicles: buoyancy.
  • Plasmid: circular dsDNA; transferable; resistance genes.

Appendages and spores

  • Flagella: locomotion; flagellin = H antigen.
  • Pili: adherence, conjugation, twitching.
  • Endospore: one dormant resistant form per cell.
  • Sporulation: nutrient depletion → spore → release.
  • Germination: favorable nutrients → vegetative cell.

Binary fission and growth

Chromosome replication → enlargement → septum + separation → two daughters

Lag → Log → Stationary → Decline

Growth phases

  • Lag: adaptation.
  • Log: rapid uniform division.
  • Stationary: division = death.
  • Decline: rapid death; resistant survivors may persist.
  • Dead: cannot form a colony.

Biofilm sequence

Planktonic cell → reversible adhesion → microcolony → glycocalyx-protected mature biofilm → dispersion

Periphery: more oxygen/nutrients. Interior: more protection from immunity/antibiotics.

Identification pathway

Gram microscopy → growth on media → biochemical test → immunologic/genetic methods
  • Nonselective: many grow.
  • Selective: competitors inhibited.
  • Differential: visible biochemical difference.

Control essentials

  • Sterilization: all life + spores.
  • Disinfection: pathogens except spores.
  • Antiseptic: living tissue.
  • Bacteriostatic: stops growth; resumes after removal.
  • Bactericidal: kills.
  • Autoclave: 121°C, 15 min, 15 psi.

Physical / chemical values

  • 100°C: microbes killed, not spores.
  • UV: 260 nm; blocks DNA replication; may not kill spores.
  • Ionizing radiation: ≤1 nm; bactericidal + sporicidal.
  • Alcohol: 60-90%; not sporicidal.
  • Peroxigens: sporicidal at 10-30% + longer contact.

Environmental growth ranges

  • Neutralophile: pH 6-8; acidophile ~3; alkalophile up to 10.5.
  • Psychrophile: -5 to -15°C.
  • Psychrotroph: 20-30°C, also lower.
  • Mesophile: 30-37°C.
  • Thermophile: 50-60°C.

Oxygen patterns

  • Obligate aerobe: needs O₂.
  • Facultative anaerobe: with or without O₂.
  • Microaerophile: 2-10% O₂.
  • Obligate anaerobe: only without O₂.
  • Aerotolerant: prefers no O₂ but survives it.
  • Capnophile: increased CO₂.

Energy pathways

  • Aerobic respiration: O₂ terminal acceptor.
  • Anaerobic respiration: nitrate/sulfate or another inorganic acceptor.
  • Fermentation: pyruvate/derivative acceptor; little energy; regenerates NAD⁺.
  • ATP: substrate-level or oxidative phosphorylation.

Metabolism chain

Nutrient acquisition → precursor metabolites + energy → building blocks → macromolecules → cellular structures
  • Simple diffusion = no energy.
  • Active transport = carrier-mediated.
  • Group translocation = nutrient modified during entry.

Pure-culture methods

  • Streak plate: progressively fewer cells → isolated colony.
  • Pour plate: diluted suspension mixed with agar.
  • Spread plate: surface spread; population counting.
  • Dilution: serial dilution then plating.

Staphylococcal differentiation

  • S. aureus: beta hemolysis, coagulase positive.
  • S. saprophyticus: novobiocin resistant, ≤16 mm.
  • S. epidermidis: novobiocin sensitive, >16 mm.

Streptococcal differentiation

  • S. pyogenes: beta hemolysis, PYR+, bacitracin susceptible.
  • S. pneumoniae: alpha hemolysis, optochin sensitive >14 mm, bile soluble, PYR-.
  • E. faecalis: gamma/nonhemolytic in lecture table.

Microscopy read order

Stain → color → morphology → arrangement → intracellular/extracellular location

Trap: acid-fast red rods are not simply Gram-negative rods.

  • Archaebacterial wall: main text says no classic peptidoglycan wall; table says no wall.
  • Bisphenols: text says broad-spectrum microbicidal; activity table marks bactericidal negative.
