Prokaryotes & Eukaryotes

Cellular organization, major structures, shared features, and the distinctions most likely to be tested.

Pages 2–3
Prokaryotes

Bacteria and archaea

  • Usually unicellular; many human infections are caused by members of this group.
  • About 1 µm in diameter in the lecture summary.
  • No nuclear membrane and no membrane-bound organelles.
  • Functions occur in the cytoplasm or cytoplasmic membrane.
  • Cell wall contains peptidoglycan, except in archaea.
  • DNA is typically a single circular chromosome.
  • Transcription and translation may occur simultaneously.
Eukaryotes

Protozoa, algae, fungi, helminths

  • Usually larger, around 10–100 µm.
  • Contain membrane-enclosed organelles with specific functions.
  • Have a cytoskeleton supporting structure, organization, and movement.
  • DNA is on several chromosomes inside a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • Transcription and translation are separate: mRNA leaves the nucleus for cytoplasmic translation.
Shared by All Cells

Whether prokaryotic or eukaryotic, cells share DNA, a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and ribosomes.

Key prokaryotic structures

  • Nucleoid: central DNA-containing region.
  • Ribosome: protein synthesis.
  • Cell wall: structure and protection; most bacterial walls contain peptidoglycan.
  • Cell membrane: separates the cell from the environment.
  • Capsule: carbohydrate layers that aid attachment and protection.
  • Pili: rod-shaped structures involved in attachment and DNA transfer.
  • Flagella: thin tail-like structures assisting movement.

Key eukaryotic structures

  • Nucleus/nucleolus: genetic information and ribosomal RNA production.
  • Plasma membrane: phospholipid bilayer around cell and organelles.
  • Cytoskeleton: shape and organelle positioning.
  • Mitochondria: ATP production.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum: protein maturation and transport.
  • Vesicles/vacuoles: transport and storage.
  • Lysosomes: controlled enzymatic degradation.
  • Golgi body: modification, internal delivery, and secretion.
Exam Trap

Nucleoid is not a membrane-bound nucleus. It is the region where prokaryotic DNA is bundled in the cytoplasm.

Viruses

Submicroscopic obligate intracellular parasites

  • Can infect animal, plant, or bacterial cells, but most are restricted to specific cells of one host species: tropism.
  • Cannot self-replicate and require a living host.
  • Only upon infection does a virus acquire the living-system attribute of reproduction.
  • They are the smallest infectious agents in the lecture, ranging from parvovirus at 18–26 nm to vaccinia virus at about 400 nm.

Virion and nucleocapsid

  • Virion: virus particle containing an inner RNA or DNA core, a protective capsid, and in some larger viruses a lipid-containing envelope.
  • Capsomeres: individual protein molecules forming the capsid.
  • Nucleocapsid: the complete nucleic acid + capsid unit.
  • Symmetry may be icosahedral, helical, or complex.
  • An envelope, when present, is a host-derived lipid bilayer with inserted viral proteins and glycoproteins.

Epidemiology & Transmission

Population-level occurrence, outbreak investigation, microbial sources, routes of transmission, and infectious dose.

Pages 4–5
Core Definition

Infectious disease epidemiology studies how and why infectious diseases emerge and spread among populations and what strategies can prevent or contain spread at the population level.

Incidence versus prevalence

  • Incidence: number of new cases or infected persons in a population.
  • Prevalence: percentage of persons with disease, including old and new cases, at a particular time.

Morbidity versus mortality

  • Morbidity: state of disease and associated effects on the host; morbidity rate is the incidence of a disease state.
  • Mortality: deaths resulting from disease; mortality rate is the incidence in which disease results in death.
TermLecture definition
EndemicDisease constantly present at some rate in a particular location.
EpidemicLarger than normal number of diseased or infected individuals in a particular location.
OutbreakLarger than normal number occurring over a relatively short period.
PandemicAn epidemic that spans the world.

Health care-associated vs nosocomial

Health care-associated infection is acquired because of medical procedures or participation/admission in a health care facility. Nosocomial infection specifically refers to acquisition of the etiologic agent in a hospital or long-term health care facility.

Outbreak tools

  • Strain typing: laboratory characterization of etiologic agents to establish relatedness during an outbreak or epidemic.
  • Surveillance: epidemiologic data collection describing circumstances around disease incidence or prevalence.

Microbial sources and chain terms

  • Etiologic agent: microorganism responsible for infection or infectious disease.
  • Reservoir: origin/location from which the agent disseminates, such as water, food, insects, animals, or humans.
  • Common source: one source or reservoir producing an outbreak or epidemic.
  • Vector: living entity that transmits an etiologic agent.
  • Carrier: asymptomatic person who harbors the agent.
  • Vehicle: contaminated non-living entity serving as transmission mode, such as water, food, soil, devices, or fomites.
  • Microbiome: an individual’s microbiologic environment in or on the host.
TransmissionDefinitionLecture example
DirectDirect contact between the microbial reservoir and hostGonorrhea, syphilis, or AIDS through sexual contact

Infectious dose

Quantity of pathogen, measured as number of organisms, required to establish infection in a susceptible host.

Minimal infectious dose

The smallest quantity of infectious material that can cause infection.

Bacterial load

The measurable quantity of bacteria in an object or organism.

Do Not Confuse

Reservoir is where the agent originates or persists; a vector is living; a vehicle is non-living; a carrier is an asymptomatic person harboring the agent.

Normal microbiota

The population of microorganisms routinely growing on the body of healthy individuals, especially the skin and mucous membranes.

Major contributions

  • First line of defense against microbial pathogens
  • Assistance in digestion
  • Role in toxin degradation
  • Contribution to immune-system maturation

Microbiome

The lecture defines the microbiome as the collective genomes of microbial symbionts and also uses the term for the host’s microbiologic environment.

How normal microbiota excludes pathogens

  • Covers attachment sites
  • Consumes available nutrients
  • Produces toxic compounds against other bacteria
  • Inhibits pathogen colonization
  • Stimulates adaptive immunity

Dynamic nature of the normal microbiota

  • Acquired rapidly during and shortly after birth and changes throughout life.
  • The lecture states that a healthy fetus is sterile until protective membranes rupture just before birth.
  • Passage through the birth canal exposes the newborn to microbes that settle on skin and in the digestive tract.
  • Food, other people, and the environment add further organisms.
  • Composition reflects a balance of forces influenced by age, nutrition, environment, hormonal changes, and host activities such as food consumption.
Resident microbiotaTransient microbiota
Relatively fixed types regularly found at a site for a given age; if disturbed, it promptly reestablishes.Non-pathogenic or potentially pathogenic organisms present for hours, days, or weeks; derived from the environment and not permanently established.
Important Qualification

Transient microbiota is generally of little significance while resident flora remains intact. If the resident microbiota is disrupted, transient organisms may colonize, proliferate, and produce disease.

Why the Sequence Matters

Understanding the steps helps diagnostic microbiology establish methods to isolate organisms from patient specimens and supports development of effective treatment strategies.

Stage 1

Physical encounter

Humans encounter microorganisms by entering or being exposed to the same environment as the microbial agent, or through indirect delivery to the host.

  • Reservoirs: humans, animals, food, water, air, soil.
  • Direct transmission: direct contact with the reservoir.
  • Indirect transmission: an intervening agent is involved.
  • Vectors: living entities.
  • Vehicles/fomites: non-living entities.
Stage 2

Colonization of host surfaces

Colonization means a microbe establishes itself and multiplies on a body surface. The outcome after contact depends on each subsequent interaction step.

  • If the relationship is parasitic, infection may be used.
  • Infection may be subclinical: symptoms are absent or too mild to notice.
  • Infectious disease is infection producing noticeable impairment of body function.
  • Primary infection: initial infection.
  • Secondary infection: additional infection resulting from the primary infection.
Stage 3

Entry, invasion, and dissemination

After colonization, the microorganism must penetrate or circumvent host physical barriers. Success depends on both host factors and microbial factors. Once surface barriers are breached, deeper host defenses are activated.

Host factors that facilitate entry

  • Disruption of skin or mucosa
  • Altered environmental conditions or surface-cell function
  • Altered normal microbiota
  • Trauma: wounds, abrasions, burns, surgery, needle sticks
  • Inhalation of toxic gases, particulates, or smoking
  • Implanted medical devices
  • Malignancy, diabetes, other infections, alcoholism/chemical dependencies
  • Childbirth
  • Overuse of antibiotics

Microbial factors that facilitate progression

  • Attachment structures and adhesins
  • Invasive behavior and tissue-damaging enzymes
  • Avoidance of phagocytes and complement
  • Immune evasion
  • Toxin production
  • Biofilm formation
Stage 4

Outcome

After completing the prior stages, the pathogen may:

Leave the host
Be destroyed by the host
Remain latent
Destroy the host

The outcome depends on host health, pathogen virulence, and whether the host clears the pathogen before irreversible harm or death.

Outcome Rule

Host factors overcome microbial factors → favorable recovery. Microbial factors outperform host defenses → less favorable outcome, including death.

Host Defenses

Surface barriers, normal microbiota, phagocytosis, inflammation, and the specific immune system.

Pages 6–9

First defenses: skin

  • Outer dermal layers: physical barrier, sloughing of attached bacteria, dry/acidic/cool conditions limiting growth.
  • Hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands: acids, alcohols, and toxic lipids limiting bacterial growth.
  • Eyes/conjunctival epithelium: tears flush microbes, lysozyme destroys bacterial cell wall, blinking mechanically removes organisms.
  • Skin-associated lymphoid tissue: specific and non-specific protection against organisms penetrating outer layers.

First defenses: mucous membranes

Mucous membranes line the oral cavity, respiratory tract, gastrointestinal tract, and genitourinary tract.

  • Mucus traps bacteria before they reach cell surfaces.
  • Lubrication prevents damage that would promote invasion.
  • Mucus contains antibodies and non-specific antibacterial substances.
  • Site-specific mucosal features add further protection.

Barrier Plus Competition

Protection is not only structural. Normal microbiota also blocks pathogens through attachment-site occupancy, nutrient consumption, toxic compound production, colonization inhibition, and adaptive immune stimulation.

Non-specific responses in deeper tissues

  1. Biochemical response: removes essential nutrients such as iron from tissues so invading organisms cannot use them.
  2. Cellular response: phagocytes ingest and destroy bacteria and foreign particles. Types include neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells.
  3. Inflammatory response: a major mechanism against survival and proliferation in tissues and organs, involving cellular and biochemical components.

Phagocytosis sequence

  1. Endocytosis of bacteria
  2. Formation of a phagosome
  3. Fusion with a lysosome
  4. Formation of a phagolysosome, where organisms are neutralized and destroyed

Pathogenesis & Virulence

How pathogens attach, invade, survive inflammation and immune responses, and damage host tissues.

Pages 9–10

Infection and disease

  • Infection: growth and multiplication of microorganisms resulting in host damage.
  • Severity depends on microbial disease-causing ability, infection site, and the patient’s general health.
  • Disease: notable physiologic changes associated with damage or loss of function in one or more organ systems.

Pathogens and virulence factors

  • Pathogen: microorganism causing infection or disease.
  • Virulence factor: a characteristic enabling a pathogen to cause disease, often by resisting host attack or damaging host cells.
  • Opportunistic pathogen: causes infection when host defense mechanisms are disrupted or malfunction.
ConceptMeaningHigh vs low
PathogenicityAbility of an organism to cause diseaseHigh = very likely to cause disease; low = less likely to cause infection
VirulenceMeasure or degree of pathogenicityHigh = severe host damage; low = lesser host damage
1. Attachment

Secure a place on the host

  • Pathogens use many of the same attachment factors as non-pathogenic colonizers.
  • Organisms not normally part of the microbiota must outcompete normal flora for host surface sites.
  • An adhesin at the tip of a pilus may bind a specific receptor on the host cell.
2. Invasion

Move below the surface

  • Occurs through barrier disruption or direct action of virulence factors.
  • Shigella can exploit mucosal M cells, cross the epithelial barrier, survive macrophage ingestion, escape, and infect other cells.
  • Enzymes such as hyaluronidases, nucleases, and collagenases hydrolyze host proteins/nucleic acids and facilitate deeper spread.
3. Survive inflammation

Avoid phagocytes and complement

StrategyMechanisms
Avoid ingestionProduce a capsule that inhibits phagocytosis.
Avoid phagocyte-mediated killingInhibit phagosome–lysosome fusion; resist lysosomal agents; multiply inside phagocytes; release toxins/enzymes that damage phagocytes.
Avoid complementCapsule or biofilm hides activating surface molecules; substances inhibit complement activation or destroy complement proteins.
4. Survive the immune system

Immune evasion strategies

  • Rapid invasion and multiplication before the immune response is fully activated.
  • Destruction of cells involved in immune response.
  • Intracellular survival and avoidance of detection.
  • Antigen masking with capsule or biofilm.
  • Altered antigen expression/presentation, repeatedly forcing a primary-type encounter.
  • Proteases that destroy or inactivate antibodies.
Concept Trap

A microbe does not always need to destroy the immune system. It may only need enough time to reach a protected site or transfer to the next susceptible host.

Microbial toxins

Toxins are biochemically active substances released by microorganisms with particular effects on host cells. They help establish infection and multiplication. A pathogen may remain at one site while its toxins produce systemic damage, and toxins may cause disease even when the producing pathogen is absent.

Biofilm formation

  • Biofilm: accumulation of microorganisms embedded in a polysaccharide matrix.
  • Begins with extracellular polymer matrix synthesis, aggregation, and recognition.
  • Matrix formation involves polysaccharide, proteins, and extracellular DNA.
  • Protects against desiccation and toxic compounds and prevents loss of protective molecules.
  • Initial biofilm develops in approximately 4–6 hours, depending on growth rate, followed by maturation.
  • Supports adherence to implants and prosthetic devices.
  • Also affects efficient wastewater treatment.

Pathogens associated with biofilm formation in the lecture

Artificial prosthetics and indwelling devices: Candida albicans, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Enterococcus spp., Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus spp. Food-borne contamination: Listeria monocytogenes.

  • Pathogen is destroyed by the host
  • Pathogen remains latent
  • Pathogen destroys the host

Favorable: host factors overcome microbial factors, allowing complete recovery. Less favorable: microbial factors outperform host defense, potentially causing death.

Principles, Course & Prevention of Infectious Disease

Recognizing disease, sequencing its stages, classifying infection by time course, and interrupting transmission.

Pages 11–12

Signs

Measurable indications or physical observations, such as fever, rash, or swelling.

Symptoms

Indicators described by the patient, such as headache, fatigue, or nausea.

Clinical Pattern

Signs and symptoms arise from host responses to microbial virulence factors and reflect the stages of infection. Examples listed include aches, headache, fever, fatigue, lymph-node swelling, rash, redness, swelling, cough, sneezing, congestion, sore throat, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.

Stages of disease

  1. Incubation: no signs or symptoms; time from introduction of a microbe to onset of illness.
  2. Prodromal: first signs and symptoms; pathogen may be highly communicable. A pre-prodromal phase may include vague malaise and headache.
  3. Clinical: peak of characteristic signs and symptoms.
  4. Decline: host may deteriorate toward death, or signs and symptoms may subside as condition improves.
  5. Convalescent: recuperation and recovery, chronic infection, or death.

Establishing the Cause of Infectious Disease

Koch’s logical framework, its limitations, and the molecular approaches used when classical culture-based criteria are insufficient.

Page 12

Koch’s postulates

  1. The microorganism should be found in all cases of the disease, with distribution consistent with observed lesions.
  2. It should be grown in pure culture in vitro.
  3. Inoculation of a susceptible animal species with the pure culture should reproduce the typical disease.
  4. The microorganism should again be isolated from the experimentally produced lesions.

The lecture notes that Koch tested whether Bacillus anthracis could cause anthrax in susceptible mice, providing a logical framework for linking a microorganism to disease.

Limitations of classical Koch’s postulates

  • Some organisms cannot be grown in laboratory medium, such as Treponema pallidum.
  • Some infected hosts are asymptomatic, as in cholera and polio.
  • Some diseases are polymicrobial, such as chronic periodontal disease.
  • Suitable animal hosts are unavailable for some diseases, and human experimentation would be unethical.

Molecular Koch’s postulates

Modern microbial genetics studies genes associated with virulence and differentiates pathogens from non-pathogens. These postulates are especially relevant to organisms such as E. coli and S. pyogenes, whose strains cause different diseases depending on virulence factors.

  1. The phenotype under study should be associated with pathogenic, not nonpathogenic, strains.
  2. Specific inactivation of the suspected virulence gene should measurably reduce pathogenicity or virulence.
  3. Restoring the wild-type gene should restore pathogenicity or virulence.

Molecular guidelines for microbial disease causation

When pathogens are difficult or impossible to culture, PCR can amplify microorganism-specific nucleic acid sequences from host tissues or fluids to identify the infecting organism.

Lecture examples where these guidelines helped establish causation include Whipple’s disease, bacillary angiomatosis (Bartonella henselae), human monocytic ehrlichiosis (Ehrlichia chaffeensis), hantavirus pulmonary syndrome, and Kaposi sarcoma (HHV-8).

Framework Comparison

Classical Koch: organism–disease association through culture, inoculation, and re-isolation. Molecular Koch: virulence gene–phenotype association. Molecular guidelines: pathogen-specific nucleic acid evidence when culture is difficult or impossible.

Final rapid review

Microbial world

  • Cellular: prokaryotes + eukaryotes.
  • Acellular: viruses, viroids, prions.
  • Size: viruses/viroids/prions < bacteria < protozoa/fungi.
  • Prions are infectious proteins; viroids are naked circular ssRNA; viruses contain either DNA or RNA.

Prokaryote vs eukaryote

  • Prokaryote: no membrane-bound nucleus/organelles, circular DNA in nucleoid, coupled transcription-translation, peptidoglycan wall except archaea.
  • Eukaryote: nucleus + organelles, multiple linear chromosomes, separate transcription and translation.
  • Both have DNA, plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes.

Viruses

  • Obligate intracellular; cannot self-replicate.
  • Virion: nucleic acid + capsid ± lipid envelope.
  • Nucleocapsid: nucleic acid + capsid.
  • Symmetry: icosahedral, helical, complex.
  • Basic classification: morphology, replication/genome, envelope.

Epidemiology

  • Incidence = new cases.
  • Prevalence = old + new cases at a time.
  • Morbidity = disease effects; mortality = deaths.
  • Endemic: constant; epidemic: above normal in a location; outbreak: above normal over short period; pandemic: worldwide epidemic.

Source and spread

  • Reservoir: origin/location of agent.
  • Vector: living transmitter.
  • Vehicle: non-living transmitter.
  • Carrier: asymptomatic person harboring agent.
  • Direct: reservoir-to-host contact.
  • Indirect: intervening agent.
  • Minimal infectious dose: smallest quantity causing infection.

Symbiosis & microbiota

  • Mutualism: both benefit.
  • Commensalism: one benefits; other unharmed.
  • Parasitism: parasite benefits at host expense.
  • Normal microbiota protects by occupying sites, consuming nutrients, making inhibitory compounds, blocking colonization, and stimulating adaptive immunity.
  • Resident reestablishes; transient does not permanently establish.

Four stages

  1. Encounter
  2. Colonization
  3. Entry/invasion/dissemination
  4. Outcome

Infection ≠ always disease. Subclinical infection can occur.

First defenses

  • Skin: barrier, sloughing, dry/acidic/cool environment, gland products.
  • Eyes: tears, lysozyme, blinking.
  • Mucosa: mucus trapping, lubrication, antibodies and non-specific antibacterials.
  • Goblet-cell products: lysozyme, lactoferrin, lactoperoxidase.

Phagocytosis & inflammation

Endocytosis → phagosome → lysosome fusion → phagolysosome → killing.

  • Complement: chemotaxis, opsonization, membrane attack complex.
  • Coagulation: chemotaxis, flow, walling off.
  • Cytokines: enhance defensive cell activities.

Specific immunity

  • Humoral: B-cells/antibodies → extracellular microbes.
  • Cellular: T-cells destroy infected host cells → intracellular microbes.
  • NK cells act like cytotoxic T-cells but do not require antigen stimulation.

Pathogenicity vs virulence

  • Pathogenicity: ability to cause disease.
  • Virulence: degree of pathogenicity / severity of damage.
  • Opportunistic pathogens exploit disrupted or malfunctioning host defenses.

Virulence sequence

Attach → invade → survive inflammation → evade immunity → toxins/biofilm.

  • Capsule can inhibit phagocytosis and hide complement-activating surfaces.
  • Enzymes: hyaluronidases, nucleases, collagenases.
  • Immune evasion: intracellular survival, antigen masking/variation, antibody-destroying proteases.

Endotoxin vs exotoxin

  • Endotoxin: Gram-negative LPS, released with cell destruction; fever, DIC, hypotension, shock.
  • Exotoxin: often Gram-positive, produced/released by living bacteria; specific cell/process effects.

Biofilm

  • Microorganisms in a polysaccharide matrix.
  • Matrix includes polysaccharide, proteins, extracellular DNA.
  • Initial development about 4–6 hours, then maturation.
  • Protects from desiccation and toxic compounds and aids device adherence.

Disease stages

Incubation → prodromal → clinical → decline → convalescent.

  • Acute: quick, short.
  • Chronic: slow, months/years.
  • Latent: persists asymptomatically, may reactivate with decreased immunity.

Prevention

  1. Prevent transmission.
  2. Control microbial reservoirs.
  3. Minimize risk before/shortly after exposure.

Koch frameworks

  • Classical: present in disease → pure culture → reproduce disease → re-isolate.
  • Limitations: unculturable organisms, asymptomatic hosts, polymicrobial disease, no ethical/suitable animal model.
  • Molecular Koch: virulence phenotype association → gene inactivation lowers virulence → wild-type restoration restores it.
  • PCR guidelines: detect organism-specific nucleic acid when culture is difficult.

One-line exam traps

  • Nucleoid ≠ nucleus.
  • Virus = DNA or RNA, not both.
  • Prion has no nucleic acid.
  • Infection can be subclinical.
  • Vector living; vehicle non-living.
  • Prevalence includes old + new cases.
  • Humoral targets outside cells; cellular targets infected host cells.
