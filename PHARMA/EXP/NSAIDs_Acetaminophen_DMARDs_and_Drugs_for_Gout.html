Overview

This lecture connects four decisions: how inflammation is produced, how pain and inflammation are relieved, how gout is treated by phase, and how rheumatoid arthritis is slowed by disease-modifying therapy.

pp. 1–16
Central question
Which drug best matches the therapeutic goal without creating an unacceptable risk for this particular patient? The lecture repeatedly answers this through mechanism, toxicity, disease phase, and the ESSC framework.
01InflammationProtective response, but harmful when misdirected or uncontrolled.
02NSAIDs & analgesicsRelieve symptoms mainly by reducing prostanoid formation or central pain signaling.
03GoutChoose agents based on acute attack, intercritical prophylaxis, chronic urate lowering, or refractory disease.
04Rheumatoid arthritisRelief alone is not enough; DMARDs slow or arrest tissue damage.
05ESSCBalance efficacy, safety, suitability, and cost-effectiveness for the individual patient.

Two therapeutic goals

  1. Relieve symptoms and maintain function. NSAIDs and analgesics can help with pain, fever, and inflammation.
  2. Slow or arrest tissue-damaging processes. In rheumatic disease, this second goal is achieved by DMARDs.
Key connection
NSAIDs can make a patient feel better, but the lecture stresses that they do not alter the course of arthritic disorders. DMARDs are named for their ability to modify the disease process.

Must-know anchors

COX-1 vs COX-2

COX-1 supports “housekeeping,” including gastric epithelial cytoprotection and platelet thromboxane. COX-2 is a major source of inflammatory prostanoids and endothelial prostacyclin.

Exceptions drive exams

Nabumetone is the non-acid NSAID. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits COX. Selective COX-2 inhibitors and non-acetylated salicylates do not inhibit platelet aggregation. Aspirin, salicylates, and tolmetin are not used for acute gout.

Toxicity–antidote pairs

Aspirin → sodium bicarbonate; paracetamol → N-acetylcysteine; methotrexate → leucovorin/folic acid; leflunomide → cholestyramine; cyclophosphamide → mesna.

How the topics connect
Inflammatory prostanoids explain why NSAIDs work. The same inflammatory goal appears in acute gout, while chronic gout requires a separate urate-lowering strategy. In RA, reducing inflammation is helpful, but only disease-modifying drugs address progressive tissue damage.

Inflammation and NSAID Foundations

Start with the protective role of inflammation, then follow arachidonic acid to prostanoids and the sites where NSAIDs act.

pp. 1–2

Inflammation: what it is and why it matters

Simple ideaInflammation brings circulating host-defense cells and molecules into vascularized tissue where infection or injury is present.
Complete explanationWithout it, infections could go unchecked, wounds would not heal properly, and injured tissue could remain persistently damaged. The problem arises when the response is directed against self, triggered by harmless substances, or inadequately controlled.
High-yield takeawayThe aim is not to abolish all inflammation, but to keep it in check while preserving its protective role.
HeatRednessSwellingPainLoss of function
Therapeutic goals
Goal 1: relieve symptoms and maintain function. Goal 2: slow or arrest tissue-damaging processes, especially in chronic disease.

What NSAIDs have in common

  • All but one are weak organic acids.
  • The exception is nabumetone, a ketone prodrug metabolized to an acidic active drug.
  • Their chief anti-inflammatory action is inhibition of prostanoid biosynthesis through cyclooxygenase inhibition.

How this flow relieves symptoms: inhibiting COX-2 decreases prostanoids involved in fever, pain, and inflammation. The lecture specifically identifies PGE₂ in increased body temperature and PGE₂/PGI₂ in pain and inflammation.

Additional lecture-listed NSAID mechanisms
  • Inhibition of chemotaxis
  • Downregulation of IL-1 production
  • Decreased free radicals and superoxide
  • Interference with Ca²⁺-mediated intracellular events
  • Reduced vessel sensitivity to bradykinin and histamine
  • Effects on lymphokine production and reversal of inflammatory vasodilation

Lock It In

  • Core concept: NSAIDs chiefly reduce prostanoid biosynthesis by inhibiting COX.
  • Differentiator: corticosteroids act earlier and have greater anti-inflammatory activity, with adverse effects of their own.
  • Sequence: membrane phospholipids → arachidonic acid → COX → prostanoids.
  • Exam cue: all NSAIDs are weak organic acids except nabumetone.

COX Selectivity, Platelets, GI Safety, and Toxicity

The desired anti-inflammatory effect comes mainly from COX-2 inhibition, but the safety profile depends on which protective or balancing prostanoids are also blocked.

pp. 2–3

COX isoenzymes

  • COX-1: produces “housekeeping” prostanoids, including gastric epithelial cytoprotection and platelet thromboxane.
  • COX-2: major source of inflammatory prostanoids; endothelial COX-2 is the primary source of prostacyclin; renal COX-2 is important for normal renal development.
Key idea
Drug selectivity helps determine which functions are reduced and which are preserved.

Thromboxane vs prostacyclin

MediatorSourceLecture-stated role
Thromboxane (TXA₂)Platelet COX-1Promotes platelet aggregation and thrombosis.
Prostacyclin (PGI₂)Endothelial COX-2Counters thromboxane and reduces platelet aggregation.

Non-selective drugs block both mediators, producing a net inhibition of platelet aggregation. Selective COX-2 inhibition removes prostacyclin while leaving thromboxane relatively unopposed.

Class-wide adverse effects and warnings

GI and renal

  • Abdominal pain, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting, ulcers, bleeding.
  • Renal insufficiency or failure, hyperkalemia, proteinuria.
  • Nephrotoxicity partly reflects interference with prostaglandin-mediated renal blood-flow autoregulation.

CV and systemic

  • Fluid retention, hypertension, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure.
  • Headache, tinnitus, dizziness, rare aseptic meningitis.
  • Asthma, rashes, pruritus; rare hematologic abnormalities.

Hepatic and pregnancy

  • Abnormal liver tests and rare liver failure.
  • Avoid at 20 weeks or later because of low amniotic fluid risk.
  • Avoid at 30 weeks or later because of premature fetal ductus arteriosus closure.
Black box warning
Cardiovascular: serious thrombotic events, MI, and stroke, with risk increasing over duration and in patients with heart disease or risk factors. Gastrointestinal: serious bleeding, ulceration, and perforation may occur without warning; elderly patients are at greater risk.
Use rule
Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible treatment duration.

Lock It In

  • COX-1 is the gastric/platelet housekeeping anchor; COX-2 is the inflammatory/prostacyclin anchor.
  • Selective COX-2 inhibitors trade better GI safety for less cardiovascular protection.
  • All newer NSAIDs are analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic; platelet inhibition is absent with coxibs and non-acetylated salicylates.

NSAID Agents

Organize these drugs by what makes each one different: irreversible action, unusual chemical form, organ toxicity, special indication, or COX selectivity.

pp. 3–5, 16

Aspirin

Mechanism and dose-dependent effects

  • Acetylsalicylic acid (ASA); pKa 3.5.
  • Irreversibly inhibits COX. The platelet effect lasts for the platelet lifespan, about 8–10 days; in other tissues, effects last 6–12 hours.
  • Below 100 mg/day, inhibition preferentially targets COX-1. Higher doses are needed for analgesia and still higher doses for anti-inflammatory effect.
  • Antiplatelet dose stated in the lecture: 80–325 mg once daily.
Unique feature
Other non-selective NSAIDs inhibit COX reversibly. Aspirin is the exception because it irreversibly acetylates platelet COX.

Uses, toxicity, and precautions

  • Used to reduce coronary thrombosis with MI, thrombosis after bypass grafting, transient ischemic attacks, and unstable angina.
  • Alkalinizing urine increases salicylate excretion. In poisoning, give IV sodium bicarbonate to achieve urine pH 7.5.
  • Main adverse effects: gastric upset and gastric/duodenal ulcers; other effects include hepatotoxicity, asthma, rash, GI bleeding, and renal toxicity.
  • Reye’s syndrome: do not use aspirin for fever in children with influenza or varicella.
Aspirin and gout
Do not use aspirin as an analgesic for gout. At doses ≤2.6 g/day it causes renal retention of uric acid; uricosuric action occurs only at doses above 3.6 g/day.
Contraindications and perioperative balance
  • Hypersensitivity to aspirin, salicylates, or NSAIDs
  • Aspirin/NSAID-triggered asthma
  • Active or previous peptic ulcer or GI bleeding; bleeding disorders such as hemophilia
  • Severe liver or renal impairment
  • Children with influenza or varicella
  • Pregnancy at 20 weeks or later, with the lecture-noted exception of low-dose aspirin for selected patients at risk of pre-eclampsia

Before elective major surgery, the lecture recommends a risk–benefit analysis comparing bleeding risk with thrombotic risk. If discontinuation is chosen, aspirin is stopped about one week before surgery.

Non-acetylated salicylates and topical methyl salicylate

  • Magnesium choline salicylate, sodium salicylate, and salsalate are weak COX inhibitors that do not inhibit platelet aggregation.
  • They are rarely used but have lesser toxicity and may be preferable in asthma, bleeding tendency, or renal dysfunction.
  • Topical methyl salicylate is more commonly encountered. Oil of wintergreen is the most concentrated and toxic form; ingestion of 5 mL is equivalent to about 7 g of aspirin and may be fatal to children.

Lock It In

  • Aspirin: irreversible and antiplatelet.
  • Nabumetone: only non-acid NSAID.
  • Ketorolac: analgesic-only NSAID, nephrotoxic, maximum 5 days.
  • Indomethacin/ibuprofen: PDA closure in preterm infants.
  • Celecoxib: better GI profile, worse thrombotic balance.

Paracetamol, Ketorolac, Tramadol, and the WHO Analgesic Ladder

These agents are organized around pain relief. The most important distinctions are anti-inflammatory activity, organ toxicity, mechanism, and place on the analgesic ladder.

pp. 5–6

Paracetamol / acetaminophen

What it does

  • Para-aminophenol derivative and active metabolite of phenacetin.
  • Important for mild to moderate pain when anti-inflammatory action is not required.
  • Weak COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitor in peripheral tissues, with additional lecture-listed interactions involving endogenous opioid, cannabinoid, and serotonergic systems.
  • Analgesic and antipyretic like aspirin, but without significant anti-inflammatory action, platelet effect, or uric-acid effect.
Why it may be preferred
For mild pain, it is relatively safer than NSAIDs and may be used when aspirin is not tolerated, in hemophilia or peptic-ulcer history, in aspirin-triggered bronchospasm, and for children with fever.

Dose and pharmacokinetics

  • Pediatric: 10–15 mg/kg/dose.
  • Adult: 325–500 mg every 4–6 hours; lecture maximum 4 g/day.
  • Peak plasma concentration 30–60 minutes after oral administration.
  • Poor plasma-protein binding; half-life 2–3 hours, at least doubled in liver disease and relatively unaffected by renal function.
  • Available orally, IV, and rectally.

In overdose, the major pathways become saturated, more drug is directed through CYP450, glutathione is depleted, and NAPQI accumulates. The result is oxidative stress and liver injury.

Antidote
N-acetylcysteine (NAC) should be administered as early as possible to neutralize NAPQI by restoring the glutathione pathway.
Hepatotoxicity thresholds and manifestations
  • Therapeutic doses may cause a mild reversible rise in liver enzymes.
  • Do not exceed 4 g/day.
  • Potential toxicity: 7 g/day in adults or 150–200 mg/kg in children.
  • 15 g/day may be fatal, with centrilobular necrosis with or without acute renal tubular necrosis.
  • Early symptoms: diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, nausea.
  • Severe injury may progress to hepatic encephalopathy and hepatorenal syndrome.

Liver disease, malnutrition, and alcoholism decrease usual metabolic pathways and induce CYP450 metabolism; the lecture advises lower dosing or avoidance.

Ketorolac

  • Non-selective COX inhibitor used exclusively as an analgesic.
  • Short-term treatment of moderate to severe or postoperative pain; use for no more than 5 days.
  • May be given IV, IM, intranasally, orally, or ophthalmically; half-life 5–6 hours.
  • About 90% is excreted by the kidneys, 60% unchanged.
  • Give with another analgesic or anesthetic to reduce the need for prolonged ketorolac exposure.
  • Halve doses in patients older than 65, under 50 kg, or with renal impairment.

Tramadol

  • Weak opioid / miscellaneous opioid.
  • Centrally acting synthetic analgesic with weak μ-opioid receptor affinity.
  • Also inhibits serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake.
  • Naloxone blocks only about 30% of its analgesic effect, reflecting its non-opioid actions.
  • Common effects: nausea, vomiting, vertigo, dizziness, fatigue, sedation, dry mouth, constipation, sweating.
  • Important risks: serotonin syndrome with antidepressants and seizures in susceptible patients.

WHO analgesic ladder

Step 1Non-opioids: NSAIDs or paracetamol ± adjuvants
→
Step 2Weak opioids such as codeine, dihydrocodeine, or tramadol ± adjuvants
→
Step 3Strong opioids such as morphine, oxycodone, methadone, buprenorphine, fentanyl + non-opioid ± adjuvants

The ladder was proposed by WHO for cancer patients and may also guide pain management in other chronic illnesses. Move upward when pain persists or increases despite the previous step.

Lock It In

  • Paracetamol: pain + fever, little anti-inflammatory effect, no platelet effect.
  • Overdose sequence: saturated safe pathways → CYP450 → NAPQI → glutathione depletion → hepatic necrosis.
  • Ketorolac: powerful analgesic, renal concern, maximum 5 days.
  • Tramadol: weak μ action plus 5-HT/NE reuptake inhibition.

Gout Pathology and Acute Treatment

An acute gout attack is an inflammatory cascade triggered by monosodium urate crystals. Acute drugs target the inflammatory response rather than lowering urate immediately.

pp. 7–8

Definition and threshold

  • Gout is a metabolic disease with recurrent acute arthritis from monosodium urate deposits in joints and cartilage.
  • It may also present with uric-acid renal calculi, tophi, and interstitial nephritis.
  • Hyperuricemia: serum uric acid greater than 6.8 mg/dL.
  • Most patients with hyperuricemia never develop gout; the lecture states there is no evidence supporting treatment of asymptomatic hyperuricemia.

→

What this flow means: the attack can continue as a self-amplifying cascade. Acute treatment therefore focuses on decreasing inflammatory-cell activity and mediator production.

Treatment by phase

PhaseLecture-listed drugsMain purpose
Acute goutNSAIDs, colchicine, glucocorticoidsRelieve current inflammation and pain
Intercritical goutNSAIDs, colchicinePrevent flares during the period between attacks or during ULT initiation
Chronic goutAllopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid, pegloticaseLower the urate burden

NSAIDs

  • Common: ibuprofen, indomethacin, naproxen.
  • Inhibit phagocytosis of urate crystals and prostaglandin synthesis.
  • All are usable in acute gout except aspirin, salicylates, and tolmetin.
  • Adverse effects involve GI, cardiovascular, and renal systems.

Colchicine

  • Binds tubulin and prevents polymerization into microtubules.
  • Decreases leukocyte migration and phagocytosis.
  • Disrupts NLRP3 inflammasome function and reduces IL-1β/IL-18; also reduces LTB₄ and other mediators.
  • Acute dose stated: 1 mg, then 500 mcg one hour later; begin within 24 hours and not later than 36 hours.
  • Often causes diarrhea.

Glucocorticoids

  • Decrease activation, proliferation, and survival of inflammatory cells.
  • Reduce neutrophil migration, prostaglandins, and proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β.
  • Alternative when NSAIDs or colchicine are contraindicated, especially in renal impairment/CKD.
  • Rule out joint infection before intra-articular treatment.

AbsorptionGood oral absorption; peak at about 2 hours.

Common effects include diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, pharyngolaryngeal pain, fatigue, headache, seizure, elevated LFTs, acute renal failure, and DIC. Rare effects include marrow suppression, peripheral neuritis, myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and death associated with IV use. Reduce dose in hepatic or renal impairment and use caution with CYP3A4 inhibitors.

Last-resort acute option
IL-1 inhibitors—anakinra, canakinumab, rilonacept—are not FDA-approved for acute gout in the lecture and are reserved for patients with contraindications to or refractory disease after traditional therapy.

Lock It In

  • Crystal → NLRP3 → IL-1/prostaglandins → neutrophils → more inflammation.
  • Colchicine: tubulin/microtubule drug; diarrhea is the classic common adverse effect.
  • Acute drugs treat inflammation; chronic drugs reduce urate burden.

Intercritical, Chronic, and Refractory Gout

Long-term management changes the urate burden. The key distinction is whether the drug decreases uric-acid production, increases excretion, or converts uric acid into another compound.

pp. 8–9
Treat-to-target orientation
Allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid, and pegloticase are urate-lowering therapies. The lecture target is serum urate below 6 mg/dL, or below 5 mg/dL in tophaceous gout. NSAID or colchicine prophylaxis is used for 3–6 months because starting urate-lowering treatment may precipitate a flare.

Allopurinol

Simple ideaAllopurinol lowers uric-acid production.
Complete explanationIt is a purine analogue that inhibits xanthine oxidase, which normally converts hypoxanthine and xanthine into uric acid. The fall in uric acid decreases the overall urate burden.
High-yield takeawayIt is the lecture’s most important chronic gout drug and a first-line urate-lowering agent.
  • About 80% absorbed orally; parent half-life 1–2 hours.
  • Converted to alloxanthine/oxypurinol, which also inhibits xanthine oxidase and has a half-life around 15 hours; once-daily dosing is possible.
  • Renally cleared.
  • Initial dose stated: 100 mg/day, titrated by 100 mg every 2–5 weeks; CKD starts at 50 mg/day and titrates by 50 mg.
Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome
Rare but potentially lethal. It may present with SJS/TEN, eosinophilia, leukocytosis, fever, hepatitis, and renal failure, usually within 2–4 weeks. Stop at the first rash. Risk increases with high starting dose, renal dysfunction, certain interacting drugs, and HLA-B*5801; the lecture recommends screening in Han Chinese, Korean, or Thai patients and avoiding allopurinol if positive.
Allopurinol adverse effects and interactions
  • Can precipitate gout at initiation; use NSAID or colchicine concurrently. Do not stop established allopurinol during a flare.
  • GI intolerance, peripheral neuritis, vasculitis, marrow suppression, hepatic toxicity, interstitial nephritis, pruritic rash, rare exfoliative dermatitis and cataracts.
  • Raises azathioprine concentration and may cause fatal cytopenia; the lecture states concomitant use requires a 75% dose reduction. It may also increase cyclophosphamide, oral anticoagulant, and hepatic iron concentrations.

Febuxostat

  • Non-purine, selective xanthine-oxidase inhibitor.
  • Alternative when allopurinol is not tolerated or ineffective.
  • Over 80% absorbed; extensively hepatically metabolized; once-daily dosing despite a 4–8 hour half-life.
  • Common: abnormal liver tests, diarrhea, headache, nausea.
  • May precipitate gout at initiation.
  • Black box: increased cardiovascular death risk.
  • Lecture notes apparent safety in moderate kidney disease.

Probenecid

  • Uricosuric: inhibits proximal-tubule uric-acid reabsorption and secretion, with a net decrease in reabsorption.
  • Used in underexcretors when a xanthine-oxidase inhibitor is contraindicated or tophi are present; may be combined with an XO inhibitor.
  • Increases urinary uric acid and therefore renal-stone risk; increase oral fluid intake.
  • Contraindicated in high uric-acid excretion, renal calculi, or moderate-to-severe CKD beyond stage 3.
  • Also reduces penicillin secretion, prolonging its plasma half-life.

Pegloticase

  • For chronic refractory gout after oral options have failed, especially with ≥2 flares/year or persistent tophi.
  • Recombinant uricase converts uric acid to allantoin, which is easily excreted.
  • PEG attachment prolongs half-life and reduces immunogenicity.
  • 8 mg IV every 2 weeks; rapid effect with a 6–14 day serum half-life.
  • Premedicate with glucocorticoid and H1 blocker for infusion reactions.
  • Avoid in G6PD deficiency because of hemolysis risk from hydrogen peroxide formation.

Lock It In

  • XO inhibitors reduce production; probenecid increases excretion; pegloticase converts uric acid to allantoin.
  • Starting ULT can trigger flares, so use NSAID or colchicine prophylaxis for 3–6 months.
  • Target: SU <6 mg/dL; <5 mg/dL in tophaceous gout.

Rheumatoid Arthritis and the DMARD Framework

RA is progressive and systemic. The lecture’s central treatment distinction is that symptom relief is not enough—therapy must also slow or arrest tissue damage.

pp. 9–10

Why DMARDs matter

  • RA is a progressive immunologic disease that causes systemic effects, shortens life, and reduces mobility and quality of life.
  • The primary goal is to arrest or slow progression by modifying the disease itself.
  • Clinical effects may take 2 weeks to 6 months to appear.
  • NSAIDs may relieve symptoms, but only DMARDs achieve the second therapeutic goal of slowing tissue-damaging processes.

Classification

ClassLecture examplesPattern
Conventional synthetic (csDMARDs)Methotrexate, leflunomide, azathioprine, chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, mesalamine, sulfasalazine, calcineurin inhibitorsMost numerous; methotrexate is the central agent.
Targeted synthetic (tsDMARDs)Baricitinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib; apremilastSmall molecules directed at JAK signaling or PDE4.
Biologic (bDMARDs)Abatacept, rituximab, IL-6 inhibitors, IL-1 inhibitors, TNF-α inhibitorsLarge molecules targeting immune cells, receptors, or cytokines.
BiosimilarsVersions of rituximab, adalimumab, etanercept, infliximabVery similar properties, efficacy, toxicity, and immunogenicity with potential cost reduction.
Combination therapy rule
For RA not responding adequately to monotherapy, combine traditional DMARDs or add TNF inhibitors, non-TNF biologics, or small molecules. Rational combinations use complementary mechanisms, non-overlapping pharmacokinetics, and non-overlapping adverse effects. Most drugs improve efficacy when added to methotrexate.
Glucocorticoids in RA
They act rapidly and dramatically and can slow new bone erosions, but chronic adverse effects can be devastating. For long-term prednisone, the lecture states do not exceed 7.5 mg/day and encourage gradual reduction; even 2.8 mg/day for more than 5 years was associated with severe infections.

Lock It In

  • NSAID = symptom control; DMARD = disease modification.
  • DMARD response may be delayed for weeks to months.
  • Methotrexate is the main conventional anchor and common combination partner.

Conventional Synthetic DMARDs

Study these by target, signature indication, and signature toxicity. Several suppress immune-cell proliferation or signaling, so infection and marrow toxicity recur across the class.

pp. 10–11

Methotrexate and leflunomide

Methotrexate

  • MOA: inhibits AICAR transformylase and thymidylate synthetase, increasing extracellular AMP and suppressing neutrophils, dendritic cells, macrophages, and lymphocytes. It also affects chemotaxis, proliferation, apoptosis, and has minor DHFR effects.
  • Indications: decreases new erosions in RA; also used in JIA, psoriasis/PsA, peripheral joint disease in AS, vasculitis, SLE, and others listed in the lecture.
  • Contraindication: pregnancy.
  • Dose: weekly dosing, beginning at 7.5 mg/week and increasing to 15–25 mg/week; effect may increase up to 30–35 mg/week with more toxicity.
  • AEs: nausea, stomatitis, leukopenia, anemia, GI ulceration, alopecia, elevated liver enzymes.
  • Rescue: leucovorin or folic acid reduces stomatitis, GI, and LFT abnormalities, with a possible 10–18% decrease in efficacy.

Leflunomide

  • Converted to A77 1726, which inhibits dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, decreasing ribonucleotide synthesis and arresting cells in G1.
  • Inhibits T-cell proliferation, decreases B-cell antibody production, and alters IL-10R, IL-8R, and TNF-α/NF-κB activity.
  • Equal to methotrexate in RA efficacy, including inhibition of bony erosions; combination with MTX improved outcomes in the lecture.
  • About 80% absorbed; parent and metabolite half-life around 19 days; enterohepatic recirculation.
  • Cholestyramine reduces half-life to about one day.
  • Common toxicity: diarrhea; also LFT abnormalities, alopecia, weight gain, increased BP, rare leukopenia/thrombocytopenia.

These oral small molecules target intracellular signaling or cyclic-nucleotide metabolism. Their most testable patterns are selectivity, metabolism, and boxed warnings.

JAK inhibitors

Mechanism

Common and important adverse effects

  • Infections, including URTIs and herpes zoster; TB screening is mentioned.
  • Increased liver tests, total cholesterol, and LDL.
  • Rare lymphoma, neutropenia, lymphopenia, anemia.
  • GI perforation in patients with diverticulitis history.
FDA black box
Venous thrombotic events, DVT, pulmonary emboli, major adverse cardiovascular events, and cancer such as lymphoma.

Apremilast

Mechanism and use

  • PDE4 inhibitor that prevents cAMP conversion to AMP.
  • Increases intracellular cAMP and decreases proinflammatory cytokines listed in the lecture, including CXCL9/10, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1, 8, 12, 23.
  • Used for plaque psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and oral ulcers in Behçet disease.

PK and toxicity

  • Bioavailability 73%; half-life 6–9 hours.
  • About 70% CYP3A4 metabolism; 58% renal and 39% fecal elimination.
  • Dose adjustment if CrCl <30.
  • Most common: diarrhea and nausea. Rare: abdominal pain, vomiting, URTI, arthralgia, weight loss, headache, mood disorders, suicidal ideation.

Lock It In

  • -nib identifies the lecture’s JAK inhibitors.
  • JAK inhibitors: infection + lipid/LFT increases + thrombotic/CV/cancer boxed warning.
  • Apremilast: PDE4 inhibition → ↑cAMP → ↓proinflammatory cytokines.
Tab 11

Biologic DMARDs

Biologics target T-cell activation, B cells, cytokines, or cytokine receptors. The recurring safety themes are infections, infusion/injection reactions, and malignancy risk.

pp. 12–14

Abatacept

  • CTLA-4–Ig fusion protein.
  • Binds CD80/CD86 on antigen-presenting cells and blocks interaction with CD28, preventing T-cell activation.
  • Used in RA, PsA, polyarticular JIA, and prevention of acute graft-versus-host disease.
  • Infection, headache, nasopharyngitis, nausea; increased lymphoma risk; rare infusion and hypersensitivity reactions.
  • Avoid live vaccines for up to 3 months after discontinuation.

Rituximab

  • Targets CD20 B lymphocytes through cell-mediated and complement-dependent cytotoxicity and apoptosis.
  • Depletes peripheral and synovial B cells, reducing antigen presentation and proinflammatory cytokine secretion.
  • Used with MTX in RA and with glucocorticoids in granulomatosis with polyangiitis / microscopic polyangiitis.
  • Infusion reactions, urticaria, anaphylaxis, SJS, serious infection.
  • Hepatitis B may reactivate and requires treatment or monitoring after discontinuation.

TNF-α inhibitors

Structural anchors

  • Adalimumab and golimumab: nearly identical human monoclonal antibodies.
  • Infliximab: chimeric antibody, 25% mouse and 75% human.
  • Etanercept: fusion protein of two p75 receptors linked to human IgG Fc; can be used as monotherapy.
  • Certolizumab: humanized Fab fragment conjugated to PEG and the only TNF inhibitor without an Fc region; the lecture identifies it as the TNF inhibitor of choice in pregnancy because it does not cross placenta or breastmilk.
Methotrexate connection
Most TNF inhibitors are combined with MTX to increase efficacy and reduce anti-drug antibody formation, especially for chimeric infliximab.
Watch out
Common: injection-site reactions and infections. Severe infections include fungal disease and HBV/TB reactivation; etanercept has a lower TB-reactivation risk. Other serious effects include new/worsening CHF, demyelinating disorders, lupus-like syndrome, and blood dyscrasias.

Biosimilars and other biologics

Biosimilars

Available for rituximab, adalimumab, etanercept, and infliximab. The lecture describes them as highly similar in properties and actions to the original biologic, with similar efficacy, toxicity, and immunogenicity, while potentially reducing cost and improving access.

Other cytokine targets

  • Guselkumab and risankizumab inhibit IL-23.
  • Ustekinumab inhibits IL-23 and IL-12 and also has IBD indications.
  • Ixekizumab and secukinumab inhibit IL-17A; secukinumab also has axial-spondyloarthritis uses.
  • Anifrolumab targets type-I interferon; belimumab targets BLyS. The lecture notes SLE-related use/trials.

Lock It In

  • Abatacept: CD80/86–CD28 costimulation block.
  • Rituximab: CD20 B-cell depletion.
  • Tocilizumab/sarilumab: IL-6 receptor blockade; LDL rises.
  • TNF inhibitors: serious infection/TB/HBV + CHF/demyelination/lupus-like syndrome.
Tab 12

ESSC, Comparisons, and Common Confusions

No single NSAID or DMARD is best for everyone. This tab turns the lecture’s distinctions into a patient-centered selection framework.

pp. 2–16

ESSC decision framework

E — Efficacy

All NSAIDs are described as equally efficacious, with notable exceptions: tolmetin is not effective for gout; aspirin is not used for gout, is less effective for ankylosing spondylitis, and is not used for fever in children with viral infection.

S — Safety

Match toxicity to patient risk. Consider GI bleeding, renal function, liver abnormalities, cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, age, weight, interacting drugs, and prior reactions.

S — Suitability

Consider current drugs, allergies, comorbidities, available dosage forms, and a dosing schedule the patient can follow. The lecture emphasizes that adherence is especially poor in gout.

C — Cost-effectiveness

Balance clinical benefit with the cost and accessibility of the option. The lecture highlights biosimilars as a possible way to reduce biologic-DMARD cost and pegloticase as a costly option reserved after oral therapy.

Bottom line
There is no universal “best NSAID.” There may be one or two best choices for a particular patient after balancing efficacy, safety, suitability, and cost-effectiveness.

Entire lecture

NSAID master rules

  1. All are weak organic acids except nabumetone.
  2. All reversibly inhibit COX except aspirin.
  3. All inhibit platelet aggregation except selective COX-2 inhibitors and non-acetylated salicylates.
  4. All are effective in acute gout except aspirin, salicylates, and tolmetin.
  5. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time.
  6. Avoid at 20 weeks pregnancy for low amniotic fluid and at 30 weeks for fetal ductus closure.

COX logic

  • COX-1: gastric protection + platelet TXA₂.
  • COX-2: inflammatory prostanoids + endothelial PGI₂.
  • Non-selective: more GI harm, but inhibits TXA₂.
  • Selective COX-2: less GI irritation, not cardioprotective, TXA₂ > PGI₂.
  • Black boxes: CV thrombosis and serious GI bleeding/perforation.

Gout sequence

Urate crystal → NLRP3 → IL-1/prostaglandins → PMN recruitment → inflammatory cascade.

Acute: NSAID, colchicine, glucocorticoid.
Intercritical prophylaxis: NSAID or colchicine for 3–6 months.
Chronic: allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid.
Refractory: pegloticase.

Target SU: <6 mg/dL; <5 mg/dL in tophaceous gout.

RA / DMARD logic

NSAIDs relieve; DMARDs modify.

Conventional: MTX anchor, leflunomide, AZA, HCQ/CQ, CYC, MMF, SSZ, calcineurin inhibitors.
Targeted: JAK inhibitors, apremilast.
Biologic: abatacept, rituximab, IL-6, IL-1, TNF inhibitors.

Combination therapy when monotherapy is inadequate; most agents improve when added to MTX.
