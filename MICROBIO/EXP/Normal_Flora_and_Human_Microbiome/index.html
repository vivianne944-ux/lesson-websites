Normal Flora & Human Microbiome

Explanation-focused lecture reviewer · MCB 1.06 · July 8, 2026

Tab 1 · Orientation only

The body is an ecosystem, not a sterile container.

The lecture replaces the old “sterile host” idea with a modern view: humans live with stable microbial communities whose collective genomes form the microbiome. These organisms defend surfaces, process nutrients, shape immunity, and can contribute to disease when their ecology is disturbed. pp. 1–7

Central question

How can the same microbes support health in one setting but contribute to disease in another? The answer depends on where they are, whether the local environment remains balanced, and whether normal bacterial interference and barriers are intact.

01IdentityResident vs transient microbiota
02LocationSkin, oral cavity, gut, genitalia, nasopharynx
03FunctionDefense, immune maturation, metabolism
04BreakdownDysbiosis and opportunist overgrowth
05RestorationPrebiotics, probiotics, other strategies

Anchor 1: Stability

Resident microbiota are fixed, stable communities that promptly reestablish after disturbance. Transient microbiota come from the environment and remain for hours, days, or weeks. p. 1

Anchor 2: Interference

Residents block transient colonization by receptor competition, nutrient consumption, and bacteriocin production. p. 1

Anchor 3: Context

A resident organism can become dangerous when moved into a foreign site: the lecture’s example is Bacteroides entering the peritoneal cavity. p. 1

Site environments shape the cast

Acidic pH and sebum help structure the skin microbiome; the mouth is a layered biofilm environment; the gut shifts toward increasing bacterial density and anaerobic dominance as contents become more alkaline. pp. 1–6

Dysbiosis follows an ecological sequence

The lecture’s framework is: homeostasis → ecological pressure → loss of bacterial interference and diversity → opportunist overgrowth → inflammation and disease. p. 6

Tab 2 · Lesson begins

Human microbiome and bacterial interference

Start with the conceptual shift, then see how resident communities protect their territory. p. 1

Old model: “The sterile host”

The outdated paradigm treated humans as isolated cellular entities and viewed all microbes as potential threats or pathogens.

Why this model failsThe lecture’s modern framework shows that microbial communities are not merely contaminants; they are required for defense, nutrient processing, and immune development.

Modern model: a host–microbe system

The lecture gives an approximate 1:1 ratio of microbial cells to human somatic/germ cells. The collective genomes of these symbionts form the microbiome.

The Human Microbiome Project, using broad 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing, showed that the microbiota provides a first line of defense, digests nutrients, and matures the immune system.

Resident microbiota

What it is: fixed, stable communities.

How it behaves: if disturbed, it promptly reestablishes.

Relationship with host: described as mutualistic/commensal.

Transient microbiota

What it is: organisms derived from the environment.

Duration: hours, days, or weeks.

Risk: may show pathogenic potential when resident flora breaks down.

How bacterial interference works

Resident organisms occupy receptorsFewer attachment sites remain for incoming microbes.
→
Residents consume nutrientsTransients face reduced access to local resources.
→
Residents produce bacteriocinsAntimicrobial factors directly inhibit competitors.
→
Colonization is resistedThe established community protects its ecological space.

What this flow means: protection is both indirect (competition) and direct (antimicrobial production). The gut section later repeats this same logic using bacteriocins and lactic acid.

Clinical pearl: the opportunist’s windowResidents can become deadly pathogens if forcefully moved to a foreign site. The lecture example is Bacteroides entering the peritoneal cavity through trauma, leading to suppuration and bacteremia.

Key distinction: “normal flora” describes an organism in its normal ecological setting. It does not guarantee harmlessness everywhere.

Lock It In

  • Core concept: microbiota are essential partners, not automatically pathogens.
  • Differentiator: stability and reestablishment define resident flora.
  • Mechanism: receptor competition + nutrient consumption + bacteriocins.
  • Exam cue: breakdown of resident flora creates an opening for transient or opportunistic organisms.

Reading a “normal flora” result

The lecture repeatedly emphasizes three questions: Which site? How much? and Is it mixed or predominant? The same organism may be expected in small numbers but concerning when it dominates.

Do not confuse“Present in normal flora” does not mean “always harmless.” Site displacement, overgrowth, loss of competition, or predominance can change clinical significance.

  • Key pattern: GI tract = abundant anaerobes.
  • Exam cue: vaginal delivery → Lactobacillus pattern; C-section → Staphylococcus pattern.

Skin microbiome: a living barrier

Different skin microenvironments select different organisms, while resident microbes help maintain acidic pH, surface renewal, immune defense, and barrier homeostasis. pp. 1–2

Three ecological zones

  • Sebaceous: glabella
  • Moist: axilla
  • Dry: volar forearm

Why this matters: moisture and sebum create different local conditions, so the microbial cast is not uniform across the skin.

Resident microbial cast

  • Diphtheroid bacilli: Corynebacterium, Propionibacterium
  • Coagulase-negative staphylococci, especially S. epidermidis; occasional S. aureus
  • Peptostreptococcus
  • Gram-positive spore-forming bacilli from air, water, and soil
  • Viridans streptococci and enterococci
  • Gram-negative coliform bacilli and Acinetobacter
  • Fungi/yeasts in folds
  • Nonpathogenic acid-fast mycobacteria in sebaceous areas

Why nonresident organisms are eliminated

Low skin pHCreates an unfavorable environment for many outsiders.
+
Fatty acids in sebumContribute to the acidic chemical barrier.
+
LysozymeAdds antimicrobial activity.
→
Resident flora persistsEstablished organisms are repeatedly replenished.

Important lecture point: sweating, washing, and bathing do not eliminate or significantly modify normal resident flora. Vigorous scrubbing may reduce superficial organisms, but flora is rapidly replenished from sebaceous and sweat glands. pp. 1–2

Core physiologic functions

  • Protease secretion aids desquamation and stratum corneum renewal.
  • Lipase secretion breaks down the lipid film.
  • Sebum and free fatty acids help regulate acidic pH.
  • Microbes make biofilm, produce bacteriocins, and sense quorums.

Immune and barrier role

The skin microbiome contributes to breakdown of natural products, development of the immune system, and defense against pathogen invasion. It can promote innate and adaptive immune responses and helps preserve skin homeostasis and barrier function.

PsoriasisGenetic susceptibility paired with altered microbial profiles provokes an exaggerated immune response.Susceptibility + altered profile + exaggerated immunity

The lecture also links lifestyle, stress, nutrition, hygiene, age, medication/antibiotic use, genetics, hormones, immune conditions, and metabolic conditions to skin illness through interconnected gut, lung, skin, and brain axes. p. 2

Lock It In

  • Core concept: the skin microbiome is part of the barrier, not just organisms sitting on it.
  • Differentiator: sebaceous, moist, and dry zones create distinct communities.
  • Mechanism: proteases renew stratum corneum; lipases break down lipid film; sebum/free fatty acids support acidic pH.
  • Exam cue: atopic dermatitis = reduced S. epidermidis protection plus S. aureus hyper-colonization.

Tab 5

Oral microbiome: the biofilm cavity

The mouth supports the second largest microbial population in the lecture, around 1,000 species. Its defining feature is a structured biofilm that develops on a salivary film—not directly on tooth mineral. pp. 2–3

Why the mouth is a biofilm ecosystem

The oral microbiome colonizes hard and soft tissues. Dental plaque is described as the most prevalent and densest of human biofilms.

This organization supports oral homeostasis, safeguards the oral cavity, and helps halt disease progression.

Physiologic roles

  • Food and nutrient digestion
  • Energy production
  • Development of host mucosa and immune system
  • Regulation of fat depot and metabolism
  • Processing/elimination of environmental chemicals
  • Conservation of body defenses
  • Balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines

The dental pellicle: the required foundation

The microbiome does not adhere directly to the mineralized tooth. It first forms on the dental pellicle, a thin organic film of salivary proteins and glycoproteins. Early colonizers such as Streptococcus sanguis bind to this film and establish the base for a complex biofilm.

Pellicle formsSalivary proteins and glycoproteins coat mineralized enamel.
→
Early colonizers bindGram-positive organisms attach to the pellicle.
→
“Cement” developsS. mutans synthesizes high-molecular-weight extracellular glucan polymers.
→
Late colonizers arriveGram-negative anaerobes appear over 2–4 days.

What this flow means: plaque assembly is ordered. The tooth surface is conditioned first, pioneer organisms establish the structure, and later organisms join the mature community.

Tooth eruption changes the habitat

When teeth erupt, the ecosystem gains the gingival crevice, which provides a sanctuary for anaerobic species. This is a major ecological transition because new protected surfaces become available.

Homeostasis can shift

The lecture shows a continuum from commensal to opportunistic to pathogenic communities. External and internal factors can move the oral microbiome along this continuum.

Factors that influence oral homeostasis

DiabetesGenetic differencesSalivary proteinsSalivary flow rateInnate/adaptive immunityOral hygieneDietSmokingAntibiotics/antimicrobials

Pattern to notice: some factors alter the physical environment (saliva, diet, hygiene), while others alter host defense or microbial selection (immune factors, disease, antibiotics).

Lock It In

  • Core concept: oral microbiota usually exist as an organized biofilm.
  • Differentiator: organisms attach to the dental pellicle, not directly to mineralized tooth.
  • Sequence: pellicle → early colonizers → glucan “cement” → late anaerobes.
  • Exam cue: tooth eruption introduces the gingival crevice and supports anaerobic colonization.

Tab 7

Gut microbiome: distribution along the tract

The gut is the lecture’s “metabolic engine.” Microbial density rises dramatically from the acidic stomach toward the alkaline distal intestine, while anaerobes increasingly dominate. p. 4

Scale and dominance

The lecture describes about 100 trillion microorganisms in the gut and states that anaerobes outnumber facultative organisms 1,000-fold.

Simple idea: oxygen availability and pH help determine which organisms can persist.

Stomach protection

Stomach acidity keeps microorganisms at a minimum: about 10²–10³/mL of contents. Pyloric obstruction can favor Gram-positive cocci and bacilli. Among hundreds of detected gastric phylotypes, the lecture states that only Helicobacter pylori persists.

Normal acid pH protects against some enteric pathogens, including Vibrio cholerae.

The bacterial density gradient

GI segmentApproximate bacterial count in lectureWhat changes
Stomach10²–10³/mLStrong acidity limits growth.
Duodenum10³–10⁴/mL effluentCounts begin to rise as contents become more alkaline.
Jejunum10⁴–10⁵/mLHigher population than duodenum.
Ileum10⁸/mLMarked increase toward distal small bowel.
Cecum and transverse colon10¹¹–10¹²/mLHighest recorded for any microbial habitat in the lecture.

Pattern to notice: the counts increase by orders of magnitude as the environment becomes less acidic and more favorable for dense anaerobic communities.

Mucosa vs lumen

In the upper intestine, mucosa-associated bacteria include Bacteroidetes and members of the Clostridiales. The lumen can contain Enterobacteriales and enterococci.

Fecal mass and motility

In the sigmoid colon and rectum, bacteria make up about 60% of fecal mass. In diarrhea, bacterial content may fall greatly; in intestinal stasis, the count rises.

What acid-suppressing drugs change

Antacids, H₂ receptor antagonists, and proton pump inhibitors increase gastric microbial flora, including organisms usually prevalent in feces. The lecture’s clinical pearl states that PPIs neutralize stomach acid and allow proximal migration of fecal flora into the stomach and duodenum.

Cause and effectLess acid → weaker gastric ecological barrier → greater survival and proximal migration of organisms.

Lock It In

  • Core concept: microbial density rises from stomach to colon.
  • Differentiator: the colon is densely anaerobic; the stomach is sparsely colonized because of acid.
  • Sequence: 10²–10³ → 10³–10⁴ → 10⁴–10⁵ → 10⁸ → 10¹¹–10¹².
  • Exam cue: acid suppression increases gastric flora and may allow fecal flora to migrate proximally.

Gut functions, defense lines, metabolism, and dysbiosis

The intestinal microbiota protects by competition, develops mucosal immunity, performs metabolic work, produces short-chain fatty acids, interacts with gut hormones, and influences drug metabolism. pp. 4–6

Three major categories of intestinal function

CategoryHow it worksLecture examples
ProtectiveResident bacteria displace and inhibit potential pathogens.Competition for nutrients/receptors; production of bacteriocins and lactic acid.
Immune-developmentalCommensals guide development and function of the mucosal immune system.Induce IgA; influence intestinal humoral immunity; modulate local T-cell responses and cytokine profiles.
MetabolicMicrobes supply or transform nutrients and metabolites.Amino acid contribution; SCFAs; vitamin K, biotin, folate; ion absorption; dietary carcinogen metabolism; fermentation of nondigestible residue; influence on fat deposition.

The three “lines of defense”

1. MicrobiomeDiet and environmental factors shape microbial composition.
↔
2. Gut barrierControls translocation of luminal components and metabolites.
→
3. Liver filterFilters toxins and metabolites arriving through the portal vein.

Breakdown: an unhealthy microbial state can increase intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”), allowing pathogens and toxins into the bloodstream. Genetics, age, sex, and lifestyle modify the process. The lecture also connects these interactions to appetite, insulin secretion, glucose metabolism, obesity, and diabetes. p. 5

SCFAs: the central metabolic output

Gut microbiota ferment dietary fiber to produce acetate, propionate, and butyrate. The lecture states that SCFAs strengthen barrier integrity, regulate immune responses, affect glucose metabolism, and mitigate inflammation.

Dietary manipulation with probiotics and prebiotics can favor beneficial bacteria and increase SCFA production.

Host metabolic effects

The lecture links increased SCFAs and microbiota modulation with decreased lipogenesis, liver inflammation and oxidative stress; decreased adipogenesis and adipocyte size/number; increased gut hormones and intestinal transit; and reduced appetite.

Oxygen tension and hypoxia

Intestinal oxygen tension balances oxidative metabolism and glycolysis and affects nutrient absorption, barrier integrity, and immune function. Optimal oxygen levels support an anaerobic environment favorable to beneficial gut bacteria, while hypoxia signaling activates protective genes required for gut homeostasis.

Drug and xenobiotic metabolism

Changes in gut microbial composition can alter drug metabolism and potentially change therapeutic outcomes. The lecture figure catalogs drug–microbe transformations but does not provide a single universal direction of effect.

Neuroprotection: IPA

The lecture identifies 3-indolepropionic acid (IPA) as a potent neuroprotective antioxidant. IPA is absorbed, binds the pregnane X receptor (PXR) on intestinal cells, distributes to the brain, and protects against ischemia.

Vitamin K

Gut microbial vitamin K synthesis is presented as essential for the host coagulation cascade.

Gut dysbiosis: metabolic and inflammatory pathology

ConditionLecture-supported mechanismHigh-yield link
ObesityAltered microbiome increases host energy harvest from indigestible carbohydrates.More harvest from otherwise indigestible substrate
Type 2 diabetesMicrobiota imbalance alters circadian rhythms and metabolic regulation, affecting glucose levels.Ecology → rhythm/metabolism → glucose

Lock It In

  • Core concept: gut microbiota perform protective, immune, and metabolic functions.
  • Differentiator: barrier failure permits translocation; liver then acts as a portal filter.
  • Key metabolites: acetate, propionate, butyrate, vitamin K, and IPA.
  • Exam cue: IBD in this lecture = loss of colonization resistance plus LPS-driven inflammation.

Tab 9

Microbiome-based therapeutics: restoring ecology

The lecture frames therapy as changing the ecological system—feeding beneficial residents, adding live organisms, transferring communities, or using microbial products and mimetics. p. 7

Prebiotics

Definition: substrates selectively utilized by host microorganisms that confer a health benefit. In simple terms, they are food for beneficial microbes.

They alter the composition and/or function of gut microbiota. Most are dietary fibers, but not all dietary fibers are prebiotics.

Probiotics

Definition: live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host.

They can support resident bacteria when challenged by antibiotics, poor diet, or travel. They are found in foods and dietary supplements.

Prebiotic sources and effects

AreaLecture details
Food sourcesWhole plant foods: fruits, vegetables, grains, legumes, and seeds. FOS and inulin are found in onions, garlic, and Jerusalem artichokes. Cooked-then-cooled potatoes provide resistant starch. Polyphenols are being studied as possible prebiotics.
Human milkProvides a rich prebiotic source for the nursing infant and encourages beneficial gut microbes.
Potential benefitsImproved digestive function and bowel regularity; immune support; improved mineral absorption; regulation of appetite, energy balance, and glucose metabolism.
Tolerance pointToo much prebiotic may cause gas or bloating. The lecture advises starting with small amounts and allowing the gut to adapt.

Probiotic effects listed in the lecture

Reduce antibiotic-associated diarrheaManage digestive discomfortReduce infant colic symptoms and eczemaAid lactose digestionTreat infectious diarrheaDecrease risk/duration of respiratory, gut, and vaginal infections

Lock It In

  • Core concept: prebiotics are substrates; probiotics are live microorganisms.
  • Differentiator: most prebiotics are fibers, but not all fibers qualify.
  • Mechanism: probiotics interact during transit; prebiotics alter resident composition/activity and metabolites.
  • Exam cue: probiotics rarely permanently colonize.

Cram Sheet

Rapid retrieval only. Every point below was taught earlier; no new information is introduced here.

Foundations

  • Microbiome: collective genomes of microbial symbionts.
  • Modern ratio: about 1 microbial cell : 1 human somatic/germ cell.
  • Resident: fixed, stable, reestablishes.
  • Transient: environmental, hours/days/weeks.
  • Interference: receptor competition + nutrient consumption + bacteriocins.
  • Opportunist window: resident in foreign site can become pathogenic.

Normal flora map

  • Skin: S. epidermidis, Corynebacterium, Propionibacterium, others.
  • Nasopharynx: diphtheroids, nonpathogenic Neisseria, streptococci, anaerobes.
  • GI/rectum: large numbers of anaerobes; selected Enterobacteriaceae except listed pathogens.
  • Genitalia: Lactobacillus and mixed flora; pure/predominant growth changes significance.
  • Delivery: vaginal → Lactobacillus; C-section → Staphylococcus.

Skin

  • Zones: sebaceous, moist, dry.
  • Elimination factors: low pH, fatty acids, lysozyme.
  • Proteases: stratum corneum renewal.
  • Lipases: lipid film breakdown.
  • Acne: Propionibacterium overgrowth in blocked sebum-rich follicles.
  • Atopic dermatitis: loss of S. epidermidis protection + S. aureus hyper-colonization.

Oral

  • Defining structure: biofilm.
  • Foundation: dental pellicle of salivary proteins/glycoproteins.
  • Sequence: pellicle → early colonizers → glucan “cement” → late anaerobes.
  • Tooth eruption: introduces gingival crevice.
  • Endocarditis cue: dental trauma → transient bacteremia → Viridans on deformed valve.
  • Diabetes cue: periodontitis → AGEs → RAGE → cytokines → altered glucose control.

Gut counts

  • Stomach: 10²–10³/mL
  • Duodenum: 10³–10⁴/mL
  • Jejunum: 10⁴–10⁵/mL
  • Ileum: 10⁸/mL
  • Cecum/transverse colon: 10¹¹–10¹²/mL
  • Anaerobes: 1,000× facultative organisms.
  • Colon/rectum bacteria: ~60% fecal mass.

Gut functions

  • Protective: competition + bacteriocins/lactic acid.
  • Immune: IgA, humoral development, T-cell/cytokine modulation.
  • Metabolic: amino acids, SCFAs, vitamin K, biotin, folate, ion absorption, fermentation.
  • SCFAs: acetate, propionate, butyrate.
  • IPA: PXR binding → brain distribution → protection against ischemia.
  • PPIs: less acid → increased flora/proximal fecal migration.

Gut dysbiosis

  • Obesity: increased energy harvest from indigestible carbohydrates.
  • T2DM: altered circadian rhythms/metabolic regulation → glucose impact.
  • IBD: loss of colonization resistance + LPS interaction → chronic inflammation.
  • Framework: pressure → interference loss/diversity drop → opportunist overgrowth → inflammation/disease.

Therapeutics

  • Prebiotic: selectively utilized substrate; food for beneficial microbes.
  • Probiotic: live microorganism in adequate amount conferring benefit.
  • Not all fibers are prebiotics.
  • Probiotics rarely permanently colonize.
  • Shared goal: normalize microbiota after antibiotics or stressors.
