1Overview: Drug, Medicine, Active Components

Must know Core definitions and the drug–medicine distinction Page 1
TermLecture meaningFast exam clue
DrugA substance of known structure that produces a biological effect when administered. It changes biological function through chemical action.Think biological effect.
MedicineA chemical preparation administered with the intention of producing a therapeutic effect. It usually, but not necessarily, contains one or more drugs.Think finished preparation for therapy.
Active principleThe specific chemical constituent in a crude animal or vegetable preparation that causes its biological activity.Think active part of the crude source.
Active ingredientThe substance or combination in a finished pharmaceutical product that directly produces pharmacologic activity in diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, prevention, or modification of physiological function.Think active part of the finished product.
ExcipientAn inactive substance formulated alongside the active ingredient for bulk, manufacturing, protection, delivery, or convenience.Think inactive but useful.
Must know
  • Drug uses in this lecture are prevention (vaccines), diagnosis (contrast materials and radiopharmaceutical contrast), and cure (antibiotics that eliminate a causative agent).
  • An endogenous substance such as insulin or thyroxine becomes a drug when it is administered intentionally or exogenously.
  • A drug can be useful outside clinical treatment. The lecture uses methicillin as a research or diagnostic tool for determining microbial sensitivity or resistance.
Common confusion
Active principle belongs to the crude natural preparation. Active ingredient belongs to the finished pharmaceutical product. The product label example on page 1 separates active and inactive ingredients.

Recall check Definitions

What is the quickest distinction between an active principle and an active ingredient?
Active principle is the biologically active constituent of a crude preparation. Active ingredient is the active substance in the finished product.
What three uses of drugs are listed?
Prevention, diagnosis, and cure.

2Nature of Drugs: Composition, Size, Shape, Chirality

Must know Two groups, drug size, and selective binding Page 2
Must know

Two general groups

  • Organic compounds: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and smaller molecules.
  • Inorganic elements: lithium, iron, and heavy metals.
Must know

Selective receptor binding

  • The molecule must be sufficiently unique in shape, charge, and other properties.
  • Drug shape should be complementary to the receptor site.
Size categoryLecture exampleImplication
Very smallLithium ion, MW 7At the low end of the size range.
TypicalMost drugs, MW 100–1000The main range emphasized in the lecture.
LargeAbove MW 1000Do not diffuse readily between body compartments. May need carriers, transporters, or additional formulations and excipients.
Very large, protein-basedAlteplase t-PA, MW 50,050Often delivered directly into the compartment of action, such as IV or intra-arterial delivery into the vascular compartment.
Exam trap from the lecture review
Presence of organic compounds is not the property that enables selective receptor binding. The tested properties are molecular weight, chirality, and charge.
Should know Chirality and potency Page 2
  • More than half of useful drugs are described as chiral molecules.
  • Chiral molecules may exist as enantiomeric pairs.
  • A drug with two asymmetric centers can have four diastereomers. The lecture figure uses ephedrine.
  • One enantiomer may be much more potent because it fits the receptor better.
  • The lecture uses dextroamphetamine as an example and states a 10-fold potency difference.
  • Dextro = right. Levo = left.
What to notice in the figures
Page 2 pairs mirror-image hands with molecular structures to show that mirror-image forms are not superimposable and may interact differently with a receptor.

Recall check Chirality

Why can one enantiomer be more potent than its mirror image?
It may fit the receptor molecule better.
What do dextro and levo indicate?
Dextro indicates right. Levo indicates left.

3Sources of Drugs

Must know Natural sources: plants, animals, microorganisms, minerals Pages 2–3

Natural products can serve as patterns or templates for synthetic versions.

Natural sourceCore ideaExamples in the lecture
PlantsMost prevalent natural drug source. Plants or plant parts may have therapeutic or toxic effects.
  • Alkaloids: atropine, vinblastine, vincristine, quinine, colchicine, morphine
  • Morphine from Papaver somniferum
  • Atropine from Atropa belladonna
  • Quinine from Cinchona bark
  • Glycosides: digoxin, ouabain
  • Digoxin from Digitalis purpurea
  • Salicin from willow tree
  • Oils: castor, olive, peppermint, spearmint
  • Gums and resins: latex
AnimalsHormones and substances were extracted from organs before biotechnology and genetic engineering became available.
  • Lanolin from sebaceous glands of wool-bearing animals
  • Insulin from pancreatic β-cells
  • Oxytocin and vasopressin associated with the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei
  • Heparin as a liver extract
  • Cyanocobalamin from animal liver
MicroorganismsMajor source of antimicrobials and a platform for biotechnology.
  • Penicillin from Penicillium chrysogenum
  • Streptomycin from Streptomyces griseus
  • Chloramphenicol from Streptomyces venezuelae
  • Erythromycin, formerly called Ilotycin before patenting, linked to its discovery in Iloilo
  • Genetically modified E. coli to produce insulin
MineralsInorganic substances used mainly for nutritional purposes or selected pathologic conditions.
  • Ferrous sulfate for iron deficiency anemia
  • Calcium for treatment and prevention of osteoporosis
  • Magnesium sulfate as a laxative
  • Magnesium trisilicate as an antacid
  • Zinc sulfate
Nice to know

The lecture states that there are 500,000 natural products, 160,000 products in worldwide use, and that half of 100,000 newly studied metabolites are from plants.

Must know Synthetic, semi-synthetic, and radiopharmaceutical sources Page 3
Synthetic

Made from starting materials not found in nature. The lecture says they can be inexpensive and mass-produced.

Examples: aspirin, sulfonamide, pethidine, procaine, methotrexate.

Semi-synthetic

Natural starting material is chemically modified to change structure, effects, or actions.

Example: ampicillin is described as a modified natural penicillin with broader coverage or spectrum.

Radiopharmaceutical

Radioactive substance used for diagnosis or treatment.

Examples: radioactive iodine, technetium, iridium-192.

Natural starting material
↓ chemical modification
Semi-synthetic drug with enhanced effect or different action

Recall check Drug sources

Ampicillin belongs to which source category in the lecture?

What is the key starting-material distinction between synthetic and semi-synthetic drugs?
Synthetic drugs start from materials not found in nature. Semi-synthetic drugs chemically modify a natural starting material.
Which radiopharmaceutical is placed inside or near a tumor?
Iridium-192.

5Dosage-Form Selection and Routes of Administration

Must know What determines the dosage form? Page 3

Dosage form: the marketed form of a pharmaceutical product, usually combining active drug components with nondrug components such as excipients and other material such as a capsule shell.

Pharmacokinetics

Liberation → absorption → distribution → metabolism → excretion

Pharmacodynamics

Effects in the body, mechanism of action, and concentration–effect relationship

Patient factors

Age and sex

Disease factors

Severity, duration, response, and comorbid conditions, including allergies to excipients

Clinical selection cue
The dosage form should match how aggressively the patient needs to be managed. The lecture’s review explicitly identifies comorbidity as a factor in dosage-form selection.
Must know Route classification and bioavailability chart Page 3

Enteral

Oral, enteral nutrition through gastric or duodenal tubes, and rectal. PO = per orem = by mouth.

Parenteral

IV, IO, IM, intracerebral, intrathecal, SQ, intradermal, intra-articular, intravitreal, intraperitoneal. The lecture summarizes this as invasive.

Topical

Dermal, transmucosal, and inhalational. Inhalational is classed as topical because it acts on respiratory mucosa.

RouteBioavailability in the chartGeneral characteristic
Intravenous100% by definitionMost rapid onset
Intramuscular75 to ≤100%Large volumes often feasible. May be painful.
Subcutaneous75 to ≤100%Smaller volumes than IM. May be painful.
Oral5 to <100%Most convenient. First-pass effect may be important.
Rectal30 to <100%Less first-pass effect than oral.
Inhalation5 to <100%Often very rapid onset.
Transdermal80 to <100%Usually very slow absorption. Used to reduce first-pass effect and prolong duration.
Exam traps
IV is the lecture’s gold standard and has 100% bioavailability. PO is the route where first-pass metabolism is most significant in the review question.

Recall check Routes

Which route has 100% bioavailability by definition?
Intravenous.
Why does the lecture classify inhalational administration as topical?
Because it acts on the mucosal layer of the respiratory tract.
Which route has the most important first-pass effect in the lecture review?
Oral, PO.

Should know Capsules, spansules, and suppositories Page 4

Capsules

  • Oblong casing, usually gelatin.
  • Contains a powdered or liquid medicinal form.
  • Spansules use variably coated particles to allow gradual release in the gastrointestinal tract.

Suppositories

  • Firm-base mixture for insertion into body cavities.
  • Melts or dissolves at body temperature.
  • Routes listed: rectal, vaginal, urethral.
  • Example: vaginal metronidazole for vaginal infection.

Suspension is solid particles in water. Emulsion is liquid droplets in another fluid. Decoction boils the plant. Infusion uses hot or cold water.

Should know Parenteral preparations and depot formulations Page 5
  • Liquid preparations injected through IV, IM, or SQ routes.
  • Often supplied as sterile solutions or clear fluids. Some start as powders that need a diluent such as normal saline.
  • Sterile suspensions have solid crystalline particles and may be given IM, SQ, or intra-articularly, but not IV.
  • Sustained-release fat-soluble liquids may be given IM as a depot injection. The lecture example is methoxyprogesterone, Depo-Provera.
Exam trap
A sterile suspension may be injectable, but the lecture specifically says it is not for intravenous use.

Recall check Route trade-offs

Why is oral administration not ideal for an emergency?
Its absorption may be too slow and variable.

Insulin.

Surface area of application and lipid solubility.

≠Commonly Confused Concepts

Do not confuse: Drug vs medicine

Drug: substance producing a biological effect. Medicine: preparation intended for therapy. Fast distinction: effect-producing substance vs therapeutic product.

Do not confuse: Active principle vs active ingredient vs excipient

Active principle: active part of crude natural material. Active ingredient: active part of the finished product. Excipient: inactive supporting component.

Do not confuse: Synthetic vs semi-synthetic

Synthetic: starting material not found in nature. Semi-synthetic: natural starting material chemically modified.

Do not confuse: Suspension vs emulsion

Suspension: solid particles in water. Emulsion: minute liquid droplets in another fluid.

Do not confuse: Decoction vs infusion

Decoction: boiling of plants or herbs in water. Infusion: hot or cold water used to extract active constituents.

Do not confuse: Buccal vs sublingual

Buccal: between cheek and oral mucosa. Sublingual: under the tongue, with faster absorption than traditional oral forms.

Do not confuse: Lotion vs cream vs ointment vs gel

Lotion: thicker than solution. Cream: thicker than lotion, oil–water emulsion. Ointment: greasy, occlusive, high viscosity. Gel: alcohol base and drying in the lecture.

!Exam Traps

Trap 1
An inactive excipient can still change performance. It may improve absorption, sustained release, stability, handling, or manufacture.
Trap 2
Organic nature alone does not explain selective receptor binding. Shape, charge, molecular weight, and chirality are the tested clues.
Trap 3
IV is 100% bioavailable, but “best” does not mean safest. Accidental overdose is difficult to reverse.
Trap 4
Oral is safe and convenient, not appropriate for every situation. It can be too slow for emergencies and has important first-pass metabolism.
Trap 5
Sterile suspension is not automatically suitable for IV use. The lecture allows IM, SQ, or intra-articular routes, but not IV.
Trap 6
Suppositories can bypass hepatic metabolism in the lecture’s review key, yet their absorption is also described as slow and erratic.
Trap 7
Inhalational is placed under topical routes because the preparation acts on respiratory mucosa.
Trap 8
The source’s keyed statement is “all medicines are drugs, but not all drugs are medicines.” Memorize this as the lecture’s exam answer.

5mLast-Minute Cram Sheet

Core definitions

  • Drug: substance producing biological effect.
  • Medicine: preparation intended for therapeutic effect.
  • Active principle: active constituent of crude preparation.
  • Active ingredient: active substance in finished product.
  • Excipient: inactive supporting substance.

Nature

  • Organic compounds vs inorganic elements.
  • Most drugs: MW 100–1000.
  • Selective binding: unique shape, charge, and related properties.
  • Chirality: one enantiomer may fit better and be more potent.

Sources

  • Natural: plants, animals, microorganisms, minerals.
  • Synthetic: starting materials not found in nature.
  • Semi-synthetic: natural starting material chemically modified.
  • Radiopharmaceutical: radioactive substance for diagnosis or treatment.

Selection

PK + PD + age + sex + disease severity and duration + treatment response + comorbidities and excipient allergy.

Routes

  • IV: 100% bioavailability, rapid, precise, difficult to reverse.
  • Oral: safest and convenient, first-pass effect, variable and slow.
  • Rectal: useful in vomiting or unconscious patients, slow and erratic.
  • Inhalational: rapid, no first pass, difficult dose regulation.
  • Topical: local action, absorption depends on area and lipid solubility.

Dosage forms

  • Buccal = cheek. Sublingual = under tongue.
  • Suspension = solid in liquid, shake.
  • Emulsion = liquid droplets in liquid.
  • Decoction = boil. Infusion = hot or cold water extraction.
  • Ointment = greasy and occlusive. Gel = alcohol-based and drying in the lecture.

Classic traps

  • Inactive excipient does not mean unimportant.
  • Organic composition alone does not produce selective binding.
  • Injectable suspension is not for IV use.
  • The lecture answer key: all medicines are drugs, but not all drugs are medicines.
