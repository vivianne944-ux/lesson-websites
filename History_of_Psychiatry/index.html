Major shifts in thinking

  • Supernatural explanations gradually gave way to anatomical, phenomenologic, psychosocial, and biologic approaches.
  • Custodial asylum care shifted toward humanitarian treatment, then toward community-based care.
  • Variable institutional diagnoses led to efforts to improve diagnostic reliability through the DSM series.
  • Childhood and social explanations were later joined by strong evidence for inherited and biologic components.

Tab 2 · Lesson proper begins here

Before the Modern Era

Early psychiatry included distinct medical recognition in India, supernatural interpretations in Greece, psychiatric wards in Arab hospitals, and custodial institutions in medieval Europe.

Page 1

Ancient India

  • Ancient India was among the earliest civilizations to recognize and practice psychiatry as a distinct medical discipline.
  • The lecture emphasizes that Asian civilizations, especially India, made major early contributions even though Ancient Greece is often credited as the origin of Western medicine.
  • Charaka Samhita, also called the Compendium of Charaka, was a pre-2nd century CE medical treatise with psychiatric sections.
  • Psychiatric hospitals existed in Ancient India.
Page 1

Ancient Greece

The Greeks viewed psychiatric illness as a supernatural disorder. The lecture uses the scene of Salmoneus on a mixing bowl as a visual representation of madness and loss of control.

  • Galen and Plato stressed a categorical approach to disease.
  • Galen focused on the disease itself.
  • Hippocrates used a more dimensional approach, seeing traits on a spectrum and emphasizing treatment of the individual.
  • Hippocrates introduced the idea that mental disorders may lie on a continuum with normal functioning.
Do not confuse Categorical means disease is treated as a distinct category. Dimensional means traits and symptoms are placed on a spectrum that can continue into normal functioning.
Page 1

Middle Ages in Arab countries

  • Arab countries included psychiatric wards in their hospitals.
  • The first psychiatric ward was built in Baghdad, Iraq around the early 9th century.
  • Islamic medical scholars wrote about treatments for different mental disorders.
  • Abu Ali Al-Hussein, known in the West as Avicenna, was among these scholars.
Page 1

Medieval Europe

  • Psychiatric hospitals appeared around the 13th century.
  • Their primary role was custodial care rather than treatment.
  • Bethlem Royal Hospital, also called Bethlem or Bedlam, was founded in 1247 to collect alms for the needy.
  • By the late 14th century it began caring for the insane, with documentation of mentally ill inmates by the early 15th century.
  • By the late 15th century it had shifted from a general hospital to an asylum for the insane.
High-yield recap India shows early psychiatric medicine. Greece frames the categorical versus dimensional debate. Baghdad marks the first psychiatric ward. Bethlem represents the move toward custodial asylum care.

Tab 3 · From asylum growth to humanitarian reform

Early Modern Age

Private lunatic asylums expanded in the late 17th century. The Age of Enlightenment then drove criticism of abusive care and promoted moral treatment.

Pages 1 to 2

Late 17th century

  • Lunatic asylums proliferated, with most under private management.
  • Bethel Hospital Norwich was the first building constructed specifically as an asylum for the insane.
  • It was founded by Mary Chapman.
  • In France, Louis XIV created the first public mental health system in Europe, although treatment received little emphasis.
  • Anatomy of Melancholy by Robert Burton was one of the earliest Western treatises on mental illness.
  • Burton discussed melancholy, including depressive illnesses, through a broader perspective on the human experience.
Lecture-specific note The growth of institutions did not automatically mean the growth of treatment. The lecture repeatedly separates housing or custody from genuine therapeutic care.
Page 2

Age of Enlightenment and moral treatment

  • Medieval care was intended to be compassionate, yet treatment was often barbaric.
  • Common practices included prolonged physical restraint and physical punishment.
  • The Age of Enlightenment brought a movement toward humanitarian reform.
  • William Battie, in Treatise on Madness, criticized treatment at hospitals such as Bethlem and argued for good hygiene and nutrition.

Major centers of moral treatment

  • The York Retreat in England, founded by William Tuke.
  • Bicêtre Hospital and Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital in France, led by Dr. Philippe Pinel.

Philippe Pinel expanded moral treatment and introduced a more scientific approach to psychiatry.

  • He was renowned for removing chains from patients with mental illness.
  • He was among the first to categorize psychiatric treatments comprehensively.
  • He focused on phenomenology rather than pathophysiology.
Key differentiator Phenomenology organizes what is observed in symptoms and clinical presentation. The lecture contrasts this with pathophysiology, which concerns the underlying disease process.

Tab 4 · Scientific concepts and major theorists

19th and Early 20th Century

Public oversight developed alongside scientific descriptions of psychosis, bipolar illness, and catatonia. Kraepelin, Bleuler, and Freud then shaped major competing frameworks.

1808
Country Asylums Act in Britain established public mental asylums.
1828
Commissioners in Lunacy were appointed to oversee and license private asylums after concerns about abuses in Bethlem and similar institutions.
1852
Benedict Morel reported a case of schizophrenic psychosis and used Démence précoce for deteriorated patients whose illness began in adolescence.
1854
Jules Falret described bipolar disorder as Folie Circulaire or circular insanity.
1867 to 1874
Likely schizophrenia was subsequently described by Griesinger in 1867, Hecker in 1871, and Kahlbaum in 1874.
1874
Kahlbaum produced the first medical case report of catatonia.
1882
Karl Kahlbaum used cyclothymia to describe mania and depression as stages of the same illness.
Context During much of the 19th and early 20th century in Europe, psychiatric disorders were largely believed to arise from congenital or degenerative diseases.
Page 2

Emil Kraepelin

  • Pioneered the scientific classification of psychiatric disease.
  • Declared that all psychiatric disorders were biologic.
  • Because etiologic classification was not feasible, he classified through detailed study of phenomenology and symptom similarities and differences.
  • Translated Démence précoce into dementia praecox, emphasizing early onset and cognitive decline.
  • Described a long-term deteriorating course with hallucinations and delusions.
Page 2

Eugene Bleuler

  • Coined schizophrenia, replacing dementia praecox in the literature.
  • Schizo means split and phren means mind.
  • The term referred to schisms among thought, emotion, and behavior.
  • It does not mean split personality, which the lecture associates more accurately with dissociative disorders.
  • His fundamental symptoms were the four As: associations, affect, autism, and ambivalence.
  • Secondary symptoms included hallucinations and delusions.
Page 2

Sigmund Freud

  • Initially sought a biological explanation for mental illness.
  • Shifted to a psychoanalytic approach because scientific tools were inadequate.
  • Developed the approach from work with private patients.
  • Sought unconscious meanings behind thoughts, behaviors, and desires.
  • Explained illness through conflicts and drives, especially sexual drives and early life experiences.
  • His work became influential in the mid 20th century.
If you see X, think YDementia praecox means Kraepelin. Schizophrenia and four As mean Bleuler. Unconscious conflict and drives mean Freud.
Exam trapBleuler’s split mind refers to schisms among mental functions, not a split personality.

Tab 5 · Changing patients and changing explanations

Latter 20th Century Models

Biologic treatment discoveries weakened the dominance of psychoanalysis. World War II also expanded psychiatry beyond the asylum and increased attention to environment, adaptation, and social context.

Page 2

Shift around World War II

  • Effective biologic treatments led many psychiatrists to become disillusioned with psychoanalysis because of its lack of empirical data and resistance to scientific testing.
  • Psychoanalysis gradually lost prominence as the primary method for understanding and treating psychiatric disorders.
  • Psychiatrists moved out of asylums and encountered patients who were described as people like us rather than only the insane.
  • Military work included treatment of combat trauma and evaluation of individuals who were psychologically unfit for service.

Adolph Meyer and psychobiology

  • Meyer increased the influence of the environment in explanations of mental illness.
  • He was the founder of psychobiology.
  • He viewed mental disorders as reactions to life stresses.
  • These disorders were maladaptations that could be understood through the patient’s life experiences.

Psychosocial model

  • Built on Meyer’s social perspective and Freud’s emphasis on early life experiences.
  • Developed by Walter Meninger and colleagues.
  • Depression was viewed as a failure to adapt rather than a neurologic illness.
  • The model proposed one basic psychosocial process: the suffering individual fails to adapt to the environment.
Cause and effect chain Life stress → unsuccessful adaptation → maladaptation → psychiatric symptoms understood through the patient’s experiences and environment.

Gradual return toward biologic models

The lecture describes a gradual shift from psychoanalytic and sociological perspectives toward a biological understanding. The next tab follows the discoveries that supported this change.

Tab 6 · Neurotransmitters, procedures, medications, imaging, genetics

Biologic Discoveries

Biological psychiatry grew through physiologic experiments, circuit models, electroconvulsive therapy, unexpected drug discoveries, neuroimaging, and genetic research.

Page 2

1921 · Acetylcholine

  • Otto Loewi used saline from one frog heart to influence the heart rate of another denervated heart.
  • The experiment established the role of neurotransmitters in physiologic responses.
  • It demonstrated that acetylcholine could control heart rate independently of electrical stimulation.
Page 2

1930s to 1940s · Circuits and ECT

  • The Papez circuit connected emotion with the limbic lobe, amygdala, septal region, and hippocampus.
  • Electroconvulsive therapy was developed in the 1930s.
  • In the 1940s, ECT began to be used for depression.
  • The lecture states that ECT remains an option for treatment-resistant depression.

Neuroimaging

  • Became an investigatory tool in the 1980s.
  • Johnstone and colleagues found enlarged cerebral ventricles in patients with schizophrenia.
  • Nancy Andreasen conducted the first MRI studies of schizophrenia and developed many later research techniques.
Genetic research The lecture reports high heritability of schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, and bipolar disorder. Specific links remained unclear, but the findings supported drug and imaging research and strengthened recognition of a major inherited component.

Tab 7 · Where care happens and how disorders are classified

Community Care and the DSM

The 20th century shifted care from institutions to the community. At the same time, the DSM series aimed to improve diagnostic consistency and reliability.

Page 3

Deinstitutionalization

  • Psychiatric treatment shifted from asylums to offices and community settings.
  • Psychoanalytic and psychosocial movements encouraged the belief that many psychiatric conditions could be treated outside institutions.
  • In 1963, President Kennedy introduced legislation that delegated the NIH to administer community mental health centers.
  • The goal was to move patients from institutions into community care.

Success and shortcomings

  • The community movement was described as successful in transitioning care.
  • Community centers often had poor funding and support.
  • Consequences included homelessness, dangerous living conditions, and incarceration.
  • Many patients continued to receive inadequate treatment.
Appraisal point Deinstitutionalization changed the location of care, but the lecture emphasizes that community placement without adequate funding and treatment created major gaps.
Page 3

Why the DSM series developed

  • Before the 1960s, institutions used varied classification systems, producing inconsistent diagnoses.
  • DSM-I criteria also led to inconsistent results.
  • The 1970s Carter Commission called for better understanding of mental disorders.
  • The DSM series developed to improve diagnostic reliability.

DSM-III and later work

  • DSM-III, introduced in 1980, shifted to a more symptomatic and symptom-based approach.
  • It removed dichotomies such as endogenous versus exogenous depression.
  • Its goal was clearer and more reliable diagnosis.
  • Work on DSM-5 began in 2000 with groups developing a research agenda for the fifth major revision.

DSM-5-TR details stated in the lecture

  • The lecture states that DSM-5-TR is being used.
  • Development began in 2019 and involved more than 200 experts.
  • Four crosscutting review groups focused on culture, sex and gender, suicide, and forensic issues.
  • An ethnoracial equity and inclusion work group addressed racism-related risk factors and non-stigmatizing language.
  • No major conceptual changes were made. Clarifications to criteria were reviewed and approved by the DSM Steering Committee, APA Assembly, and Board of Trustees.
  • DSM-5-TR was published in March 2022.

Tab 8 · Evolution of Philippine psychiatry

Philippine Psychiatry

Philippine psychiatry moved from supernatural explanations and religious or folk interventions toward medical recognition, dedicated institutions, workforce training, and academic departments.

Pre-Spanish era
Information was limited. Mental and other illnesses were attributed to natural and supernatural occurrences.
Spanish era · 1521 to 1898
Aberrant thinking and behavior were attributed to religious factors and supernatural forces. Explanations included offended deities, witches or mangkukulam, and devilmen or mangangaway. Patients were brought to churches for purification or exorcism, or to albularyos who used physically and psychologically distressing practices.
American regime · 1898 to 1946
Mental disability began to be recognized like other medical illness, with advocacy for a more humane approach.
November 1904
The Insane Department of San Lazaro Hospital became the country’s first hospital unit dedicated to people with mental illness. Elias Domingo, the first Filipino psychiatrist, later headed it after psychiatric training in Pennsylvania as a Rockefeller scholar.
1918
The City Sanitarium was established at San Juan del Monte.
December 18, 1928
The Insular Psychopathic Hospital, later renamed the National Psychopathic Hospital, formally opened as the first hospital exclusively dedicated to patients with mental and nervous disorders.
Japanese occupation · December 1941
World War II halted psychiatric progress. Electroconvulsive therapy became the foremost treatment, using antiquated Japanese apparatus followed by local medicinal herbs.
Pages 3 to 4

Era of Liberation

The period brought expansion and rehabilitation of psychiatric facilities together with workforce training.

YearMilestone
1945University of the Philippines College of Medicine began teaching psychiatry as a subject.
1946Victoriano Luna General Hospital established the Philippines’ first 100-bed neuropsychiatric unit. In July, the National Psychopathic Hospital was renamed the National Mental Hospital.
1947Major Romeo Gustilo performed the first prefrontal lobotomy using an improvised leucotome on a violent patient with schizophrenia who had not responded to ECT and other available treatments. The University of Santo Tomas also structured a Section of Neurology and Psychiatry under the Department of Medicine.
December 1949Australia-trained Major Jaime Zaguirre performed the first trans-orbital lobotomy on a patient with schizophrenia.
1956The Department of Psychiatry was established at the University of the East Ramon Magsaysay Medical Center under the chairmanship of Jaime Zaguirre.

Tab 9 · Looking to the future

Future Directions

The lecture presents rapid progress in imaging, genetics, and data analysis, while emphasizing unresolved problems in biomarkers, translation, animal models, and treatment development.

Research gains

  • High-resolution functional imaging, total gene mapping, and data analysis have strengthened research.
  • Disorders highlighted include schizophrenia, autism, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and major depression.
  • These advances improve understanding of genetics and pathophysiology.

Persistent gap

  • A gap remains between basic research and clinical research.
  • Clinical diagnoses often fall short for research needs.
  • Attempts to identify valid biomarkers have been largely unsuccessful.
  • Endophenotypes may be important, but their predictive power remains unproven.

Translational barriers and new targets

  • Many psychiatric disorders lack a valid animal model.
  • Researchers are trying to improve animal testing methods.
  • New approaches explore multi-target psychopharmacology.
  • The lecture uses the term dirty drugs for agents that affect multiple receptors.
  • Research is moving beyond traditional monoamine targets to glutamatergic and G-protein coupled receptors.

Clinical direction

  • Psychiatric disorders are complex and require multifaceted approaches.
  • Management includes both psychosocial and pharmacologic treatments.
  • Viewing disorders as neurodevelopmental rather than neurodegenerative emphasizes early identification and intervention.
  • Earlier intervention may improve outcomes at earlier stages.
Main future message Better technology does not remove the need for integrated care. The lecture closes by pairing biologic discovery with psychosocial treatment and early intervention.
