Rational Drug Therapy

Rational prescribing means making a treatment decision that is clinically justified, individualized, practical, and economical—not merely choosing a familiar medicine.

Why prescribing is an “art”

The lecture describes prescribing as an art because it requires several abilities to work together:

  • Diagnostic skills
  • Knowledge of medicines
  • Understanding of clinical pharmacology principles
  • Communication skills
  • Appreciation of a drug’s risks and benefits for a particular patient

A drug can be pharmacologically active yet still be a poor choice if the diagnosis, patient factors, communication, or risk–benefit balance is wrong. p. 1

Why good prescribing matters

  • More than 50% of medicines are prescribed, dispensed, or sold inappropriately.
  • 50% of patients fail to take medicines correctly.
  • About one-third of the world’s population lacks access to essential medicines.
Meaning
The problem is not only selecting the wrong drug. Rational therapy also addresses access, correct use, affordability, and adherence.
p. 1

Definition: the “right” treatment has several parts

“The rational use of drugs requires that patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their own individual requirements for an adequate period of time, and the lowest cost to them and their community.”WHO definition stated in the lecture, 1952 p. 1

The revised version condenses the same idea into five linked requirements:

1 · Drug
Right drug for the clinical problem.
2 · Dose
Adequate dose for the individual patient.
3 · Duration
Sufficient duration to meet the objective.
4 · Context
Appropriate to the patient’s clinical needs at the lowest cost.
Do not reduce rational prescribing to one question
“Does the drug work?” is only part of the decision. The lecture also requires the correct dose, duration, patient fit, safety, communication, monitoring, and cost.

Lock It In

Core conceptAppropriate medicine for the patient’s clinical need.
Key differentiatorIndividualized dose and duration—not a one-size-fits-all order.
SequenceAssess → choose → prescribe → counsel → monitor.
Exam cue“Lowest cost” belongs to the definition, but not at the expense of appropriateness.

Rational or Ethical Prescribing

Ethical prescribing asks not only, “Can this medicine help?” but also, “Is this the most responsible way to help this patient?”

GOAL 1

Maximize effectiveness

Choose treatment likely to achieve the intended clinical objective.

GOAL 2

Minimize harms

Consider adverse effects, contraindications, interactions, and patient vulnerability.

GOAL 3

Avoid waste

Do not consume scarce healthcare materials without a justified benefit.

GOAL 4

Respect choice

Give the patient enough information to understand and participate in the plan.

Each step protects the next. A correct drug choice can still become unsafe if the order is unclear or the patient is not counseled. p. 1

Prescription orders must communicate clearly

All orders should be:

  • Unambiguous
  • Dated
  • Timed for chart orders
  • Signed clearly
Why it matters
The prescription is a communication tool between the prescriber, pharmacist, and nurse. Ambiguity can convert a correct clinical decision into a medication error.
p. 1

Prescribing Errors, Adherence, and Context

This section explains why treatment can fail even when a medicine exists: information may be omitted, the order may be misread, the drug may not fit the patient, or the patient may not use it as intended.

Prescribing errors

Omission of information
“Continue present IVF” does not state the exact intravenous fluid. “Resume preoperative meds” assumes a complete and accurate medication record is available.
Poor prescription writing
Illegible handwriting or an ambiguous decimal can change a dose: 0.1 may be read as 1, or 1.0 as 10.
Inappropriate prescription
May result from missed contraindications, incomplete medication history, or failure to identify physicochemical incompatibilities.

The lecture examples include insulin preparations that should not be mixed and simultaneous administration of antacids or products described in the trans as having high “methyl content,” which may compromise intestinal absorption of drugs such as tetracycline. This wording is retained from the source. p. 1

Four major patterns of noncompliance or nonadherence

PatternWhat happensExamples in the lecture
1. Medication is not obtainedThe treatment never begins.Patient fails to obtain the medication.
2. Medication is not taken as prescribedThe patient takes it incorrectly.Wrong dose, frequency, timing or sequence, route, or purpose.
3. Medication is stopped earlyTreatment ends before the intended duration.Symptoms improve, or the bottle becomes empty and the patient assumes treatment is finished.
4. Medication is used inappropriatelyThe medicine is used outside the intended plan.Patients may share medications.
Key connection
This is why counseling is part of prescribing. The written order alone cannot prevent wrong timing, premature discontinuation, or sharing.
p. 1

Legal and regulatory factors

  • OTC drugs: described as safe for lay use in self-limiting conditions.
  • Prescription drugs: require a licensed prescriber; controlled substances listed include opioids, hallucinogens, stimulants, depressants, and anabolic steroids.
  • The lecture notes that physicians need an S-2 license before prescribing regulated or controlled drugs.
  • Government controls labeling and distribution; licensing boards control who may prescribe.
  • The Philippine National Formulary affects government purchasing and PhilHealth reimbursement as stated in the lecture.
p. 1

Drug safety and labeled use

  • The FDA monitors drug safety through mandatory reporting by drug companies and voluntary reporting by healthcare practitioners.
  • Labeled indications may not include every condition in which a drug might be useful.
  • Off-label use is described as use for an unapproved disease or condition, dosage form, or dose.
Lecture-specific wording
The source contains a brief example involving statins and malpractice that is not fully explained. No additional interpretation is added here.
p. 1

Socioeconomic factors and generic prescribing

The lecture emphasizes that physicians must prescribe the generic name, allowing the pharmacist to supply a generically equivalent and less expensive product. The prescriber may also indicate a brand.

Cost-related considerations

  • Price differences may be paid by the patient.
  • FDA-provided bioequivalence and price information should be available.
  • Other costs include the prescription fee, quantity dispensed, and refill frequency.
  • Cost should refer to the whole treatment duration, not just the price of one tablet or vial.

Problems listed for some generic products

  • High ratio of inert ingredient to active drug content
  • Poor solubility
  • Low therapeutic index
pp. 1–2
RA 6675 and DOH issuances
The trans states that physicians are not allowed to block substitution through instructions such as “Dispense only as Prescribed” or “No Substitution.” It cites the Generics Act of 1988, DOH Administrative Order No. 169 (2004), and DOH Administrative Order No. 62 (1989). The source also explicitly notes that this portion was not discussed during the lecture and was corrected or supplemented by the trans team. p. 2

ESSC: How to Compare Drug Options

ESSC separates the decision into four questions. This prevents one strong feature—such as efficacy or low price—from hiding an important weakness in another area.

E

Efficacy

Can the drug produce the intended effect, and what pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic features shape that effect?

S

Safety

What adverse effects can occur, how often, how seriously, and how predictably?

S

Suitability

Does this drug fit this particular patient, their comorbidities, other drugs, likely adherence, dosage form, and local availability?

C

Cost

What is the total cost for the full treatment, and are similarly effective alternatives available?

1. Efficacy

Efficacy is described as effectiveness in relieving symptoms through the drug’s mechanism of action. The lecture asks the prescriber to examine both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties.

PropertyWhat to examineWhy it affects the choice
Onset and durationHow quickly the effect begins and how long it lasts.May influence adherence and whether the drug matches the needed timing.
BioavailabilityConsistency of drug availability.More consistent bioavailability makes the response more predictable.
Tissue distributionWhere the drug reaches.May produce adverse effects at sites other than the target.
Metabolism and excretionHow the drug is handled and removed.Helps anticipate variable responses.
Specificity and selectivityHow narrowly the drug acts on its target.Influences the likelihood of adverse effects.
Dose-responseHow response changes with dose.Influences ease of dose titration.
Therapeutic indexRelationship between useful and harmful doses.Influences ease of dose selection.
Spectrum of activityMicrobial organisms affected, when evaluating antibiotics.Determines whether antimicrobial coverage matches the intended target.
p. 2

2. Safety

Safety is assessed through the adverse-effect profile:

  • Type of adverse effects
  • Frequency
  • Seriousness
  • Predictability
  • Possible toxicity
Key question
How harmful is the drug itself, and how likely are those harms?
pp. 2–3

3. Suitability

  • History of previous ADRs
  • Vulnerability: very young, old, pregnant, or with comorbidities
  • Current drug therapy and possible interactions
  • Likely adherence: dosing frequency, taste, and form
  • Appropriate dose and dosage form for pediatric or geriatric patients
  • Local availability and practicality
Key question
Even if the drug is generally acceptable, does it fit this patient?
pp. 2–3

4. Cost

Cost is the total cost of the entire treatment. The lecture instructs the learner to begin with the retail price per tablet or vial, then calculate the amount required for the full duration. Lower total price receives a higher rating. Alternatives with similar efficacy should also be considered. p. 2

Do not confuse Safety and Suitability
Safety describes the drug’s adverse-effect and toxicity profile. Suitability describes whether the drug fits a specific patient. A pregnancy restriction or age limitation belongs under Suitability, not automatically under Safety. p. 3

Lock It In

Core conceptESSC prevents one-dimensional drug selection.
Key differentiatorSafety = drug harms; Suitability = patient fit.
SequenceAssess E, then S, then S, then C for each option.
Exam cueCost means total treatment cost, not price per unit alone.

How ESSC Scoring Works

The score is a comparison tool. Its value comes from keeping each column conceptually clean and comparing options consistently.

Scoring rules

  1. List the candidate drug groups.
  2. Score each criterion from + to ++++.
  3. Compare options vertically under one criterion at a time.
  4. For small-group discussions, present data column by column rather than revealing the full completed grid at once.
  5. Select the highest-scoring drug group.
  6. List specific drugs within that group and repeat the ESSC comparison.
p. 3
Column discipline
Scores must reflect only the criterion in that column. Do not lower Efficacy because of adverse effects; place those under Safety. Do not lower Safety solely because the drug is contraindicated in pregnancy; that patient-specific restriction belongs under Suitability. p. 3

Counseling the Patient

Counseling converts a prescription into a usable treatment plan. The patient needs to know what the medicine is for, how to use it, what to expect, when to worry, and when to return.

Effects of the drug

  • Why the drug is needed
  • Which symptoms will disappear and which will not
  • When the effect is expected to start
  • What may happen if it is taken incorrectly or not taken at all
Why it matters
Expectation-setting helps the patient judge whether treatment is working without assuming every symptom should disappear immediately.

Side effects

  • Which side effects may occur
  • How to recognize them
  • How long they may continue
  • How serious they are
  • What action to take if they happen
Why it matters
Naming a side effect is incomplete unless the patient also knows its seriousness and the appropriate response.

Instructions

  • How and when to take the drug
  • How long treatment should continue
  • How to store it
  • What to do with leftover drugs

Warnings

  • When the drug should not be taken
  • Maximum dose
  • Why the full treatment course should be completed

Future consultations

  • When to return—or when routine return is unnecessary
  • When to come back earlier
  • What information to bring to the next appointment

The lecture example says a hypertensive patient may be expected to bring a record of blood-pressure measurements.

Confirm understanding

  • Ask whether everything is understood.
  • Ask the patient to repeat the most important information.
  • Ask whether there are more questions.
Clinical meaning
The patient repeating key instructions tests whether communication was successful; it does not merely ask for a yes/no response.
Counseling sequence
Effect → side effects → instructions → warnings → follow-up → confirm clarity. This order follows the lecture’s categories. p. 5

Lock It In

Core conceptCounseling is part of prescribing, not an optional add-on.
Key differentiatorExplain both what to expect and what action to take.
SequenceEffects → harms → use → warnings → return → teach-back.
Exam cueAsk the patient to repeat the most important information.

Common exam traps
  • Safety ≠ Suitability.
  • Do not pick a drug before defining the diagnosis and objective.
  • Do not assume all conditions require drug treatment.
  • Do not compare cost using one tablet alone; use the whole course.
  • A correct drug choice can still fail through unclear writing, poor counseling, or nonadherence.
