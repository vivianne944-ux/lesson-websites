The Big Picture

Bacterial genetics explains how bacteria store information, copy it, express it, and change or exchange it. The lecture moves from the structure of DNA and RNA to the mechanisms that generate bacterial diversity.

PDF pp. 1–8
Central Question

How can bacteria preserve essential genetic information while still gaining new traits that support survival, including traits such as antibiotic resistance?

1. BuildNucleotides form DNA and RNA.
2. StoreChromosomes carry essential genes; plasmids and transposons add mobile information.
3. CopyReplication produces two identical chromosomes.
4. ExpressTranscription and translation turn genetic information into RNA and proteins.
5. DiversifyMutation, recombination, transformation, transduction, and conjugation alter genetic makeup.
How the topics connect

Information flows, then diversity modifies the flow

DNA structure provides a stable information system. Replication preserves that information across daughter cells. Transcription and translation use the stored sequence to produce RNA and proteins. Finally, mutation and genetic exchange alter which sequences are present, creating diversity that can affect phenotype and bacterial survival.

DNA structure → gene storage → replication → RNA/protein expression → mutation or exchange → altered genotype/phenotype
Major topic anchors
  • Chromosome: essential, double-stranded, closed, circular bacterial DNA.
  • Plasmid: non-chromosomal DNA that may provide an environmental advantage.
  • Transposon: mobile DNA or “jumping gene.”
  • Mutation: change in nucleotide sequence.
  • Genetic exchange: transformation, transduction, or conjugation.

Nucleic Acid Foundations

Before studying bacterial chromosomes or gene transfer, first understand the units that make DNA and RNA.

PDF pp. 1–2
What it is

Two major classes of nucleic acid

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is the most common macromolecule that encodes genetic information. The other major class is ribonucleic acid (RNA).

Nucleic acids are natural polymers. Their monomers are called nucleotides.

Build the monomer

A nucleotide has three parts

  1. 5-carbon sugar (pentose): 2-deoxyribose in DNA; ribose in RNA.
  2. Organic or nitrogenous base
  3. Phosphoric acid / phosphate group
Do Not Confuse

Nucleoside = base + sugar. Nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate.

Base families

Purines and pyrimidines

FamilyBases listed in the lectureRNA-specific point
PurinesAdenine, GuanineIn RNA, uracil pairs with adenine instead of thymine.
PyrimidinesThymine, Cytosine, Uracil
DNA molecular structure

Backbone outside, bases inside

DNA contains two strands of deoxynucleotides. The sugar and phosphate form the backbone of each strand, while the bases point inward and hold the two strands together through hydrogen bonds, forming the double helix.

The lecture also describes 3′–5′ ester bonds and a phosphodiester bond connecting the deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups.

The Key Idea

The order of the bases carries the coded genetic information.

Orientation

DNA is antiparallel

One strand runs 3′ to 5′, while the other runs 5′ to 3′. The two strands therefore appear upside down relative to each other.

This orientation is easiest to recognize by following the pentose sugars in the diagram.

RNA molecular structure

RNA is abundant and occurs in three major forms

The lecture describes RNA as a lower-molecular-weight but much more abundant nucleic acid distributed throughout the cell, especially in ribosomes.

RNA typeLecture-supported role or feature
rRNALargest subgroup, about 85–90% of RNA; a major component of ribosomes together with proteins; generally less than one-thousandth the size of DNA.
mRNAMore transient and smaller than rRNA.
tRNAMore transient and smaller than rRNA.

Key differentiatorNucleoside lacks the phosphate group.

The sequence becomes meaningful when organized into genes and stored in the bacterial genome.

A genome is all the genes in an organism.

Bacterial chromosome

Essential genetic information in a compact circular molecule

Unlike eukaryotic chromosomes, bacterial chromosomes are not found in membrane organelles. The chromosome:

  • contains genes essential for viability;
  • exists as a double-stranded, closed, circular macromolecule;
  • is extensively folded and twisted so that it fits inside the bacterial cell.
Why It Matters

The chromosome is the core genetic structure because it contains the genes the bacterium requires for viability. Non-chromosomal elements can be gained or lost, but the chromosome carries the essential set.

Do Not Confuse

Chromosome refers to the essential, main bacterial DNA molecule. Plasmids and transposons are discussed as non-chromosomal or mobile elements that may carry additional traits.

Lock It In

Key differentiatorGenome = all genes in the organism.
Key structureBacterial chromosome is double-stranded, closed, and circular.

Non-Chromosomal and Mobile Genetic Elements

Plasmids and transposons expand what bacteria can carry and share beyond the essential chromosome.

PDF pp. 3–4, 8
Plasmids · what they are

Small DNA molecules that can provide an advantage

Many bacteria contain small non-chromosomal DNA molecules called plasmids. They usually contain about 5–100 genes.

Plasmids are not essential for normal bacterial growth, so bacteria may gain or lose them without harm. Their value appears under specific environmental conditions.

Lecture Example

A plasmid may encode an enzyme that degrades an antibiotic. When that antibiotic is used against the bacterium, the plasmid-bearing bacterium can survive and grow, so the plasmid becomes an advantage.

Structure

Usually circular, but not always

  • Double-stranded, helical, non-chromosomal DNA
  • Usually forms a physical circle because the ends are covalently joined
  • Some plasmids contain linear DNA
  • Replicate independently of the host chromosome
Types and behavior

How plasmids relate to the chromosome and to other cells

TermLecture-supported meaning
EpisomeA plasmid that can insert or integrate into the host chromosome; its replication is then regulated by the chromosome.
Conjugative plasmidCan be transmitted by cell-to-cell contact and contains genes coding for proteins needed for transfer.
Self-transmissible plasmidCan transmit from one cell to another independently.
Non-transmissible plasmidCannot undergo independent transmission.
F plasmid / fertility factorConfers donor characteristics, including production of the sex pilus.

Plasmids may be transferred by transformation or generalized transduction, but the lecture identifies conjugation as the most common mechanism of plasmid transfer.

Transposons · what they are

“Jumping genes”

Transposons, also called transposable elements, are small DNA segments that encode enzymes allowing them to move from one DNA location to another, either on the same DNA molecule or a different one.

  • May be located in the bacterial nucleoid or in plasmids
  • Usually contain about 1–12 genes
  • May carry genes for antibiotic resistance or other traits
How they move

Insertion sequences and transposase

A transposon contains genes flanked by insertion sequences that code for transposase.

Transposase catalyzes cutting and resealing of DNA during transposition. This lets the transposon leave a nucleoid or plasmid and insert into another nucleoid or plasmid.

Conjugative transposon

Movement from one cell to another

1
Excision
Leaves the donor chromosome and becomes a circular transposon.
2
Contact and transfer
A nearby recipient contacts the donor, allowing transmission.
3
Copying
The recipient produces a copy of the transposon.
4
Integration
The transposon integrates into the recipient chromosome.

What this flow means: a resistance trait can move first within DNA and then between cells, helping spread the trait through a bacterial population.

Replication and Expression of Genetic Information

Replication preserves the DNA sequence; transcription and translation use that sequence to produce RNA and proteins.

PDF pp. 4–5
Replication

Copying the bacterial chromosome

Replication is a complex process mediated by enzymes such as DNA polymerase. It must happen quickly and accurately.

01
Unwinding
The supercoiled DNA is unwound.
02
Separation
Complementary parental strands separate so each can serve as a template.
03
Synthesis
New daughter DNA strands are formed.
04
Termination
Two identical chromosomes are released, one for each daughter cell.

What this flow means: each original strand provides the pattern for making a new complementary strand, ending with two identical chromosomes.

Gene expression

Processing encoded information

Gene expression is the processing of information encoded in chromosomes, plasmids, and transposons, resulting in production of RNA molecules and proteins.

Gene expression = transcription → translation
Bacterial feature

Transcription and translation are tightly coupled

In bacteria, transcription and translation are described as tightly coupled, making it impossible to disengage the two processes.

Transcription

DNA sequence becomes RNA

Gene expression begins with transcription. The DNA base sequence of a gene is converted into an mRNA molecule complementary to the gene’s DNA sequence.

The lecture states that usually one DNA strand—the sense strand—encodes a functional product and serves as the template for mRNA synthesis.

Transcription produces mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA.

RNA polymerase

The central transcription enzyme

RNA polymerase unwinds the DNA and builds a growing mRNA strand by placing the base complementary to the base on the DNA template strand.

Cause and Effect

DNA base order determines the complementary RNA base order; that RNA sequence then affects which amino acids are incorporated during translation.

Translation

Protein synthesis in three stages

1
Initiation
mRNA, translational components, and a tRNA carrying the first amino acid assemble at the start codon AUG.
2
Elongation
tRNAs bring amino acids to mRNA and add them one by one to the growing polypeptide.
3
Termination
A stop codon UAG is recognized by a protein release factor, and the completed peptide is released.

What this flow means: translation starts at a start codon, lengthens the polypeptide one amino acid at a time, then stops when a release factor recognizes the stop codon.

Lecture-Specific Note

The figure labels the pathway as the Central Dogma, while the accompanying lecture text calls it the “natural dogma.” The lecture-supported sequence is DNA → RNA → protein.

Mutation

A mutation changes the DNA sequence. Because DNA determines RNA and protein sequence, a mutation can change phenotype—but not every mutation is expressed or even retained.

PDF pp. 5–6
Definition

A change in genotype

A mutation is an alteration in the original nucleotide sequence of one or more genes in the genome. It may affect one base, an entire gene, or several genes.

It is therefore a change in the organism’s genotype.

How it arises

Spontaneous or induced

  • Spontaneous: an error during DNA replication.
  • Induced: caused by chemical or physical mutagens in the environment.
  • Biologic factor: introduction of foreign DNA into the cell.
Mechanism to consequence

Why a DNA change can alter the organism

DNA base sequence changes
mRNA base sequence may change
Different amino acid may be incorporated
Protein type or function may change
Physiology or phenotype may change

What this flow means: the mutation’s effect depends on whether the altered DNA changes the RNA and protein that are produced.

Possible outcomes

Not all mutations behave the same way

OutcomeLecture-supported meaning
LethalThe organism dies; the mutation is lost with it.
SilentThe mutation exists in the gene but is not detected in the phenotype.
Phenotypic alteration with survival advantageThe changed trait may help survival.
Stable non-lethal mutationPassed from one generation to another as part of the genotype; may also be transferred to other bacteria.
Lost mutationMay disappear through cellular repair or spontaneously during later replication cycles.
Substitution

One nucleotide replaces another

A substitution mutation replaces one nucleotide with another. It may not alter the amino acid sequence because several codons can code for the same amino acid.

Lecture Example

Changing DNA GAA to GAG changes mRNA CUU to CUC, but both code for leucine, so the polypeptide remains unaltered.

Insertion

One or more nucleotides are added

An insertion mutation adds DNA where it does not belong. It may simply add amino acids, or it may alter the entire reading frame.

A single-base insertion can create a frameshift. Insertions may be described as microinsertions or macroinsertions.

Deletion

One or more nucleotides are removed

A deletion mutation removes nucleotide material. A deletion of three bases usually affects only one or two amino acids, while deletion of one or two bases disrupts downstream codons and shifts the remaining reading frame.

Deletions may range from one nucleotide to an entire chromosome segment and may be microdeletions or macrodeletions.

Duplication

A DNA segment is copied

A duplication mutation produces one or more additional copies of a DNA segment. The duplicated segment may be only a few bases or a major chromosomal region.

Frameshift

Insertion or deletion not divisible by three

A frameshift mutation occurs when nucleotides are inserted or deleted in numbers that are not multiples of three. Because the code is read in three-base triplet codons, the normal reading frame is disrupted.

  • The entire sequence after the mutation may be read incorrectly.
  • Wrong amino acids may be added.
  • A premature stop codon may be created.
  • The result may be a very short polypeptide or an entirely different protein.
Understand It Better: mutation categories shown in the lecture figures

The figures also list broader structural changes: deletion, duplication, inversion, insertion, and translocation. The lecture does not further explain inversion or translocation in the text, so this reviewer preserves them as figure-listed categories without adding outside mechanisms.

Genetic Recombination and Horizontal Gene Transfer

Asexual reproduction rapidly produces nearly identical offspring. Recombination and horizontal transfer create diversity without requiring sexual reproduction.

PDF pp. 6–7
Vertical gene transfer

Parent to offspring

During asexual reproduction, prokaryotes transfer a nearly identical copy of their genetic material to offspring. This is vertical gene transfer.

It produces offspring quickly, but the benefit of diversity among offspring is limited.

Horizontal gene transfer

Organism to organism in the same generation

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) introduces genetic material from one organism into another within the same generation. It is more prevalent in prokaryotes and is an important source of genetic diversity.

It can allow even distantly related species to share genes and change phenotype. The lecture notes that only a small fraction of the prokaryotic genome may be transferred at one time.

Homologous recombination

Donor DNA replaces or integrates with recipient DNA

Donor DNA enters
A DNA segment originates from a donor bacterial cell.
Recipient receives it
The segment enters a second bacterial cell.
Exchange occurs
Donor DNA recombines with the recipient genome.
Genotype changes
A genetically transformed cell is produced.

The lecture example describes DNA fragments from lysed donor cells being taken up by recipient cells, then integrated into the recipient chromosome through recombination.

Key Connection

Acquisition of foreign DNA provides the material; recombination is the process that can integrate that material into the recipient genome.

Transformation

Transformation is the uptake of free DNA from the environment by a living recipient bacterium.

PDF p. 7
What It Is

Transformation involves recipient-cell uptake of naked or free DNA released when a donor bacterium dies and undergoes lysis.

Step-by-step

From a lysed donor to a transformed recipient

1
Donor lysis
A bacterium dies and releases DNA fragments.
2
Uptake
A nearby living, competent recipient takes up free DNA.
3
Recombination
The incoming fragment recombines with the host chromosome.
4
Transformation
The recipient becomes genetically altered.

What this flow means: the DNA itself is exposed in the environment; no phage and no direct donor-recipient contact are required.

Competence

Only certain bacteria readily take up DNA

Competent bacteria have the characteristic needed to undergo transformation.

The lecture commonly associates competence with Haemophilus, Streptococcus, and Neisseria.

Consequences

Why transformation matters

Transformation and recombination contribute to:

  • development of antibiotic resistance;
  • dissemination of genes that encode factors essential for causing disease;
  • enhancement of virulence.
Important Scope

Gene exchange by transformation is not limited to the same species, allowing important characteristics to spread to a broader variety of bacteria.

Exam Trap

Because transformation depends on naked extracellular DNA, the lecture’s review question identifies transformation as the process most likely to be inhibited by DNase.

Lock It In

SourceDNA from a lysed donor.
CarrierNone; DNA is naked/free.
Recipient requirementCompetence.
Exam cueDNase inhibits free-DNA uptake.

Transduction

Transduction uses a bacterial virus—a bacteriophage—to carry donor DNA into a recipient bacterium.

What It Is

Transduction brings DNA from two bacteria together in one cell through a bacteriophage, allowing recombination to occur.

The bacteriophage injects DNA into the donor cell.
2

What this flow means: the phage acts as the delivery vehicle, while recombination stabilizes donor DNA in the recipient.

Understand It Better: the supplementary-video explanation

The lecture’s supplementary text emphasizes that phages use bacterial cell components to generate new phage particles. In some cases the host is killed; in others, viral DNA becomes incorporated into bacterial DNA. When new phages emerge, some may carry bacterial DNA and create new bacterial genetic types after infecting another cell. This bacteriophage-assisted recombination is transduction.

Do Not Confuse

Transformation uses free DNA from the environment. Transduction uses a bacteriophage. Both may end in recombination, but the delivery route is different.

Conjugation

Conjugation transfers genetic material through direct contact between two living bacterial cells.

PDF pp. 3, 8
What It Is

Conjugation occurs between two living cells, requires cell-to-cell contact, and mobilizes plasmid or chromosomal DNA from a donor bacterium.

Contact structure

Sex pilus

In Escherichia coli, contact is mediated by the sex pilus. The F plasmid or fertility factor gives the bacterial cell donor characteristics, including pilus production.

Why it matters

Efficient spread of resistance

Conjugative plasmids transfer themselves between bacteria. The lecture connects this process to the spread of antibiotic resistance among pathogenic bacteria.

Extrachromosomal transfer

Plasmid conjugation

Pilus pulls cells together
A bridge or pore forms
One plasmid DNA strand enters the recipient
Each single strand becomes double-stranded
Recipient can produce a pilus

What this flow means: both donor and recipient restore complete double-stranded plasmids after one strand is transferred.

Chromosome transfer

Integrated F factor carries host DNA

  1. The independent F factor may combine with the E. coli chromosome.
  2. The cells contact through the pilus.
  3. A portion of the integrated chromosome can transfer to the recipient.
  4. When the integrated F factor transfers, it carries attached E. coli DNA, moving chromosomal markers into the new cell.
Conjugal transfer of a conjugative transposon

Excision, nicking, transfer, replication, integration

1
Excision
The integrated transposon forms a covalently closed circular intermediate.
2
Nick at oriT
A single-stranded nick is made at the origin of transfer.
3
Transfer
The nicked strand is presumed to move like plasmid DNA.
4
Replication
Single-stranded copies become double-stranded in donor and recipient.
5
Integration
The transposon integrates into both chromosomes.
Do Not Confuse

Conjugation requires two living cells and direct contact. Transformation begins with a dead, lysed donor. Transduction uses a bacteriophage.
