Reading Materials / Didactic Study Guide 2028AItem 15

Which of the following describes an “essential medicine”?

  1. Effective treatment for a select group of people in the population
  2. Benefits the majority of the population
  3. Chosen based on the relevant medical needs of the population
  4. Procured to treat rare diseases for people in the community
AnswerC — Chosen based on the relevant medical needs of the population

Essential medicines are not just for a select group; they are meant for broad, priority needs.

Although essential medicines often benefit large groups, the WHO definition is not limited to the majority. Some essential drugs may be used for less common but high-priority conditions, such as antituberculars, antimalarials, and antiretrovirals.

Correct answer: Selection is based on priority health needs, safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness.

Choice D describes orphan drugs, not essential medicines.

Pharmacodynamics & Drug Receptors 2028AItem 5

The type of drug interaction where the effect is greater than the sum of individual effects

  1. Synergism
  2. Potentiation
  3. Additive/Summation
  4. Antagonism
AnswerA — Synergism

Synergism: Total effect is greater than the sum of individual effects; e.g., beta-lactam drugs + aminoglycoside antibiotics (1 + 1 = 3).

Potentiation: One drug has no direct effect but enhances the effect of the other; e.g., beta-lactam drugs + beta-lactamase inhibitors (1 + 0 = 3).

Additive/Summation: The resulting effect is the sum of the individual effects; e.g., paracetamol + ibuprofen for fever and body malaise (1 + 1 = 2).

Antagonism: One drug has an opposing effect on another; no effect is produced (1 + 0 = 0).

Adverse Drug Reactions 2028AItem 10

Patient X underwent tooth extraction and was given Midazolam. A few hours after the procedure, the patient experienced drowsiness. What adverse effect of the drug does this indicate?

  1. Augmented
  2. Bizarre
  3. Continuous
  4. Delayed
AnswerA — Augmented

Augmented: Dose-related, predictable, and linked to known pharmacologic actions of the drug. It may be an exaggeration of a normal therapeutic effect or relate to other pharmacological actions beyond the target tissues. High incidence but low mortality.

Bizarre: Not dose-related, unpredictable, often immune-mediated or genetic; caused by genetic, immunologic, or teratogenic factors. High mortality; best managed by drug withdrawal.

Continuous: Dose- and time-related; occurs with prolonged drug use that causes another illness or pathology.

Delayed: Time-related; occurs after prolonged latency and does not tend to accumulate.

NSAIDs, Paracetamol, DMARDs & Drugs for Gout 2028BItem 14

Anti-gout drug with a black box warning risk for cardiovascular health

  1. Allopurinol
  2. Colchicine
  3. Febuxostat
  4. Pegloticase
AnswerC — Febuxostat

Febuxostat vs. allopurinol:

  • Like allopurinol, febuxostat has a urate-lowering effect regardless of whether hyperuricemia is due to overproduction or underexcretion.
  • It has a renoprotective effect over long treatment periods.
  • Unlike allopurinol, febuxostat appears safe in patients with moderate kidney disease.
  • Febuxostat has a black box warning: high risk for cardiovascular death.
Other source-provided visual notes

Adverse effects of allopurinol:

  • May precipitate gout; use an NSAID or colchicine concomitantly.
  • If the patient is already taking allopurinol, it should not be stopped during a flare because stopping may worsen attack recovery.
  • GI intolerance: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea.
  • Peripheral neuritis, necrotizing vasculitis, bone marrow suppression, and aplastic anemia may rarely occur.
  • Hepatic toxicity and interstitial nephritis.
  • Allergic skin reaction (pruritic lesions), exfoliative dermatitis in isolated cases, and very rare cataracts.
  • Safety in children and pregnancy is not yet established.
  • Caution in patients taking CYP3A4 inhibitors; these may inhibit colchicine metabolism and increase adverse effects.

Adverse effects of pegloticase:

  • Gout flare can occur during the first 3–6 months; give an NSAID or colchicine.
  • Infusion reactions can be avoided by giving a glucocorticoid and an H1 receptor blocker before treatment.
  • Combination with methotrexate may increase efficacy and decrease most allergic reactions.
  • Other adverse effects: nephrolithiasis, arthralgia, muscle spasm, headache, anemia, and nausea.
  • Less common: URTI, UTI, peripheral edema, and diarrhea.
  • Avoid in G6PD deficiency because of potential hemolytic anemia from hydrogen peroxide formation by uricase.

What is the first-line treatment in urate-lowering therapy for chronic gout?

  1. Allopurinol
  2. Colchicine
  3. Probenecid
  4. Pegloticase
AnswerA — Allopurinol

Allopurinol: First-line treatment of chronic gout between attacks. During initiation, use concurrently with an NSAID, except aspirin, or colchicine for 3–6 months to prevent flares.

Colchicine: Relieves pain and inflammation of gouty arthritis in 12–24 hours by inhibiting leukocyte migration and phagocytosis.

Probenecid: For patients with underexcretion of uric acid when a xanthine oxidase inhibitor is contraindicated or when tophi are present.

Pegloticase: Last resort for chronic refractory gout.
