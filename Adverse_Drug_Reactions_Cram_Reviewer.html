Must Know

If You Only Remember These

Pages 1–8, 12
  1. An ADR has a suspected or established causal link to the drug. An adverse event only needs a time association.
  2. All ADRs are adverse events, but not all adverse events are ADRs.
  3. A potential adverse drug event is a medication error stopped before harm, also called a near-miss or close call.
  4. Type A = Augmented, predictable, dose-related, common, usually preventable, and often reversible by dose reduction or stopping.
  5. Type B = Bizarre, unpredictable, not dose-related, uncommon, often serious, and commonly allergic or idiosyncratic.
  6. Type C = Continuous or Chronic, related to time and cumulative dose during long-term therapy.
  7. Type D = Delayed, appearing months or years after exposure, including teratogenicity and carcinogenicity.
  8. Type E = End-of-use, withdrawal or rebound after stopping a drug, prevented mainly by gradual tapering when appropriate.
  9. Type F = Failure, reduced or absent therapeutic effect due to causes such as interactions, resistance, poor adherence, poor technique, storage problems, or incorrect dose.
  10. For a suspected serious Type B reaction, stop the suspected drug immediately, treat emergencies, document, avoid severe rechallenge, educate, and report.
  11. Higher ADR risk is linked to extremes of age, impaired liver or kidney function, genetics, pregnancy, breastfeeding, coexisting disease, narrow therapeutic index, and more medications.
  12. The risk–benefit rule is simple: treatment is justified only when expected benefit outweighs possible harm.
  13. Dechallenge means improvement after stopping. Rechallenge means recurrence after restarting.
  14. Naranjo score in the lecture: >9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, 0 or below doubtful.
  15. Practical prevention: use few familiar drugs, the lowest effective dose, monitor both benefits and harms, review all OTC and herbal products, and think ADR when symptoms do not fit the illness.
  16. Pharmacovigilance is detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects of medicines.

1. Core Definitions and Boundaries

Must KnowPages 1–2

Core idea

An adverse drug reaction is a noxious, unintended response occurring at doses used for prophylaxis, diagnosis, therapy, or modification of physiological function. The lecture emphasizes a causal link and harm caused during normal use.

TermLecture meaningFast exam clue
Side effectExpected but not the intended therapeutic outcome. Related to pharmacologic activity. May be beneficial or harmful, usually less harmful and predictable.S.A.F.E. = Side effects Are Fairly Expected
Adverse effectViewed from the drug causing injury. An unexpected and harmful exaggeration of therapeutic effect.H.A.R.M. = Harmful Adverse Reactions Manifest
Adverse reactionViewed from the patient. A subjective or objective response such as pain, rash, or bleeding.Patient experiences the harm
Adverse eventAn injury or occurrence while the patient is receiving a drug, but the drug may not be the cause.Time association is enough
Adverse drug reactionLikely drug-related harm with suspected or established causality.Causality matters
Potential adverse drug eventA medication error stopped before harm occurs.Near-miss or close call
Exam Trap

Do not reverse the set relationship. All ADRs are adverse events, but not all adverse events are ADRs. Medication errors can cause adverse drug events even when the drug itself is not responsible.

Lecture example

  • Diphenhydramine for allergy can cause sedation.
  • Sedation is predictable and may be harmful.
  • It may also be useful when sleep or intense itching is a concern.

First response when ADR is suspected

The lecture advises considering discontinuing the drug or reducing the dose, depending on severity and the risk–benefit balance.

2. Significance and Risk Factors

Must KnowPage 2

Why ADRs matter

  • They affect patient well-being and can require admission or ICU care.
  • They add healthcare costs through longer hospitalization.
  • They may cause death.
  • They may mimic disease and trigger unnecessary investigations.
  • They may reduce patient adherence.

Lecture-reported figures

  • 30% of admissions in the USA and Canada, with a reported annual cost of 30.1 billion dollars in the USA.
  • 20% of admissions in Australia.
  • 11% of admissions in Europe.
  • 5.2% of pediatric ADRs lead to hospitalization, 39% may be life-threatening or fatal.
  • 36% prevalence of drug-related ER visits in older adults.

Source note: These are presented here as figures reported by the lecture, without outside verification.

Risk-factor map

Population

  • Newborns, immature liver and kidneys
  • Elderly, altered physiology, memory issues, multiple diseases and drugs
  • Gender differences noted in the lecture
  • Ethnicity and acetylator status
  • Genetic polymorphisms
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Drug handling

  • Reduced liver metabolism with age or disease
  • Reduced kidney elimination
  • Drug accumulation
  • Narrow therapeutic index
  • Excipients and other ingredients may trigger hypersensitivity

Disease and treatment context

  • Renal, hepatic, and cardiac dysfunction
  • Diabetes, HIV, and thyroid disease
  • Coexisting illnesses
  • More drugs, higher likelihood of ADR

Recall Check Significance and risk

Why are newborns and older adults both at higher risk, despite being opposite age groups?
Newborns have immature liver and kidney function. Older adults have altered organ function, more diseases, more medications, and possible adherence problems.
What happens to ADR probability as the number of drugs rises?
The lecture states a strong positive correlation. More drugs mean a higher probability of ADR.

Idiosyncratic reaction

  • Genetic mechanism or mechanism not fully understood
  • Unpredictable
  • Often serious but affects a small number of people
  • Examples include rash, hemolytic anemia, low WBC count, kidney injury, and nerve injury

Lecture examples

  • G6PD deficiency with aspirin, isoniazid, ibuprofen, sulfonamides, chloroquine, primaquine, or nitrofurantoin → hemolytic anemia
  • Low plasma AChE activity with procaine → altered local anesthetic response
  • Polymorphism = gene-sequence variation present in at least 1% of the population

Recall Check Types A and B

Insulin causes profound hypoglycemia after the wrong dose. What type?
Type A, Augmented. It is a predictable extension of insulin’s glucose-lowering action.
Methyldopa causes immune hemolytic anemia. What type?
Type B, Bizarre, specifically an immune-mediated reaction.
What is the immediate management priority for a serious Type B ADR?
Stop the suspected drug immediately and provide emergency treatment if needed.

Type D, Delayed

Prevention: avoid known teratogens, use pregnancy prevention programs for high-risk drugs, counsel on delayed risks, monitor high-risk therapy, use safer alternatives, and record exposure history.

Type E, End-of-use

  • Withdrawal or rebound after stopping prolonged therapy
  • Physiological dependence
  • Return of disease symptoms
  • Symptoms may be more severe than baseline
  • Beta-blockers → rebound hypertension, tachycardia, angina
  • Clonidine → rebound hypertension
  • Corticosteroids → acute adrenal insufficiency
  • Benzodiazepines → insomnia, agitation, anxiety, seizures
  • Opioids → tremors, headache, vomiting

Main prevention strategy: gradual tapering when appropriate. Management includes distinguishing withdrawal from relapse or another illness, temporary restart when clinically necessary, monitoring, education, and supportive care.

Type F, Failure of efficacy

Patient or administration

  • Poor adherence
  • Incorrect dose or schedule
  • Incorrect technique
  • Wrong food timing
  • Not completing therapy

Drug or product

  • Expired or poorly stored medicine
  • Counterfeit or adulterated product
  • Poor absorption
  • Drug–drug or drug–food interactions
  • Pharmacogenetic variation

Disease or organism

  • Disease progression
  • Microbial resistance
  • Critical illness changing protein binding or elimination
Mechanism
Rifampicin induces CYP450 enzymes
↓
Oral contraceptive metabolism increases
↓
Contraceptive effect decreases
↓
Type F failure, unintended pregnancy may occur

Management: confirm diagnosis and goal, check dose and schedule, assess adherence and technique, review interactions, consider drug monitoring, switch therapy for resistance or true failure, and educate the patient.

Exam Trap

Type C and Type D both involve time, but the timing pattern differs. Type C builds during continuous long-term exposure. Type D appears after a delay and may emerge after therapy has ended.

Recall Check Types C to F

Isoniazid for six months followed by neuropathy is which type?
Type C, Continuous or Chronic, due to long-term cumulative exposure.
Agitation after abrupt benzodiazepine discontinuation is which type?
Type E, End-of-use.
Phenytoin lowers oral contraceptive effectiveness, followed by pregnancy. Which type?
Type F, Failure of efficacy.

5. Ethical Prescribing, P-Drug Selection, and ESSC

Should KnowPages 5–6

Ethical prescribing sequence

Define the problem→ Set therapeutic objectives→ Choose an appropriate, affordable medicine→ Give information, warnings, and instructions→ Monitor regularly

Prescription orders should be legible, unambiguous, dated, timed when needed, and clearly signed.

Prescribing errors

  • Omitted information, such as “continue present IVF” without identifying the fluid
  • Illegible writing and ambiguous decimal points
  • Failure to identify contraindications
  • Failure to obtain a complete medication history
  • Failure to recognize physical or chemical incompatibilities
  • Interactions that reduce absorption, illustrated by antacids and tetracycline

Four noncompliance patterns

  1. Patient fails to obtain the medication.
  2. Patient takes it incorrectly, including wrong dose, frequency, timing, route, or purpose.
  3. Patient stops prematurely after feeling better or when the bottle is empty.
  4. Patient uses it inappropriately, including sharing medicines.

Legal and socioeconomic points stated by the lecture

  • OTC medicines are described as suitable for lay use in self-limiting conditions.
  • Controlled drugs require an S-2 license for prescribing.
  • Off-label use means a use, dosage form, or dose not approved for that condition.
  • The lecture states that physicians must use generic names and cites Republic Act No. 6675.

Source boundary: This reviewer reports the lecture’s statements. It does not update or independently verify current law or policy.

No medicine is completely safe. The drug is justified only when expected benefits outweigh possible harm. The lecture stresses involving both patient and doctor in balancing risk and benefit.

Common Confusion

ADR type and ADR severity are different axes. “Type A” describes mechanism and predictability. “Severe” describes clinical seriousness. A Type A reaction can still be severe.

Recall Check Severity

Which severity level usually requires stopping the drug?
Severe reactions usually require stopping the drug. Lethal reactions cause death and may trigger product withdrawal.
Does a predictable Type A reaction have to be mild?
No. Type describes the reaction pattern. Severity is assessed separately.

7. Detecting, Assessing, and Responding to ADRs

Must KnowPages 7–8, Figure 10

Why ADRs are missed

  • The reaction is unusual for a familiar drug.
  • The ADR mimics a common disease.
  • There is a long delay after therapy begins.
  • The drug triggers relapse or disease in a susceptible person.
  • The clinical situation is too complex.
  • Healthcare professionals do not recognize the possibility.

Clinical questions

  • Did the symptom begin after starting the drug?
  • Is the reaction known for the drug or class?
  • Did it improve after stopping?
  • Did it recur after restarting?
  • Are there alternative explanations?
  • Are there interacting medicines?
  • Is the dose appropriate for age, kidney, and liver function?
High-Index-of-Suspicion Rule

The lecture states: any symptom should be considered a drug side effect until proven otherwise.

8. Avoiding and Reporting ADRs

Must KnowPage 8

Practical prevention checklist

  • Use as few drugs as possible and favor familiar medicines.
  • Use the lowest effective dose and titrate.
  • Educate the patient on expected effects and what to do if problems occur.
  • Do not switch to an unfamiliar drug without good reason.
  • Monitor expected benefits and unexpected harms.
  • Check drug–drug, drug–food, alcohol, and household-chemical interactions.
  • Review prescription, OTC, and herbal products regularly.
  • Use extra caution in children, older adults, pregnancy, breastfeeding, serious illness, and hepatic or renal disease.
  • If symptoms do not fit the illness, think ADR.
  • If ADR is suspected, consider stopping or reducing the dose promptly after weighing risk and benefit.

Pharmacovigilance goals

  • Promote awareness and rational medicine use.
  • Promote safe and effective pharmaceutical use.
  • Identify and report suspected ADRs.
  • Compare benefits and harms to optimize use.
  • Identify medicine-related risks.
  • Enhance patient safety and care.
Reporting Impact
Suspected serious or life-threatening ADR is reported
↓
Rare reactions, new safety signals, interactions, and real-world risk factors are identified
↓
Regulatory warnings, studies, publications, dosing changes, modified indications, or product withdrawal

The lecture cites some COX-2 inhibitors as examples of medicines withdrawn after post-marketing safety concerns involving stroke, heart failure, or death.

Exam Traps

Details That Change the Answer

Pages 1–8
Type A is not simply “an overdose.” It can happen at a normal dose when the expected pharmacologic action is exaggerated.
Type B can occur with a small therapeutic amount. Dose reduction is not the key solution for a serious bizarre reaction.
“Delayed” does not mean “long duration of use.” That pattern is Type C. Type D means the effect appears after a delay.
Do not stop certain medicines abruptly. Corticosteroids, beta-blockers, clonidine, benzodiazepines, and opioids are lecture examples linked to Type E reactions.
A pregnancy after rifampicin plus oral contraceptive is Type F. The issue is reduced efficacy due to enzyme induction.
No improvement after stopping is not positive dechallenge. The PDF figure calls it negative dechallenge, while an adjacent note is inconsistent.

5-Minute Review

Last-Minute Cram Sheet

Condensed from whole PDF

Definitions

  • ADR: unintended, noxious response with suspected or established drug causality.
  • Adverse event: occurs during treatment, drug may not be the cause.
  • Side effect: expected secondary pharmacologic effect.
  • Potential ADE: near-miss stopped before harm.

A–F classification

  • A Augmented: predictable, dose-related, common.
  • B Bizarre: unpredictable, not dose-related, allergy or idiosyncrasy.
  • C Continuous: chronic, cumulative dose and time.
  • D Delayed: late teratogenic or carcinogenic effect.
  • E End-of-use: withdrawal or rebound.
  • F Failure: reduced therapeutic efficacy.

Classic associations

  • Heparin → bleeding → A
  • Insulin → hypoglycemia → A
  • Penicillin → anaphylaxis → B
  • Corticosteroids → adrenal suppression → C
  • Thalidomide or isotretinoin → teratogenicity → D
  • Clonidine or beta-blocker withdrawal → E
  • Rifampicin plus OCP → contraceptive failure → F

Causality

  • Timing after exposure
  • Known reaction
  • Positive dechallenge
  • Positive rechallenge
  • No better alternative cause
  • Dose–response relationship
  • Naranjo: >9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful

Risk factors

Newborn, elderly, liver disease, kidney disease, genetics, pregnancy, breastfeeding, coexisting disease, excipients, narrow therapeutic index, drug interactions, and polypharmacy.

Management anchors

  • Type A → reduce dose or stop if serious.
  • Serious Type B → stop immediately and treat emergency.
  • Type C → lowest dose, shortest duration, scheduled monitoring.
  • Type D → prevent exposure and counsel on delayed risk.
  • Type E → taper gradually when appropriate.
  • Type F → verify diagnosis, dose, adherence, technique, interactions, storage, and resistance.

Final rule

No medicine is completely safe. Choose and continue treatment only when expected benefit outweighs possible harm, then monitor and report serious suspected ADRs.
