If You Only Remember These

Rational drug therapy means the right drug, adequate dose, sufficient duration, appropriate clinical need, and lowest cost.
Ethical prescribing must maximize effectiveness, minimize harm, avoid waste, and respect patient choice.
The rational prescribing sequence is diagnosis → goals → treatment selection → prescribe and counsel → monitor.
Not every health problem needs a drug. Treatment can be advice, non-drug care, a P-drug, or referral.
ESSC stands for Efficacy, Safety, Suitability, and Cost.
Do not mix criteria when scoring. Adverse effects belong under Safety. Patient contraindications belong under Suitability.
Choose the best drug group first, then compare specific drugs within the winning group.
Cost means the total treatment cost, not merely the price of one tablet or vial.
All prescription orders should be legible, unambiguous, dated, timed when needed, and clearly signed.
Major adherence failures include not obtaining the drug, taking it incorrectly, stopping early, and using it inappropriately.
The lecture states that physicians must prescribe the generic name. “No substitution” instructions are treated as violative under the cited issuances.
Patient counseling must cover effects, side effects, instructions, warnings, follow-up, and confirmation of understanding.
A well-chosen P-drug depends on both correct patient assessment and knowledge of the drugs’ pharmacologic properties.
Once-daily dosing, acceptable taste and form, local availability, age, pregnancy, comorbidities, and current medicines all affect Suitability.
In the urticaria mini case, the lecture’s ESSC process selects second-generation antihistamines, then cetirizine.
Before ending the consultation, ask the patient to repeat the most important information.

1. Rational Drug Therapy
Must know Page 1

Core definition

Rational use means giving medication appropriate to the patient’s clinical needs, in a dose that meets individual requirements, for an adequate period, at the lowest cost to the patient and community.

1985 revision: right drug + adequate dose + sufficient duration + appropriate clinical need + lowest cost.

Why prescribing is an art

  • Diagnostic skills
  • Knowledge of medicines
  • Clinical pharmacology principles
  • Communication skills
  • Appreciation of risks and benefits for the specific patient

Why it matters

  • More than 50% of medicines are prescribed, dispensed, or sold inappropriately.
  • 50% of patients fail to take medicines correctly.
  • About one-third of the world’s population lacks access to essential medicines.

Fast recall cue

5 Rights of rational therapy: right clinical need, right drug, right dose, right duration, right total cost.

Recall check
What five elements appear in the revised definition?
Right drug, adequate dose, sufficient duration, appropriateness to clinical need, and lowest cost.
Why is drug knowledge alone insufficient for good prescribing?
The lecture also requires diagnostic skills, clinical pharmacology, communication, and risk–benefit appreciation for the particular patient.
2. Rational or Ethical Prescribing
Must know Pages 1–2

Four ethical aims

Maximize clinical effectiveness
Minimize harms
Avoid wasting scarce resources
Respect patient choice

Ethical prescribing workflow

  1. Evaluate and define the patient’s problem.
  2. Determine therapeutic objectives.
  3. Select an appropriate medication with cost in mind.
  4. Give information, warnings, and instructions.
  5. Monitor regularly.

Prescription order standards

  • Legible
  • Unambiguous
  • Dated
  • Timed for chart orders
  • Clearly signed

Purpose: optimal communication among prescriber, pharmacist, and nurse.

Prescribing errors

  • Omitted information
  • Illegible writing
  • Ambiguous decimal points
  • Missed contraindications
  • Incomplete drug history
  • Unrecognized incompatibilities

Four adherence failures

  1. Fails to obtain the medicine
  2. Takes it incorrectly
  3. Stops prematurely
  4. Uses it inappropriately, such as sharing

Examples to remember

  • “Continue present IVF” omits the exact fluid.
  • “Resume preoperative meds” assumes a complete record.
  • 0.1 may be misread as 1.
  • 1.0 may be misread as 10.
Exam trap
A medication error can arise before the drug reaches the patient. Missing information and poor communication are prescribing problems, not merely patient adherence problems.
Recall check
List the four ethical aims without looking.
Maximize effectiveness, minimize harms, avoid waste, respect patient choice.
Name the four major forms of noncompliance.
Failure to obtain, failure to take as prescribed, premature discontinuation, and inappropriate use.
Which prescription feature is especially relevant to chart orders?
Timing the order.
3. Legal and Socioeconomic Considerations
Should know Pages 1–2

Drug classes and control

  • OTC drugs: may be used by laypersons for self-limiting conditions.
  • Prescription drugs: require a licensed prescriber.
  • Controlled substances listed include opioids, hallucinogens, stimulants, depressants, and anabolic steroids.
  • The lecture states that an S-2 license is required before prescribing regulated or controlled drugs.

Drug safety surveillance

  • Government controls labeling and distribution.
  • Licensing boards control who may prescribe.
  • The FDA monitors drug safety through mandatory company reports and voluntary healthcare-practitioner reports.
  • The Philippine National Formulary is linked in the lecture to government hospital purchasing and PhilHealth reimbursement.

Generic prescribing

  • Physicians must prescribe the generic name.
  • This allows the pharmacist to supply a generically equivalent and less costly product.
  • The prescriber may include a particular brand, but the lecture’s cited issuances prohibit obstructing proper generic substitution.

RA 6675 and cited issuances

  • The lecture states doctors are not allowed to instruct the pharmacist not to substitute a brand.
  • DOH AO 169, s. 2004 is presented as requiring generic names in DOH transactions.
  • DOH AO 62, s. 1989 treats “No Substitution” wording that obstructs generic dispensing as violative.

Product and cost considerations

  • Possible generic-product concerns listed: high inert-to-active ratio, poor solubility, low therapeutic index.
  • Bioequivalence and price information should be available from the FDA.
  • Other costs include prescription fees, refills, and quantity dispensed.

Labeled vs off-label

The lecture defines off-label use as use for an unapproved disease or condition, dosage form, or dose.

Recall check
What name must physicians place on prescriptions according to the lecture?
The generic name.
What is “off-label use” in the lecture?
Use for a disease or condition, dosage form, or dose not approved in the product labeling.
4. Choosing the P-Drug
Must know Pages 2–3
Mechanism / process
Determine diagnosis
Identify treatment goals
Select treatment
Prescribe and counsel
Monitor patient

Expanded P-drug outline

  1. Diagnosis
  2. Therapeutic objectives
  3. Non-drug measures
  4. Inventory of effective drug groups
  5. Choose specific drugs from the winning group
  6. Write the prescription
  7. Counsel the patient

Treatment options

Treatment selection ├── Advice and information ├── Non-drug treatment ├── Drug treatment with a P-drug └── Referral

Key point: not all health problems need drug therapy.

Diagnosis

Base prescribing decisions on the primary diagnosis and relevant secondary diagnoses.

Lecture example: pneumonia as primary diagnosis, type 2 diabetes as secondary diagnosis.

Treatment goals

Goals may be cure, relief, or prevention.

Pneumonia example: cure infection, ease cough, prevent complications or recurrence.

What makes a good P-drug?

Proper assessment of the patient’s problem plus knowledge of pharmacologic properties.

Recall check
What comes immediately after identifying treatment goals?

What should be inventoried before selecting a specific P-drug?
Effective drug groups.
What are the three broad therapeutic goals given by the lecture?
Cure, relief, and prevention.
5. ESSC Criteria and Scoring
Must know Pages 2–3

E — Efficacy

  • Mechanism of action
  • PK: onset, duration, bioavailability, distribution, metabolism, excretion
  • PD: specificity, selectivity, dose response, therapeutic index
  • Spectrum of activity for antibiotics
  • Supporting journal reports, when available

S — Safety

  • Adverse effects
  • Frequency
  • Seriousness
  • Predictability
  • Potential toxicity

S — Suitability

  • Previous ADRs
  • Age, pregnancy, vulnerability, comorbidities
  • Current therapy and interactions
  • Likely adherence
  • Dose, frequency, dosage form, taste
  • Local availability and practicality

C — Cost

  • Total treatment cost
  • Compute across the whole treatment duration.
  • Lower total price receives a higher rating.
  • Consider alternatives with similar efficacy.
Scoring rule that changes answers
Scores must stay inside their own criterion. Do not lower Efficacy because a drug has adverse effects. Do not lower Safety because the drug is contraindicated in pregnancy. Put population constraints and contraindications under Suitability.

Two-stage selection

List drug groups
Score each group from + to ++++
Choose highest group total
List drugs in that group
Score again
Choose highest drug total

For SGDs, the lecture recommends presenting data column-by-column, comparing all drugs under one criterion before moving to the next.

Recall check
Where should a contraindication in the patient be scored?
Suitability.
Where should the frequency and seriousness of adverse effects be scored?
Safety.
Does cost mean the unit price?
No. It means the cost of the complete treatment course.
Why are drug groups scored before specific drugs?
The process first identifies the best therapeutic group, then identifies the best specific P-drug within that group.

7. Counseling the Patient
Must know Page 5

Effects

  • Why the drug is needed
  • Which symptoms will and will not disappear
  • When the effect should start
  • What happens if taken incorrectly or not at all

Side effects

  • Which may occur
  • How to recognize them
  • How long they may continue
  • How serious they are
  • What action to take

Instructions

  • How and when to take it
  • How long to continue
  • How to store it
  • What to do with leftovers

Warnings

  • When not to take it
  • Maximum dose
  • Why the full course must be completed

Future consultations

  • When to return
  • When to return earlier
  • What information to bring next time

Confirm understanding

  • Ask whether everything is clear
  • Ask the patient to repeat the key points
  • Ask whether there are more questions
Fast distinction
Counseling is not finished when information has been delivered. The final step is to verify that the patient understood it.
Recall check
What are the six counseling domains?
Effects, side effects, instructions, warnings, future consultations, and confirmation that everything is clear.
What teach-back method does the lecture recommend?
Ask the patient to repeat the most important information.

Commonly Confused Concepts

Do not confuse: Efficacy vs Safety
Efficacy asks whether and how well the drug works. Safety asks about adverse effects, their frequency, seriousness, predictability, and toxicity.
Do not confuse: Safety vs Suitability
Safety is the drug’s harm profile. Suitability is whether that drug fits this patient, considering contraindications, comorbidities, interactions, age, dosage form, adherence, and availability.
Do not confuse: Unit price vs treatment cost
The ESSC Cost score is based on the total cost for the complete course, not the cost of a single tablet, vial, or bottle.
Do not confuse: P-drug group vs P-drug
First choose the winning drug group. Then score the specific drugs inside that group to select the final P-drug.
Do not confuse: Drug therapy vs treatment
Treatment may be advice, information, non-drug measures, a P-drug, or referral. A treatment plan does not automatically require medication.
Do not confuse: Labeled vs off-label
Labeled use follows approved labeling. The lecture uses “off-label” for an unapproved disease or condition, dosage form, or dose.

Exam Traps

Trap 1
A highly effective drug can still score poorly overall because Safety, Suitability, or Cost is weak.
Trap 2
Do not penalize Efficacy for adverse effects. Put adverse effects under Safety.
Trap 3
Pregnancy, age restriction, comorbidity, and contraindications are Suitability issues in the lecture’s scoring method.
Trap 4
The cheapest single tablet does not automatically have the best Cost score. Calculate the entire treatment course.
Trap 5
Choosing a drug is not the last step. Prescription writing, counseling, and monitoring remain part of rational prescribing.
Trap 6
Once-daily dosing may improve adherence, so frequency belongs under Suitability.
Trap 7
OTC status does not mean a drug is appropriate for every patient. The lecture’s rational process still applies.
Trap 8
