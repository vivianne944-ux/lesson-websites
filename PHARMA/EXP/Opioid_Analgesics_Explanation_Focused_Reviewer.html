Central question

How do opioids reduce pain, why do they produce predictable organ effects, and how do drug choice, metabolism, receptor activity, and duration change their clinical behavior?

The lecture moves from terminology and endogenous opioid biology to classification, then connects pharmacokinetics and receptor signaling to analgesia, adverse effects, toxicity, and pain-control strategies.

How the topics connect

Receptor identity helps predict effects. Intrinsic activity tells you how fully the receptor is activated. Affinity/potency tells you how much drug is needed for a given effect. Pharmacokinetics tells you how quickly and how long that effect appears. These together explain both analgesia and toxicity.

Terms, Sources, and Endogenous Opioids

Start by separating the names, then connect endogenous peptide families to receptor functions.

PDF p. 1
Definition

Opioids

The lecture uses opioids as the broadest group. It includes natural and semisynthetic alkaloid derivatives from opium, opioid-like and synthetic drugs whose effects are blocked by naloxone, and endogenous peptides acting at opioid receptors.

Key idea

Opioid describes the receptor-linked pharmacologic family, not just drugs physically obtained from opium.

Definition

Opiates

Opiates are naturally occurring alkaloids and their derivatives. The lecture lists morphine and codeine, and also discusses semisynthetic drugs derived from opium alkaloids.

Thebaine is presented as a source for semisynthetic opioids. Papaverine is specifically noted as not an opioid and as having smooth-muscle relaxant activity.

Definition

Narcotics

Originally, narcotics referred to sleep-inducing medications. The lecture emphasizes that U.S. and Philippine usage shifted toward a legal term for abused or illegal drugs.

Do not confuse

A narcotic can be non-opioid. The lecture’s example is cocaine, a local anesthetic.

Opium

  • Source plants: Papaver somniferum and Papaver album.
  • Described as a crude extract from the poppy seed pod.
  • Lecture-listed uses: medicinal, nutritional, euphoric, and religious.

Morphine

  • The prototype opioid agonist.
  • A pure alkaloid isolated from opium sap.
  • First isolated by Sertürner in 1803.
  • Named after Morpheus, the Greek god of dreams.
  • Lecture states it is 10-fold more potent than opium.

Three endogenous opioid peptide families

When are they released?

The lecture links release to painful stimuli, stress associated with pain, anticipation of pain, the placebo effect, and conditioned pain modulation (CPM).

For CPM, the lecture describes a second painful stimulus reducing the original pain. The important pattern is that the body’s own pain-modulating system can be recruited by the pain context itself.

Classification of Exogenous Opioids

The same drug can be classified in four different ways: origin, speed, efficacy, and receptor-binding strength.

PDF p. 2

Do not collapse the classification systems into one.

Synthesis tells where the drug comes from. Onset/offset tells how quickly the effect appears and disappears. Intrinsic activity tells how much effect the receptor can produce once occupied. Affinity/potency tells how strongly the drug binds and how much is required for effect.

κ agonist + μ antagonist. κ activation produces supraspinal and spinal analgesia, while μ antagonism limits μ-mediated effects. The lecture calls its analgesic behavior a ceiling effect.

Partial μ agonist and κ antagonist in the lecture. It is long acting for 24–36 hours, used for detoxification from μ agonists such as morphine, and is available sublingually with naloxone.

Understand It Better: potency, efficacy, and duration

Potency answers “how much drug is needed?” Efficacy answers “how large a maximum effect can receptor activation produce?” Duration answers “how long does the effect persist?” The lecture’s opioid table shows that these properties do not always move together.

Remifentanil is the memorable example: strong μ binding, high efficacy, but extremely short analgesic duration, so the dose is titrated by infusion.

Lock It In

Classify every opioid with four separate questions: Where did it come from? How fast is it? How fully does it activate receptors? How strong is its binding/potency?

Pharmacokinetics

Route, tissue distribution, metabolic pathway, and renal excretion determine when effects appear, how long they last, and which metabolites accumulate.

PDF pp. 2–3, 7

Absorption and route

Oral and parenteral

  • Opioids generally have good absorption orally and parenterally.
  • Parenteral administration is highlighted for severe pain.
  • Oral administration is affected by the first-pass effect and shows interpatient variability.
  • For morphine, the lecture gives an oral equianalgesic rule of 3 times the IV dose.
  • Codeine and oxycodone are described as having a high oral:parenteral ratio, with oral and parenteral doses nearly similar in the lecture.

Alternative routes

  • Nasal insufflation: rapid therapeutic blood levels.
  • Oral mucosa: lozenges.
  • Transdermal patches: slow onset and analgesia lasting over days.

Fentanyl patch sequence

Apply patchTransdermal delivery begins.
Initial effectAbout 6–12 hours.
StabilizationBlood level stabilizes at about 24 hours.
Long effectGiven over about 3 days for analgesia.

Because onset is slow, the lecture recommends first obtaining quick pain control by a parenteral opioid, then switching to the patch for longer relief.

Distribution

Highly perfused tissues

Brain, lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen. These receive drug early because blood flow is high.

Less perfused but large reservoirs

Skeletal muscle and fat. Although less perfused, their greater bulk makes them important reservoirs.

High doses, frequent administration, and continuous infusion can saturate tissue reservoirs, prolonging drug effects. All opioids bind plasma proteins, but with different affinities.

CYP2D6 polymorphism: why the same dose may behave differently

Ultra-rapid metabolizer

For codeine, more morphine is generated. The lecture links this to respiratory depression and death and cites pediatric deaths. In the appendix, codeine and tramadol show increased active metabolites, analgesia, and side effects in ultrarapid metabolizers.

Poor metabolizer

Little active metabolite is produced, so there may be no significant analgesic effect. The lecture uses this to explain a patient reporting no relief from codeine or tramadol.

Lecture warning: codeine and tramadol are not recommended for nursing mothers because the mother may be an ultrarapid metabolizer.

Excretion and renal failure

Polar glucuronide metabolites are excreted mainly in the urine, with some enterohepatic circulation. With renal failure, high doses, or prolonged use, metabolites can accumulate and exaggerate effects.

Lock It In

Route predicts onset, tissue reservoirs predict prolonged effect, enzyme phenotype predicts active-metabolite exposure, and renal function predicts metabolite accumulation.

Mechanism of Action and Pain Pathways

Opioids inhibit pain processing at peripheral, spinal, and higher-center levels and recruit descending inhibitory pathways.

PDF p. 3
Simple idea

Opioid receptors make pain-signaling neurons less able to release excitatory transmitters and less likely to fire.

The lecture describes this through a paired ion-channel effect: close presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels and open postsynaptic K⁺ channels.

Complete receptor mechanism

1. Receptor bindingOpioid binds μ, δ, or κ Gi/Go-coupled GPCR.
2A. Presynaptic effectVoltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels close.
3A. Transmitter effectRelease of excitatory neurotransmitters falls: glutamate, NE, ACh, serotonin, substance P.
2B. Postsynaptic effectK⁺ channels open.
3B. Electrical effectPostsynaptic neuron becomes hyperpolarized.
4. OutcomeNeuronal activity and pain processing are inhibited, producing analgesia.

What this flow means

The presynaptic neuron releases less excitatory signal, while the postsynaptic neuron becomes harder to activate. The two effects converge on less transmission of nociceptive information.

Ascending pain pathway

The lecture identifies three putative sites of opioid analgesic action:

  1. Primary afferent nociceptor in inflamed or injured peripheral tissue.
  2. Dorsal horn of the spinal cord.
  3. Higher centers: amygdala, ACG, and VPL of the thalamus.

Activation of μ-opioid receptors at any of these sites inhibits pain processing and produces analgesia.

Descending pain pathway

Key sites are the periaqueductal gray (PAG), rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM), and the dorsal horn.

1. MOR activationInhibits GABAergic inhibitory interneurons.
2. Indirect resultDescending pain-inhibitory neurons become activated.
3. Spinal outcomeNociceptive processing in the dorsal horn is more strongly inhibited.
4. Final effectAnalgesia.

The important connection: inhibiting an inhibitory GABA neuron indirectly frees the descending inhibitory pathway to suppress pain.

Understand It Better: ascending versus descending

Ascending refers to nociceptive information moving upward from the periphery and spinal cord toward higher centers. Opioids can interrupt processing at several levels.

Descending refers to brainstem systems that project downward to inhibit spinal nociceptive processing. MOR activation in the PAG/RVM circuit strengthens that inhibitory control.

Lock It In

Presynaptic Ca²⁺ closes → less transmitter release. Postsynaptic K⁺ opens → hyperpolarization. Analgesia occurs by inhibiting ascending processing and enhancing descending inhibition.

Pattern recognition

Think of the organ effects in clusters: CNS depression (sedation, respiratory depression), parasympathetic/autonomic signs (miosis, nausea), and smooth-muscle effects (constipation, urinary retention, biliary/ureteral colic, prolonged labor).

Lock It In

The classic high-yield cluster is analgesia + sedation + respiratory depression + miosis + constipation, with important smooth-muscle complications in the biliary and urinary systems.

Tolerance, Dependence, OIH, and Toxicity

These are related but distinct consequences of repeated or excessive opioid exposure.

PDF p. 4

1. Tolerance

The lecture places onset after about 2–3 weeks of frequent exposure and links it to cAMP-system upregulation, receptor recycling behavior, and receptor uncoupling. Cross-tolerance may occur.

High toleranceModerate toleranceMinimal or no tolerance
Analgesia, euphoria, dysphoria, mental clouding, sedation, respiratory depression, antidiuresis, nausea/vomiting, cough suppression. Bradycardia. Miosis, constipation, convulsions.
Understand It Better: proposed mechanisms of tolerance

cAMP pathway

MOR activation engages Gi, inhibits adenylyl cyclase, and decreases cAMP. The lecture says compensatory overproduction of adenylyl cyclase occurs when signaling is chronically suppressed; however, cAMP upregulation alone is not enough to explain tolerance.

Receptor recycling

Endogenous ligands normally trigger receptor endocytosis, resensitization, and return to the plasma membrane. The lecture states that morphine fails to induce endocytosis and recycling, whereas methadone induces endocytosis.

Receptor uncoupling

Structural dysfunction develops between the receptor and the G protein, second-messenger system, or target ion channels.

2. Physical dependence

Physical dependence means body physiology has adapted to repeated drug exposure, so abrupt discontinuation produces withdrawal.

Withdrawal features

Rhinorrhea, lacrimation, yawning, chills, piloerection, hyperventilation, hyperthermia, mydriasis, muscle aches, vomiting, diarrhea, anxiety, and hostility.

Morphine withdrawal timing

  • Appears: 6–10 hours
  • Peaks: 36–48 hours
  • Disappears: about 5 days

Detoxification principle

Detoxification is described as a method to counter dependence while avoiding abrupt discontinuation. The lecture also emphasizes that fear of tolerance or dependence does not justify withholding adequate pain relief from patients who need it, such as patients with cancer pain.

3. Opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH)

OIH is an increased sensation of pain, including pain from rubbing or massage in the lecture example. It is associated with prolonged administration and high doses.

Most notorious agents listed

Morphine, fentanyl, remifentanil.

Possible causes stated

Microglial-expressed receptor, spinal dynorphin, activation of bradykinin and NMDA receptors, and tissue injury or inflammation.

Lecture statement: opioid is not recommended for chronic noncancer pain.

4. Opioid toxicity

PRC triad

Pinpoint pupils + Respiratory depression + Coma strongly suggest opioid poisoning.

RecognizePRC toxicity triad.
First responseSupport vital functions.
AntagonizeAdminister naloxone.

Do not confuse these four

Lock It In

Acute and Chronic Pain Therapy

The lecture emphasizes multimodal therapy and presents separate stepwise frameworks for chronic noncancer pain and cancer pain.

PDF pp. 5, 7

Acute versus chronic

Acute painChronic pain
Hours to daysMonths to years

For chronic neuropathic pain, the lecture notes that pain is already in the nerves or nervous system and may require agents beyond usual analgesics, including antiseizure drugs, antipsychotics, and a novel voltage-gated Ca²⁺-channel blocker.

Multimodal approach

Multiple agents with complementary mechanisms are combined:

  • COX inhibitors / NSAIDs
  • Gabapentinoids
  • Selective norepinephrine receptor inhibitors
  • Local anesthetics
  • Opioids

Why it matters

Complementary mechanisms provide analgesia that is superior to one medication alone and reduce opioid requirements with fewer adverse effects.

Modified analgesic ladder for chronic noncancer pain

Step 1
Non-opioid analgesic + adjuvant analgesic.
Step 2
Weak opioid + non-opioid + adjuvant.
Step 3
Non-opioid + adjuvant + minimally invasive intervention.
Step 4
Strong opioid + non-opioid + adjuvant.

Step 1 examples include paracetamol/NSAIDs plus adjuvant or integrative measures such as acupuncture, music therapy, and other nonpharmacologic measures. Step 3 includes implantation of a mechanism that continually delivers local anesthetic to a specific body area.

Lecture emphasis: for noncancer pain, strong opioids are not given right away.

WHO cancer-pain framework in the appendix

Step 1
Non-opioid ± adjuvant.
Step 2
Opioid for mild to moderate pain ± non-opioid ± adjuvant.
Step 3
Opioid for moderate to severe pain ± non-opioid ± adjuvant.
Step 4
Invasive and minimally invasive treatments.

The lecture gives lysis of nerves in specific areas as an example of the invasive/minimally invasive step. It also lists adjuvant medicines such as steroids, antipsychotics, and antiseizure drugs for cancer pain and pain from metastases.

Do not merge the two ladders

The lecture displays a modified chronic noncancer pain ladder on page 5 and a separate cancer-pain framework in the page 7 appendix. Their step order and labels differ.

Lock It In

Multimodal = complementary mechanisms, better analgesia, less opioid requirement. Escalation differs between the chronic noncancer and cancer-pain frameworks shown in the lecture.

Strong affinity: fentanyl group.
Intermediate: morphine, methadone, oxycodone, buprenorphine.
Weak: codeine, tramadol.

Intrinsic activity

Full: morphine, fentanyl group, methadone.
Partial μ: buprenorphine.
Mixed: nalbuphine, butorphanol, pentazocine, buprenorphine.
Antagonist: naloxone.

Core mechanism

Gi/Go GPCR → close presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels → ↓ glutamate/NE/ACh/5-HT/substance P.
Open postsynaptic K⁺ channels → hyperpolarization → neuronal inhibition.

Pain pathways

Ascending sites: primary afferent, dorsal horn, amygdala/ACG/VPL.
Descending: PAG → RVM → dorsal horn; MOR inhibits GABA interneuron → descending inhibition increases.

PK numbers

Morphine oral ≈ 3× IV in lecture.
Fentanyl patch: onset 6–12 h, stabilization 24 h, effect over 3 days.
Buprenorphine duration: 24–36 h.

Metabolites

M3G: CNS excitation, seizure.
M6G: active, 4–6× morphine; respiratory depression/sedation/decreased motility if accumulated.
Normeperidine: CNS excitation, seizure.

CYP2D6

Ultrarapid: more active metabolite → more analgesia + more toxicity.
Poor: little active metabolite → poor analgesia.
Codeine/tramadol not recommended for nursing mothers in lecture.

Organ effects

Analgesia, cough suppression, euphoria/dysphoria, miosis, nausea/vomiting, respiratory depression, sedation, hypothermia, truncal rigidity, constipation, urinary retention, biliary/ureteral colic, prolonged labor, pruritus.

Tolerance

Starts after 2–3 weeks frequent exposure.
High: analgesia, euphoria, sedation, respiratory depression, N/V, cough suppression.
Minimal/none: miosis, constipation, convulsions.

Dependence

Withdrawal: rhinorrhea, lacrimation, yawning, piloerection, mydriasis, aches, vomiting, diarrhea, anxiety.
Morphine: onset 6–10 h, peak 36–48 h, resolves about 5 d.

OIH vs toxicity

OIH: increased pain after prolonged/high-dose opioids; morphine, fentanyl, remifentanil noted.
Toxicity PRC: Pinpoint pupils + Respiratory depression + Coma → support vital functions + naloxone.

Naloxone

Adult 0.1–0.4 mg IV; neonate 0.01 mg/kg IV/IM. Repeat 3–5 min. Reversal 1–3 min. Duration 1–2 h; recurrent toxicity may require repeat/infusion.

Signature agents

Methadone: detox, long acting oral.
Meperidine: tachycardia, normeperidine, avoid MAOI.
Nalbuphine: κ agonist/μ antagonist, ceiling.
Tramadol: weak μ + 5-HT/NE mechanism.

Mixed mnemonic

P*PE NG BUBU
Pentazocine • Nalbuphine • Butorphanol • Buprenorphine

Multimodal pain care

NSAIDs/COX inhibitors + gabapentinoids + selective NE inhibitors + local anesthetics + opioids → complementary analgesia, reduced opioid requirement, fewer adverse effects.

Noncancer ladder

1 non-opioid + adjuvant → 2 weak opioid + non-opioid + adjuvant → 3 minimal intervention + non-opioid + adjuvant → 4 strong opioid + non-opioid + adjuvant.

Cancer ladder

1 non-opioid ± adjuvant → 2 opioid mild–moderate ± non-opioid/adjuvant → 3 opioid moderate–severe ± non-opioid/adjuvant → 4 invasive/minimally invasive treatment.

Final retrieval anchors

μ = analgesia + sedation + respiratory depression. Ca²⁺ closes, K⁺ opens. M6G accumulates in renal failure. PRC = opioid toxicity. Remifentanil = very short, infusion. Naloxone = dramatic rapid reversal, but short duration.
