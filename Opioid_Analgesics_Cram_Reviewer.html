Exam trap: Cannabis in the lecture review question is classified as a narcotic in the legal-context usage, not as an opiate or opioid.

Source

SHOULD KNOW
  • Opium: crude extract from the poppy seed pod of Papaver somniferum and Papaver album.
  • Morphine: prototype opioid agonist, pure alkaloid from opium sap, first isolated by Sertürner in 1803, named after Morpheus, and stated to be 10-fold more potent than opium.
  • Thebaine: lecture states it is not an opioid, but it is a source for semisynthetic opioids.
  • Papaverine: lecture states it is not an opioid and has smooth muscle relaxant activity.

SHOULD KNOW

Released in response to:

  • Stress associated with pain
  • Anticipation of pain
  • Placebo effect
  • Conditioned pain modulation, where a second pain reduces the main pain

N/OFQ system

NICE TO KNOW
  • ORL1 is now called the NOP receptor.
  • Nociceptin or Orphanin FQ is structurally similar to dynorphin and binds only to ORL1 or NOP.
  • NOP is G protein-coupled, widely expressed in the CNS and periphery, and has pro- and antinociceptive activity.
  • The lecture also links this system with cough suppression, reward, learning, mood, anxiety, and Parkinsonism.

3. PharmacokineticsPages 2–3, 7

Absorption and administration

MUST KNOW
  • Oral: generally well absorbed, but first-pass effect causes interpatient variability.
  • Morphine oral conversion: the lecture states oral morphine may require about 3 times the IV dose because of a low oral:parenteral potency ratio.
  • Codeine and oxycodone: high oral:parenteral ratio, so oral and parenteral doses are closer.
  • Nasal insufflation: rapid therapeutic blood levels.
  • Oral mucosa: delivery via lozenges.
  • Transdermal fentanyl: slow onset and long duration. Initial effect 6–12 hours, stabilization about 24 hours, and used over about 3 days in the lecture.
Practical sequence from the lecture: because the fentanyl patch is slow, first use a parenteral opioid for rapid control, then switch to the patch for prolonged relief.

Distribution

SHOULD KNOW
  • Highly perfused: brain, lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen.
  • Less perfused: skeletal muscle and fat, which become major reservoirs because of their bulk.
  • High doses, frequent administration, and continuous infusion can saturate reservoirs and prolong effects.
  • All opioids bind plasma proteins, but with different affinity.

Excretion and renal failure

MUST KNOW
  • Polar glucuronide metabolites are excreted mainly in urine. Some enterohepatic circulation occurs.
  • M3G: CNS excitatory, enhanced CNS entry with probenecid in the lecture, accumulation may cause seizure.
  • M6G: active, 4–6 times more potent than morphine, accumulates in renal failure → increased efficacy but increased respiratory depression, sedation, and decreased motility.
  • Normeperidine: water-soluble meperidine metabolite, CNS excitatory, accumulation may cause seizure.
RECALL CHECK
Which morphine metabolite is more potent than morphine and accumulates in renal failure?
Morphine-6-glucuronide, M6G.
Which two metabolites are most associated with seizures in this lecture?
M3G and normeperidine.
Why might codeine fail to relieve pain in a poor CYP2D6 metabolizer?
Too little morphine is formed.

Ascending pain pathway

MUST KNOW
  • Primary afferent nociceptor in inflamed or injured peripheral tissue
  • Dorsal horn of the spinal cord
  • Higher centers, including amygdala, anterior cingulate gyrus, and VPL of the thalamus
μ-receptor activation at any of these sites inhibits pain processing.

MUST KNOW

↓

  2. Descending pain-inhibitory neurons are indirectly activated.

5. Tolerance, Dependence, OIH, and ToxicityPage 4

Tolerance

MUST KNOW
  • Lecture onset: after 2–3 weeks of frequent opioid exposure.
  • Associated with upregulation of the cAMP system, receptor recycling, and receptor uncoupling.
  • Morphine is stated to fail to induce receptor endocytosis and recycling, while methadone induces endocytosis and is used in treatment of tolerance and dependence.
High toleranceModerate toleranceMinimal or no tolerance
Analgesia, euphoria, dysphoria, mental clouding, sedation, respiratory depression, antidiuresis, nausea and vomiting, cough suppression Bradycardia Miosis, constipation, convulsions
Exam trap: tolerance to respiratory depression develops, but tolerance to miosis and constipation is minimal or absent.

Physical dependence and withdrawal

MUST KNOW
  • Abrupt discontinuation causes rhinorrhea, lacrimation, yawning, chills, piloerection, hyperventilation, hyperthermia, mydriasis, muscular aches, vomiting, diarrhea, anxiety, and hostility.
  • Morphine timeline: appears 6–10 hours, peaks 36–48 hours, disappears by about 5 days.
  • Detoxification: counters physical dependence while avoiding abrupt discontinuation.
  • For patients who need analgesia, especially cancer patients, withholding opioids solely because tolerance or dependence may develop is not justified in the lecture.

Opioid-induced hyperalgesia, OIH

MUST KNOW
  • Definition: increased sensation of pain, even from rubbing or massage.
  • Triggers: prolonged administration and high doses.
  • Most notorious in the lecture: morphine, fentanyl, remifentanil.
  • Possible causes listed: microglial-expressed receptor, spinal dynorphin with bradykinin and NMDA receptor activation, tissue injury or inflammation.

Which opioid effects show little or no tolerance?

How is OIH different from tolerance?

7. Acute, Chronic, Cancer, and Non-Cancer PainPages 5–7

Multimodal pain therapy

MUST KNOW

Use multiple agents with complementary mechanisms to improve analgesia and reduce opioid requirements and adverse effects.

Examples listed: COX inhibitors or NSAIDs, gabapentinoids, selective norepinephrine receptor inhibitors, local anesthetics, opioids, antiseizure drugs, antipsychotics, and a novel voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel blocker.

  • Acute pain: hours to days.
  • Chronic pain: months to years.

Modified chronic non-cancer pain ladder

MUST KNOW
Step 1
Non-opioid analgesic + adjuvant analgesic
Step 2
Weak opioid + non-opioid + adjuvant
Step 3
Non-opioid + adjuvant + minimal invasive intervention
Step 4
Strong opioid + non-opioid + adjuvant

The lecture also places integrative measures in Step 1, such as acupuncture, music therapy, and nonpharmacologic measures. It says strong opioids are not given right away for non-cancer pain.

WHO cancer pain ladder in the appendix

MUST KNOW
Step 1
Non-opioid ± adjuvant
Step 2
Opioid for mild to moderate pain ± non-opioid ± adjuvant
Step 3

Examples include nerve lysis. Adjuvants named include steroids, antipsychotics, and antiseizure drugs for cancer pain and pain due to metastases.

Do not confuse the two ladders: in the modified chronic non-cancer ladder, the lecture places minimally invasive intervention at Step 3 and strong opioid at Step 4. In the appendix cancer ladder, Step 3 is opioid therapy for moderate to severe pain and Step 4 is invasive treatment.

Cancer pain dosing principle from the review questions

EXAM TRAP

For a cancer patient starting morphine, the lecture answer favors ensuring adequate pain relief with round-the-clock treatment rather than PRN-only dosing or asking the patient to tolerate pain.

RECALL CHECK
What is the main reason for multimodal analgesia?
Complementary mechanisms provide better analgesia while reducing opioid requirements and adverse effects.
What is Step 4 in the appendix WHO cancer pain ladder?
Invasive or minimally invasive treatments.
For cancer pain, should morphine be PRN only?
No. The lecture favors round-the-clock therapy to ensure adequate pain relief.
