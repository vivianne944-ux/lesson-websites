1Trans 1 — Introduction to Basic Pharmacology

What term describes a drug that typifies the most important characteristics of its pharmacologic class and serves as a reference for comparison?

Answer: B. Prototype drug
A prototype drug represents the characteristic properties of its class. A prodrug requires conversion to an active form, a placebo has no active therapeutic ingredient, and an active metabolite is produced during biotransformation.
Source basis: Trans 1, Definition of Terms / Prototype Drugs, p. 1
2Trans 1 — Introduction to Basic Pharmacology

A large group of patients with the target disease receives an investigational drug to confirm efficacy, compare it with standard treatment, and further assess safety before approval. Which clinical trial phase is this?

Answer: C. Phase III
Phase III studies involve larger patient populations and are designed to confirm therapeutic efficacy and safety, often in comparison with existing treatment, before regulatory approval.
Source basis: Trans 1, Phases of Clinical Drug Investigation, p. 3
3Trans 2 — Nature, Sources & Dosage Forms

Which term refers to the specific chemical constituent in a crude plant or animal preparation that is responsible for its biological activity?

Answer: B. Active principle
The active principle is the biologically active chemical constituent found in a crude natural preparation. Excipients, vehicles, and preservatives support formulation but are not the principal source of the intended activity.
Source basis: Trans 2, Active Principle, p. 1
4Trans 2 — Nature, Sources & Dosage Forms

Which dosage form is designed for insertion into a body cavity, where it melts or dissolves, and may be useful for a patient unable to swallow?

Answer: B. Suppository
Suppositories are inserted into a body cavity, commonly the rectum, and melt or dissolve at body temperature. The rectal route may be used when swallowing is not possible.
Source basis: Trans 2, Dosage Forms / Suppositories, pp. 4–5
5Trans 3 — Pharmacokinetics 1

Which pharmacokinetic measure most directly reflects the extent of systemic drug exposure and is commonly used when comparing bioavailability?

Answer: A. Area under the plasma concentration–time curve
The area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) represents total systemic exposure and is a principal parameter for comparing the extent of bioavailability.
Source basis: Trans 3, Parameters in Determining Bioavailability, pp. 3–4
6Trans 3 — Pharmacokinetics 1

Which fraction of a drug in plasma can most readily leave capillaries, reach receptors, and undergo glomerular filtration?

Answer: B. Free unbound fraction
The free, unbound fraction is available for distribution, receptor interaction, metabolism, and filtration. Protein-bound drug generally serves as a reservoir and is less immediately available.
Source basis: Trans 3, Drug Forms in Blood / Protein Binding, pp. 1 and 4
7Trans 4 — Pharmacokinetics 2

Which parameter is most directly used with the desired plasma concentration to calculate a loading dose?

Answer: A. Volume of distribution
Loading dose is based principally on the desired target concentration and the apparent volume of distribution, with adjustment for bioavailability when needed.
Source basis: Trans 4, Loading Dose, pp. 5–6
8Trans 4 — Pharmacokinetics 2

What pharmacokinetic change is expected in a patient with significant renal impairment when a drug is primarily eliminated by the kidneys?

Answer: B. Clearance decreases and half-life prolongs
Renal impairment reduces renal drug clearance. Reduced elimination causes plasma half-life to become prolonged and increases the risk of accumulation and toxicity.
Source basis: Trans 4, Factors Affecting Drug Excretion, p. 1
9Trans 5 — Pharmacodynamics & Drug Receptors

How does a noncompetitive antagonist typically alter the graded dose–response curve of an agonist?

Answer: B. Decreases maximal response
A noncompetitive or insurmountable antagonist reduces the maximal response that the agonist can produce. Increasing agonist concentration cannot fully restore the original maximum.
Source basis: Trans 5, Antagonism and Dose–Response Relationships, pp. 2–3
10Trans 5 — Pharmacodynamics & Drug Receptors

Drug X produces the same maximal effect as Drug Y but at a lower concentration. Which statement is correct?

Answer: B. Drug X is more potent
Potency refers to the amount or concentration needed to produce a given effect. A lower EC50 or a left-shifted curve indicates greater potency. Equal maximal effects indicate similar efficacy.
Source basis: Trans 5, Properties of Drugs / Potency and Efficacy, pp. 2–3
11Trans 6 — Rational Drug Therapy

Which information should be included when counseling a patient about a newly prescribed drug?

Answer: D. All of the above
Patient counseling should explain the drug’s effects, instructions for use, important warnings or adverse effects, and follow-up or monitoring plans.
Source basis: Trans 6, Counseling the Patient, p. 5
12Trans 6 — Rational Drug Therapy

Which prescription is the clearest example of irrational drug use in an uncomplicated illness?

Answer: C. Antibiotic for uncomplicated influenza
Influenza is viral, so an antibiotic is not indicated unless a bacterial complication is suspected or confirmed. Unnecessary antibiotics expose patients to harm and promote antimicrobial resistance.
Source basis: Trans 6, Rational Drug Therapy / Appropriate Drug Selection, pp. 1–2
13Trans 7 — Adverse Drug Reactions

A patient develops Stevens–Johnson syndrome after receiving a usual dose of a new antibiotic. The reaction is unpredictable and unrelated to the expected pharmacologic action. Which ADR type is this?

Answer: B. Type B — Bizarre
Type B reactions are bizarre, not dose-related, and unpredictable from the drug’s known pharmacologic actions. Severe immune-mediated skin reactions are typical examples.
Source basis: Trans 7, Type B (Bizarre), pp. 3–4
14Trans 7 — Adverse Drug Reactions

A child receiving isoniazid for several months develops peripheral neuropathy related to prolonged therapy. Which ADR type best fits this pattern?

Answer: C. Type C — Continuous/Chronic
Type C reactions are dose- and time-related, develop during prolonged use, and are associated with cumulative exposure. The lecture uses neuropathy during prolonged isoniazid therapy as an example.
Source basis: Trans 7, Type C and Review Questions, pp. 4 and 9
15Trans 8 — NSAIDs, Paracetamol, DMARDs & Gout

Why may a selective COX-2 inhibitor have a lower risk of gastric injury yet a higher thrombotic cardiovascular risk?

Answer: B. It preserves gastric COX-1 but reduces endothelial prostacyclin
Selective COX-2 inhibition preserves gastric COX-1-mediated cytoprotection, reducing GI irritation. However, inhibition of endothelial prostacyclin may leave platelet thromboxane relatively unopposed, increasing thrombosis risk.
Source basis: Trans 8, COX Isoenzymes and Selective COX-2 Inhibitors, p. 2
16Trans 8 — NSAIDs, Paracetamol, DMARDs & Gout

A patient receiving methotrexate for rheumatoid arthritis develops mouth sores and nausea. Which agent is used to reduce methotrexate toxicity?

Answer: B. Leucovorin
Leucovorin, a folinic acid derivative, is used to reduce methotrexate adverse effects. Mesna protects against acrolein from cyclophosphamide; N-acetylcysteine treats paracetamol toxicity; cholestyramine enhances leflunomide elimination.
Source basis: Trans 8, DMARD Antidotes / Methotrexate, pp. 10 and 14
17Trans 9 — Opioid Analgesics

A patient with chronic renal failure receives morphine and develops increasing sedation and respiratory depression. Accumulation of which metabolite is most responsible?

Answer: A. Morphine-6-glucuronide
Morphine-6-glucuronide is an active, potent metabolite eliminated in urine. Renal failure causes accumulation, increasing analgesia but also sedation, respiratory depression, and reduced GI motility.
Source basis: Trans 9, Metabolism and Excretion in Renal Failure, p. 3
18Trans 9 — Opioid Analgesics

Which receptor profile correctly describes nalbuphine?

Answer: B. κ agonist and μ antagonist
Nalbuphine acts as a κ-receptor agonist and μ-receptor antagonist and has a ceiling effect. Tramadol is the weak μ agonist with monoamine effects, while naloxone is the broad opioid antagonist.
Source basis: Trans 9, Mixed Agonist–Antagonists, pp. 2 and 5
19Laboratory — Prescription Writing (Solids)

Which part of the prescription contains the directions that will appear on the patient’s medication label?

Answer: D. Signatura
The signatura contains directions to the patient and is introduced by “Label” or “Sig.” The subscription is directed to the pharmacist.
Source basis: Prescription Writing (Solids), Parts of a Prescription, p. 1
20Laboratory — Prescription Writing (Liquids)

What does the prescription abbreviation “h.s.” mean?

Answer: B. At bedtime
“h.s.” is derived from hora somni and means at bedtime. “stat” means immediately, “prn” means when needed, and “ac” means before meals.
Source basis: Prescription Writing (Liquids), Formative Quiz / Abbreviations, pp. 7–8
21Laboratory — Prescription Writing (Solids)

Amoxicillin 500 mg capsules are prescribed as 1 capsule three times daily for 7 days. How many capsules should be dispensed?

Answer: C. 21 capsules
One capsule × 3 doses/day × 7 days = 21 capsules.
Source basis: Prescription Writing (Solids), Capsule Prescription and Quantity Computation, p. 2
22Laboratory — Prescription Writing (Liquids)

A 20-kg child is prescribed paracetamol 15 mg/kg/dose every 6 hours round the clock for 2 days. The preparation is 250 mg/5 mL. What total volume is required?

Answer: C. 48 mL
Dose = 20 kg × 15 mg/kg = 300 mg. At 250 mg/5 mL, 300 mg equals 6 mL. Every 6 hours gives 4 doses/day; 6 mL × 4 × 2 days = 48 mL. A 60-mL bottle would be sufficient.
Source basis: Prescription Writing (Liquids), Weight-based Dose and Total-volume Computation, pp. 1–2
